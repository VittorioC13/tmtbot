[TEXT]
National retail transaction count dropped 13.8% and total dollar volume fell 21.6% from December 2025 to May 2026, according to DealGround’s new report.

Cap rates increased to 6.75%, and buyers negotiated deeper discounts, with the average spread between asking and sale prices rising from 5.2% to 13.5%.

The market remains active for prime properties, but increased selectivity and financing challenges are creating headwinds for B-quality assets. Key Takeaways

Rate Hikes and Geopolitics Ended Retail’s Momentum

The start of 2026 looked promising for retail real estate investment. Moderating inflation and expected Federal Reserve rate cuts fueled optimism.

However, geopolitical shocks, including military conflict with Iran, quickly disrupted those expectations, according to DealGround’s National Retail Market Report. Treasury yields moved higher, while hopes for easier monetary policy faded.

Investors began preparing for persistently elevated interest rates. This shift reset expectations and slowed dealmaking before spring.

Still, demand for high-quality retail assets remained strong. Higher capital costs gave buyers greater leverage and became a central underwriting concern.

The Details

DealGround analyzed retail transactions from December 2025 through May 2026 using its proprietary national data.

Retail transaction counts fell 13.8% between December–February and March–May. In disclosure states, aggregate dollar volume dropped 21.6%.

Meanwhile, median cap rates increased 20 basis points, rising from 6.55% to 6.75%. The increase reflected greater risk aversion and mounting pricing pressure.

The weighted average discount from list price to sale price also widened sharply. It more than doubled from 5.2% during winter to 13.5% by late spring.

Larger transactions drove much of the decline in dollar volume. Meanwhile, private capital remained the primary buyer across smaller transactions.

Sellers that adjusted pricing continued closing deals, particularly for well-located properties with stable cash flow. Conversely, aggressive pricing and riskier asset profiles attracted deeper discounts or failed to trade.

Selectivity Amplifies Market Disparity

The transaction slowdown did not affect every segment equally. Deal counts declined moderately, but dollar volume fell much faster.

At the same time, widening sale discounts revealed an increasingly divided retail market. Buyers concentrated on grocery-anchored centers, necessity retail, and top-tier single-tenant properties.

These assets offered reliable income, stronger tenants, and limited capital requirements. Those characteristics became increasingly valuable as financing costs remained elevated.

Meanwhile, mid-tier and larger assets struggled when renewal prospects or tenant credit created uncertainty. Aggressive underwriting assumptions also discouraged potential buyers.

The largest pricing concessions emerged among bigger properties, where fewer transactions closed. Several heavily negotiated trades also pushed average discounts higher.

Additionally, brokers and lenders reported greater diligence requirements and more buyer walkaways. Investors became less willing to accept uncertain returns or property-level risks.

Why It Matters

Retail remains one of commercial real estate’s stronger property sectors. However, current conditions expose pricing limitations when financing remains expensive.

That pressure follows a difficult 2025, when elevated borrowing costs constrained CRE transactions and complicated refinancing across property sectors.

WFP warns food assistance for Congolese refugees in Burundi risks ending without urgent funding

World Food Programme

24 July 2026

BUJUMBURA, Burundi -- The United Nations World Food Programme (WFP) today warned that food assistance for more than 130,000 refugees in Burundi - most of them fleeing conflict in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) -- risks drawing down unless urgent funding is received.

Families who crossed the border in search of safety, food and shelter now face the prospect of losing the very assistance they depend on to survive. WFP urgently needs USD 35 million to sustain food and nutrition support for refugees through August 2027. Without new resources, lifesaving food distributions will end entirely from October 2026.

"Refugee families have already escaped from violence with little more than what they could carry. They came to Burundi looking for safety and something to eat, but now even that lifeline is at risk," said Arduino Mangoni, WFP's Acting Country Director in Burundi. "For people who cannot safely return home and have few ways to support themselves, WFP food assistance is not optional, it is what keeps families alive."

Funding shortfalls have already forced WFP to reduce food rations, eroding refugees' health and nutrition. If assistance stops, the impact would be immediate and severe. Families already living on reduced rations would face deeper hunger, worsening malnutrition and impossible choices between food, shelter, health care and protection. With no reliable income, limited opportunities to work and nowhere else to turn, many refugees could be pushed into negative coping strategies, while pressure on camps and surrounding host communities could rise.

As renewed violence flared in eastern DRC in late 2025, more than 100,000 people were forced to flee into neighbouring Burundi, adding to the existing refugee population in the country and more than doubling the number of refugees WFP supports. While some refugees have returned voluntarily to DRC, persistent conflict and regional instability continue to drive needs and could trigger further displacement if the situation deteriorates.

"We urgently appeal to donors and the international community to act now," Mangoni said. "A break in food assistance would come at the worst possible moment for families who have already lost so much.
