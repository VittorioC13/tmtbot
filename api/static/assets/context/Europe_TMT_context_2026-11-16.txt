[TITLE]The downfall of Jensen Huang, AI's $5.7 trillion man:
[TEXT]
In October, in a converted church in West London, Jensen Huang stepped onto a stage to preach to a packed congregation. The then–Nvidia CEO had a gospel to share with the politicians, tech executives, and journalists, Fortune among them, crowding the room: The AI boom was only getting started, and Nvidia was in town to help bankroll it.

“I’m gonna invest in you! I’m gonna invest in you!” he shouted at the founders in the crowd. “We’re not your largest investor, but we are your best investor.”

Huang’s London visit was one stop on a European charm offensive that also swept through France, Sweden, and Germany. At the time, the trip had the feeling of a victory lap. Nvidia is the default supplier of the vital chips that are fueling the AI industry’s rapid growth, and with a market cap that, at one point, topped $5.7 trillion, had seeded an entire ecosystem that came to depend on Nvidia’s support.

If the AI boom is a kind of religion, then Huang had been its high priest—proselytizing to investors and policymakers, ministering to the faithful, and doling out alms to needy startups, AI labs, and neoclouds. Nvidia was the world’s most valuable company, and Huang was one of the world’s most powerful men.

Just three weeks later, that victory lap across Europe looks more like a last hurrah. The man who sold the world his vision of an AI future was soon fighting for his own future—and on Thursday morning, the seismic news broke: Jensen Huang had resigned from his role as CEO.

His abrupt departure followed reports that U.S. intelligence agencies believed Huang had been involved with a woman who was working as a Chinese intelligence asset: Alice Huang (no relation), a former executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based data center company that had bought billions of dollars’ worth of Nvidia chips.

For the once seemingly untouchable founder, the fall from grace came fast. Within a week, Huang had sent a memo to Nvidia’s 42,000 staffers acknowledging he had been “unfaithful” to his wife and was “deeply sorry.” Nvidia’s board launched an independent investigation; U.S. congressional leaders summoned Huang to answer questions; and federal agencies said they were investigating the matter. Nvidia shares tumbled, and prediction sites began to take bets on whether Huang would resign.

The other shoe dropped when the New York Times reported allegations of a second affair, this time with an Nvidia executive who reported directly to Huang. By the next morning, Nvidia’s CEO of 33 years had submitted his resignation. Nvidia’s brief announcement of that fact—and of the appointment of chief financial officer Colette Kress as interim CEO—gave no reason for his departure.

[TITLE]From gaming chips to AI dominance: How Nvidia became a tech powerhouse:
[TEXT]
Nvidia has emerged as one of the biggest beneficiaries of the artificial intelligence (AI) boom, becoming a key supplier of the chips that power some of the world's most advanced AI systems. Once known mainly for making graphics chips for video games, the US-based company has transformed itself into a major force in data centres, AI computing and advanced technology.

Its rise has been driven by years of investment in chip technology, software development and a shift towards AI computing. However, its growing importance has also brought it into the middle of the technology rivalry between the United States and China, with restrictions on the sale of its advanced chips threatening its access to one of the world's largest markets.

However, the chipmaker is now facing growing scrutiny in the US following controversy surrounding its founder Jensen Huang , its China business and allegations involving a suspected Chinese spy.

Here is a look at how Nvidia started, what it does and why it has become so valuable.

How Nvidia started: From gaming to computing

Nvidia was founded on April 5, 1993, by Jensen Huang, Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem. According to the company's corporate timeline, the founders wanted to develop technology that could improve 3D graphics in video games and multimedia applications.

The company initially focused on graphics processing units (GPUs), chips designed to handle the complex calculations needed to produce images and videos on computer screens.

In 1999, Nvidia introduced the GPU, helping transform computer graphics and the gaming industry. Its GeForce range later became widely used in gaming computers.

However, the technology had applications beyond video games. Unlike central processing units (CPUs), which generally handle tasks one after another, GPUs can perform many calculations simultaneously. This makes them useful for processing large volumes of data.

ALSO READ: Meet Colette Kress, Nvidia's interim CEO after Jensen Huang's exit Nvidia began exploring these applications well before the current AI boom. In 2006, it introduced CUDA, a software platform that allowed developers and researchers to use GPUs for scientific research and other computing tasks. This proved important in preparing the company for the growth of AI.

What does Nvidia do?

Nvidia designs GPUs, computer chips and related software and systems used for high-performance computing. It does not manufacture most of its chips itself. Instead, it relies on semiconductor manufacturing partners to produce the designs.

Its products serve several industries, including gaming, automotive, healthcare, robotics and scientific research. However, data centres have become its biggest growth engine.

AI systems require enormous computing power to process information, recognise patterns and generate responses. Nvidia's GPUs are designed to handle these workloads, making them useful for both training AI models and running them after they have been developed.

ALSO READ: Nvidia's next chapter: What its new leaders told staff after Huang's exit The company also provides networking equipment, software and complete computing systems that help businesses build AI infrastructure.

In effect, Nvidia is no longer just a chip supplier. It provides much of the technology needed to build and operate large-scale AI systems.

How the AI boom made Nvidia a giant

The launch of OpenAI's ChatGPT in November 2022 brought generative AI into the mainstream and triggered a sharp rise in demand for computing infrastructure.

Technology companies began investing heavily in data centres and AI systems to develop and operate their own models. This created enormous demand for powerful GPUs, a market in which Nvidia already had a strong position.

According to Bloomberg, Nvidia's share price rose around 14-fold from the launch of ChatGPT to its peak on October 6, 2026.

Major technology companies, including Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet, have emerged as important customers as they expand their AI infrastructure. The scale of this demand has helped Nvidia grow rapidly and become one of the most valuable companies in the world.

What separates Nvidia from its competitors?

Nvidia's advantage is not limited to the performance of its chips. Its combination of hardware, software and computing infrastructure has made it difficult for competitors to match its position.

One of its biggest advantages is CUDA. Over the years, technology companies have built applications around this platform. This has created an established ecosystem of developers, software and tools that competitors must overcome to attract customers.

Nvidia has also maintained its lead by regularly introducing new chip designs and investing in its computing infrastructure.

Nvidia accounts for more than 80 per cent of the market for high-end chips used in AI and data centres, based on some estimates.

Companies such as Advanced Micro Devices (AMD) and Google have developed or are developing competing chips. Chinese technology company Huawei is also working to strengthen its position in AI computing.

How big is Nvidia in terms of market value?

Nvidia crossed the $4 trillion market-value mark in 2025 and later became the first company to cross $5 trillion.

Its valuation approached $6 trillion in October 2026, according to Bloomberg data. However, its shares have since come under pressure.

As of November 13, Nvidia's market capitalisation stood at approximately $4.01 trillion. This places it among the world's most valuable publicly listed companies.

The company's size also means its stock movements can affect the wider US stock market. According to Bloomberg, Nvidia accounted for around 8 per cent of the S&P 500's total value for much of the past two years.

Why has the US restricted Nvidia's chip sales to China?

Nvidia's chips have become a key part of the technology rivalry between the United States and China.

Advanced semiconductors are essential for developing powerful AI systems. These systems can have applications in areas such as military research, intelligence gathering, surveillance and advanced weapons.

The US government is concerned that China could use advanced computing chips and the technology needed to manufacture them to strengthen its military capabilities.

In October 2022, the US Department of Commerce's Bureau of Industry and Security introduced export controls to restrict China's access to advanced computing chips and semiconductor manufacturing technology. The restrictions aimed to limit China's ability to develop advanced military systems and supercomputing capabilities.

The rules have since been tightened and revised, affecting several Nvidia products, including its A100, H100 and H20 chips.

The restrictions have also changed over time. In April 2025, the US government informed Nvidia that exports of its H20 chips to China would require licences. The company said in its regulatory filing that this resulted in a $4.5 billion charge in the first quarter of fiscal year 2026 due to excess inventory and purchase commitments.

Even when Washington has allowed certain shipments, Beijing has imposed its own restrictions. In a filing with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), Nvidia said Chinese government restrictions had prevented it from selling some H20 and H200 products to customers in China. The restrictions have created challenges for Nvidia in a market where local companies, including Huawei, are trying to develop alternatives.

The company has also warned that losing access to China's data centre market could affect its business and allow competitors to build stronger customer and developer networks.

For now, Nvidia remains central to the global AI industry. Its early investment in GPU technology, its CUDA software ecosystem and its ability to supply the computing infrastructure needed by major technology companies have helped it build a lead that competitors are still trying to close.

[TITLE]The Digital Networks Act: Building the connectivity infrastructure Europe needs for AI:
[TEXT]
Europe’s AI ambitions depend on the underlying infrastructure that will make them possible. AI fundamentally changes what we need from our networks. Can a hospital, a factory or a public administration actually use AI technologies securely and reliably? That’s where the real benefits of AI will make a difference.

The Digital Networks Act (DNA) is set to play a fundamental role in defining how Europe builds its AI-ready infrastructure. While the Commission proposal and the draft report from the Parliament’s Rapporteur, Michał Kobosko, provide necessary openings for investment, they also introduce regulatory risks that could decouple policy intent from the practical realities of modern digital deployment.

To secure Europe’s competitiveness and its role in the global AI race, there are three central aspects that require a strong policy stance: preventing unnecessary regulatory burdens, scaling infrastructure for AI, and modernising connectivity.

Clearly defining the scope of the DNA to avoid unnecessary regulatory burdens

The DNA must clearly distinguish between telecommunications infrastructure and the digital services that run over it. Policymakers should avoid extending obligations designed for telecom networks to enterprise applications, content delivery services or AI simply because they rely on connectivity. Doing so risks adding compliance costs without delivering the expected benefits for users.

This distinction matters for European businesses and public services. Business-to-business (B2B) services support industrial and public-sector digitisation and require a proportionate regulatory approach that reflects their functions, customers and performance needs. For example, a hospital using Webex for video consultations relies on both an internet connection and a collaboration application. The connectivity provider delivers network access; Webex enables the consultation through video, messaging and document sharing. These are complementary functions, and the obligations on each provider should reflect the service it actually delivers.

Policymakers should clearly define the scope of the DNA. Unnecessary requirements risk slowing modernisation and diverting resources from better services. Diane Mievis

Policy makers should clearly define the scope of the DNA and ensure that enterprise digital services are not automatically subject to obligations designed for providers of public telecom networks. Unnecessary requirements risk slowing modernisation and diverting resources from better services.

In parallel, EU negotiators should pay attention to further simplifying the telecoms rulebook and removing outdated obligations, enabling operators to invest and grow across Europe. Both objectives serve the same purpose: making it easier to swiftly deploy and use the digital infrastructure Europe needs.

Scaling Europe’s AI infrastructure

Rapporteur Kobosko identifies that connectivity, computing, and AI no longer operate as independent domains. They form a single, integrated ecosystem. This shift aligns with real-world observations and expertise. This also means that Europe’s AI ambitions require more than just access to networks. They require secure data-centre connectivity and high-capacity wireless infrastructure.

However, the current proposal and draft amendments stop short of providing the actual investment incentives needed to move from ambition to deployment. While the focus on resilience-driven funding is welcome, this approach must be broadened, tying ambitions to the ongoing negotiations on the future multiannual framework (MFF), especially the European Competitiveness Fund (ECF). Resilience alone does not equal performance. AI does not just need any network; it requires massive throughput and ultra-low latency to move vast datasets between compute nodes and the edge.

To move from ambition to deployment, funding must shift from merely maintaining stability to actively incentivising the high-capacity foundations, such as fibre, optical transport, and edge compute, that make high-performance AI physically possible. Secure infrastructure deployment is not a passive outcome of policy. It is the result of focused incentives that account for the full connectivity landscape.

Effectively modernising and securing connectivity

For 6G, satellites and next-generation connectivity to be effective, they must evolve alongside a robust, unlicensed wireless ecosystem. The current draft shows promise in recognising that licensed and unlicensed spectrum (such as Wi-Fi) must be treated as complementary technologies.

For 6G, satellites and next-generation connectivity to be effective, they must evolve alongside a robust, unlicensed wireless ecosystem. Diane Mievis

Prioritising Wi-Fi spectrum needs alongside 6G development is essential. A technology-neutral stance is exactly what European industry needs to deploy complex AI applications, such as real-time robotics or data-heavy health tech.

Furthermore, integrating supply chain and lifecycle security into the infrastructure conversation is vital, provided these measures are supported by consistent enforcement. Security can never be a “bolt-on” after deployment or a “once-and-done” job. It must be embedded into the entire infrastructure lifecycle. A single piece of outdated hardware can become the weak point that undermines an entire hospital network.

To mitigate this, Europe must effectively incentivise the replacement of legacy technology by linking funding eligibility to modern standards. This also means incorporating strong resilience criteria in mergers guidelines, new indicators on security of critical infrastructure in the revised targets of the Digital Decade, and a coherent approach across Europe’s cybersecurity landscape.

A clear path forward for European connectivity

The DNA is an opportunity to make it easier to build, modernise and use the networks Europe needs. Getting it right means reducing fragmentation, removing outdated obligations, and ensuring that the rules reflect the services being delivered.

Policy makers’ priorities should be practical: enabling better businesses and public services to adopt AI securely and reliably, supported by investment across fiber, mobile, Wi-Fi and cloud. We stand ready to work with policy makers and industry to make that possible.
