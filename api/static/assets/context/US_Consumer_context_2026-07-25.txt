[TITLE]Could EU’s Clothing Destruction Ban Change How Fashion Overproduces?:
[TEXT]
For Anne-Laure Descours, the question fashion observers should be asking isn’t why so many brands destroy unsold merchandise but why they produce enough overstock to make it necessary in the first place.

“If you plan better and produce the right amounts of goods, you’d be less exposed to mass destruction,” the Hong Kong-based supply chain veteran said.

The practice is among the industry’s worst-kept secrets, especially among luxury companies that employ this ruthless calculus to maintain exclusivity through scarcity, prevent their products from being devalued by clearance racks and thwart counterfeiters seeking to intercept genuine stock on the gray market. But mass-market retailers also do this because their high-volume, low-margin business models make processing ecommerce returns and storing past-season inventory more expensive than shredding the surplus.

The European Environment Agency estimates that roughly 4-9 percent of all textile products placed on the European market, amounting to between 264,000 and 594,000 metric tons of material, are destroyed each year before use.

Everyone did it, Descours said, even Puma, where she directed development and sourcing for nearly 14 years, though a spokesperson for the sportswear giant said it doesn’t destroy any products that can still be worn, instead selling them through its network of outlet stores or donating them to charity.

As of last Sunday, however, any large brand, wholesaler or manufacturer with more than 250 employees and a turnover of 50 million euros ($57 million) selling into the European Union is legally prohibited from destroying any apparel, footwear and accessories, with medium-sized companies to follow from 2030.

The move, introduced under the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation, aims to prevent valuable products and the resources used to make them from going to waste, the European Commission’s Directorate-General for Environment said in a statement on Friday.

“When new, usable goods are discarded, the raw materials, water, energy and labor invested in their production are lost, while their disposal generates avoidable greenhouse gas emissions,” said the department, which is responsible for developing and delivering the 27-member bloc’s environmental laws and sustainability policies. “By encouraging reuse, repair and more resource-efficient business practices, the new rules support the transition to a more circular and competitive European economy.”

This was a long time coming, Descours said, though it’s a big deal in that it will force brands and retailers to invest much more in better planning. Depending on whom you ask, anywhere from 20-40 percent of clothing produced is never actually sold. These items, whether liquidated, destroyed or left as deadstock, represent hundreds of billions in lost potential sales even before aggressive markdowns—a whole other problem in itself—are factored in.

But as they say in her part of the world, in crisis, Descours said, there is opportunity. Under the new law, businesses must prioritize keeping products in use by selling them, including through discounts or alternative markets; donating them to charities or social enterprises; or preparing them for reuse, repairing, refurbishing or remanufacturing. However, FDI remains prohibited in business-to-consumer (B2C) e-commerce as well as inventory-based e-commerce, where an e-commerce entity owns inventory and sells goods directly to consumers.To operationalise the change, the DPIIT has inserted a new provision in the FDI policy allowing e-commerce entities to adopt the inventory-based model solely for exports.The newly added clause states: "An e-commerce entity is permitted to engage in inventory-based model of ecommerce exclusively for the export of goods/products manufactured and/or produced in India as per the applicable provisions of the Foreign Trade Policy 2023...and the Foreign Exchange Management (Export of Goods & Services) Regulations, 2015".The relaxation is limited to exports of goods manufactured or produced in India, meaning the existing restrictions on inventory-led e-commerce for the domestic market will continue to remain in force.
