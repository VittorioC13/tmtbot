[TITLE]FCC Backs AT&T's Plan to Remove Copper Landlines in California:
[TEXT]
The Federal Communications Commission is backing AT&T’s push to override a California regulator so that the carrier can discontinue copper landline service to consumers.

On Friday, the FCC announced that it was preempting "specific requirements in California law that prevent consumers from transitioning off of unreliable, slow copper lines,” even as some residents fear they’ll be saddled with spotty cell service as an alternative.

AT&T argues that California’s "Carrier of the Last Resort” (COLR) rules prevent it from removing the old-school copper network that currently serves only 3% of California households, a number that continues to shrink. In May, the company urged the commission to declare that federal laws preempted California's regulations, thus clearing the way for AT&T to discontinue the service as it prioritizes high-speed fiber and expanded 5G cellular coverage.

Friday’s FCC order partially grants AT&T’s petition, agreeing that under federal law it has “explicit jurisdiction over the discontinuance of interstate telecommunications services.”

Still, the order refrains from “wholly” preempting California’s Carrier of the Last Resort rules, and seems to instead focus on the AT&T situation. “While we preempt those requirements that necessitate that AT&T obtain a variety of CPUC [California Public Utilities Commission] approvals before it can discontinue POTS [plain old telephone service] in accordance with federal authorization, we do not preempt the COLR and other obligations more generally,” the 74-page document says. (Photo by Kevin Dietsch/Getty Images)

The FCC issued the order, even though many locals opposed AT&T’s proposal to discontinue the landline service. “Please do not allow AT&T to eliminate the last reliable communication tool for rural residents. The VOIP and cellular infrastructure is not robust enough to provide...equal access for their communication needs,” one concerned user wrote in public comments.

The CPUC also told the FCC “nothing in California’s COLR rules prevents AT&T from retiring its copper infrastructure," even though it argued against preemption. CPUC’s main concern has been that the company’s replacement, called AT&T Phone - Advanced, won’t perform as well indoors compared with outdoor cell coverage, and it’ll cost more for consumers.

However, the FCC disagreed and was convinced that AT&T Phone - Advanced would be offered at a lower price, including “at a discounted rate of $25 per month to existing Lifeline customers in California,” while providing sufficient signal strength. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr also said: “Importantly, this decision does not come at the expense of consumer safeguards. The FCC’s rules require providers to notify consumers of proposed service discontinuances and provide an opportunity for public comment. We also preserve important protections for service reliability and public safety, including access to 911.”

Despite the FCC’s order, it’s not entirely clear if AT&T will pursue the copper landline phase-out in California. In May, the carrier sued to overturn the local regulations and block the CPUC from enforcing them against AT&T, which noted it spends $1 billion each year to maintain the copper network. That court case remains ongoing.

For now, AT&T EVP Rhonda Johnson praised Chairman Carr for the decision. “California deserves fast, reliable, future-ready technology – and with the right policies in place, we’re delivering it and transitioning away from copper," she said. "No customer will be left without access to voice or 911 service, and we’re committed to working with our customers every step of the way during this transition.”

However, Democratic FCC Commissioner Anna Gomez opposed the order, saying: “Californians deserve to know that when they raise a concern, someone in their state with the authority to act on it is listening. I cannot support an item that moves that authority further away from them. I respectfully dissent.”

In a statement, CPUC also said the FCC's decision "without adequately considering the concerns of California is deeply concerning, particularly for customers in rural communities, high-fire-threat areas, and communities where reliable communications are essential to public safety." The regulator also hinted it'll explore legal options.

"Customers deserve confidence that their communications service they pay for will work when they need it most, CPUC said. "California will continue to ensure that confidence is borne out in practice, and will continue to defend its responsibility to protect consumers, preserve essential communications services, and promote public safety. We will carefully evaluate the FCC’s order and all available legal and regulatory options."

According to the regulator, AT&T has proposed discontinuing the legacy telephone service in the state on or after June 1, 2027. About 184,000 residential customers and 15,000 business customers will be affected.

[TITLE]AT&T CEO warns telecom needs nearly 200,000 more workers to wire the AI boom:
[TEXT]
John Stankey says the industry must add construction and technician talent by 2032 as data center spending surges

The AI boom runs on chips, power, and capital. According to AT&T CEO John Stankey, it also runs on people who know how to splice fiber, and the US is running short of them.

Stankey says the telecommunications industry will need nearly 200,000 additional construction and technician workers by 2032. That labor is needed to connect the wave of data centers now under construction across the country.

The math behind the shortage

Data center development has become one of the biggest drivers of US economic growth. Roughly $800 billion is expected to be spent on it in 2026 alone.

Stankey made his comments on a Business Roundtable podcast. He framed the worker gap as an increasingly urgent problem rather than a distant concern.

The figure he cited covers construction crews and technicians, the people who trench, install, connect, and maintain fiber lines.

What AT&T is doing about it

AT&T is not waiting for the labor market to sort itself out. The company has hired around 10,000 broadband construction and fiber technicians over the past three years.

It plans to bring on another 3,000 technicians in 2026. That hiring is part of a $250 billion multi-year investment in fiber infrastructure.

AT&T’s planned 2026 hiring works out to about 1.5% of the nearly 200,000 workers Stankey says the sector requires. Even its three-year total of roughly 10,000 hires covers only a sliver of the gap.

A crowded competition for the same workers

The squeeze extends well beyond telecom. Demand for skilled trades workers is intensifying across multiple sectors, from AI infrastructure projects to rural broadband programs.

The shortage of trained technicians remains a critical bottleneck even as companies like AT&T step up recruiting.

Why this matters for the AI buildout

Most public conversation about AI infrastructure focuses on GPUs, electricity, and the eye-watering capital budgets of large tech firms. Labor tends to get treated as an afterthought.

Stankey’s warning flips that framing. Money can be raised and hardware can be ordered, but a skilled fiber technician cannot be manufactured overnight.

AT&T appears to be betting on exactly that. By hiring thousands of technicians ahead of demand and tying that effort to its $250 billion fiber commitment, it is positioning labor as a competitive asset rather than a line-item expense.

If roughly $800 billion flows into data center development in 2026 but connectivity lags, some projects could face delays between construction and full operation.

Policy could become part of the conversation too. With rural broadband initiatives competing for the same technicians, government programs and private AI projects may end up bidding against each other for a finite workforce.

The things to watch are fairly concrete. Track whether AT&T hits its target of 3,000 new technicians in 2026, whether other carriers announce similar hiring pushes, and whether training pipelines expand fast enough to close a gap measured in the hundreds of thousands.
