Net overweight in technology stocks fell from 37% to 12% in a single month as fund managers questioned the AI spending boom

Fund managers just made their biggest monthly exit from technology stocks in nearly two decades. Bank of America’s November 2026 Global Fund Manager Survey shows net overweight positioning in tech fell to 12%, down from 37% a month earlier.

That 25 percentage point drop is the steepest single-month decline since June 2006. For context, the iPhone hadn’t launched yet the last time professionals bailed on the sector this fast.

What the survey found

Bank of America ran the survey from November 6 to 12. It polled between 169 and 193 fund managers overseeing between $461 billion and $526 billion in assets.

The headline number is the tech reversal. A net overweight position means more managers hold more of an asset than their benchmark calls for. At 37%, tech was a clear favorite. At 12%, it is closer to a polite maybe.

The AI skepticism shows up across several questions. A record net 38% of respondents said companies are overinvesting in AI projects, the highest reading the survey has recorded.

Meanwhile, 52% expect at least one major AI hyperscaler to announce cuts to capital expenditures within the next six months.

Michael Hartnett, a strategist at Bank of America, indicated that the enthusiasm around the AI boom appears to be fading. Many respondents are forecasting a slowdown in data center spending, which has been the engine of the trade.

Semiconductors tell a similar story. Long global semiconductors remain the most crowded trade in the survey at 39% overweight. That sounds high until you compare it to July, when the figure peaked at 82%. More than half of that positioning has unwound in roughly four months.

Cautious on tech, not on stocks

The push comes as farmers in countries such as Nigeria face growing difficulties in determining when to plant, manage agricultural inputs and prepare for adverse weather conditions, with limited access to timely and locally relevant climate information adding to the risks facing agricultural production.

For millions of farmers across Africa, the timing of rainfall can make the difference between a successful harvest and financial losses. Planting before the rains begin risks seed failure, while late planting may deny crops the adequate growing period needed to reach maturity.

Tadesse said the challenge was no longer simply obtaining climate information but ensuring that farmers and public institutions could translate forecasts into practical decisions before climate risks developed into crises.

“As climate variability intensifies, the challenge is no longer simply accessing climate information, but translating it into practical, timely decisions that protect agricultural productivity, strengthen resilience and safeguard livelihoods,” he said.

Artificial intelligence could help tackle some of these challenges by enhancing current weather forecasting systems and tailoring climate information to the specific agricultural needs of local communities.

One approach highlighted by Tadesse is Probabilistic Bias Correction, a machine-learning framework developed by Microsoft Research to correct recurring errors in existing weather models and improve forecasts over the medium term.

[TEXT]
Artificial intelligence may exist in the digital world, but the infrastructure supporting it has a considerable physical footprint. This contradiction has moved towards the centre of discussions around COP31 in Türkiye. As governments wrestle with the much larger questions of fossil fuels, climate finance and decarbonisation, another problem is emerging: how can the world accommodate rapidly growing AI infrastructure without simultaneously driving up electricity demand and carbon emissions?

The issue is particularly significant for data centres. These facilities require enormous quantities of electricity to run servers and cooling equipment, along with physical land, grid capacity and, in many cases, substantial amounts of water.

The International Energy Agency has previously warned that expanding AI workloads are becoming an increasingly important driver of electricity demand. The problem is not simply how much energy AI consumes. Where that electricity comes from is equally important.

A highly efficient data centre connected to a carbon-intensive electricity system can still carry a significant environmental footprint. Conversely, AI infrastructure increasingly supplied by renewable and other low-carbon energy could allow computing capacity to expand without producing a corresponding rise in emissions. This makes the AI revolution as much an energy and infrastructure challenge as a technological one.
