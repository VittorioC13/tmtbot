[TITLE]PNGRB proposes 14% assured returns for natural gas pipeline developers:
[TEXT]
India’s oil and gas regulator has proposed a new framework for awarding natural gas pipeline projects that would guarantee developers a 14 per cent post-tax return on equity while insulating them from the risk of lower-than-expected gas volumes, shifting the focus of competition from transportation tariffs to construction costs.

The Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board (PNGRB), in a concept paper and two sets of draft regulations dated November 23, has proposed selecting pipeline developers through competitive bidding based on the lowest quoted capital expenditure, replacing the existing approach under which bidders compete on tariffs and transportation capacity.

Under the proposed framework, developers of pipelines awarded through the capital expenditure-based bidding route would receive a fixed 14 per cent post-tax return on the normative equity component of project cost over the pipeline’s 40-year economic life.

Transportation tariffs, meanwhile, would be calculated on the basis of actual gas volumes carried, exposing shippers to higher per-unit charges if pipelines operate below capacity.

Existing bidding framework to be replaced

The regulator said the existing bidding framework, which encourages aggressive tariff or capacity commitments, had threatened the financial viability of projects when actual gas flows fell short of projections. The proposed changes also draw on recommendations from two government-appointed high-level committees examining India’s gas infrastructure requirements under Vision 2040 and competition in gas transmission and distribution.

Under the draft authorisation regulations, PNGRB would appoint separate independent agencies to assess demand and prepare a detailed feasibility report (DFR) for each proposed pipeline.

The feasibility report would establish the project’s technical specifications, scope, expected demand and estimated capital expenditure. It would be placed in the public domain for consultation before the regulator issues the bidding documents.

Financial bids would then be ranked solely on the basis of the lowest capital expenditure quoted against the DFR estimate. PNGRB would retain the authority to reject bids considered unreasonably high or low, with scope to relax such limits in the public interest, provided the reasons are recorded.

The proposed framework also removes the annual transportation-volume target for pipelines awarded through this route. Under the existing provisions, failure to meet the prescribed target can result in forfeiture of the performance bond, which is set at 1 per cent of project cost, subject to a maximum of Rs 20 crore.

Developers would be required to commission projects within 36 months of authorisation, although extensions could be granted for delays beyond their control.

The draft also proposes increasing the additional capacity that must be made available to third parties under open access to 100 per cent of the combined requirement, from the existing 33 per cent.

14 per cent return on normative equity

The proposed tariff framework provides a separate mechanism for pipelines awarded through capital expenditure-based bidding.

The 14 per cent post-tax return would be calculated on a normative equity base equivalent to 30 per cent of the quoted project cost, assuming a standard debt-equity ratio of 70:30, irrespective of the developer’s actual financing structure.

For example, a pipeline with a quoted capital cost of Rs 10,000 crore would have a normative equity base of Rs 3,000 crore on which the prescribed return would be calculated.

Normative debt would be repaid over 15 years following a three-year moratorium, through equal annual instalments.

Operating expenditure would be benchmarked at 2 per cent of the quoted capital cost, with an annual escalation of 4.5 per cent. Fuel, employee costs and debt servicing would be accounted for separately under the proposed framework.

Tariffs linked to actual pipeline utilisation

Tariffs would be determined using actual transportation volumes. If gas flows exceed the higher of the regulator’s projected volume or 75 per cent of the pipeline’s capacity, half of the additional volume-related benefit would be passed on to users through lower tariffs.

The framework seeks to provide developers with greater certainty on returns while linking the recovery of transportation costs to actual pipeline utilisation.

Differences in proposed tariff review mechanisms

The concept paper proposes a tariff true-up every two years and sets the normative interest rate at the State Bank of India’s one-year marginal cost of funds-based lending rate (MCLR). The draft tariff regulations instead prescribe the benchmark plus one percentage point and provide for tariff reviews every five years, without incorporating the proposed two-year true-up in the relevant schedule.

The proposed changes could make pipeline development more attractive to investors by reducing exposure to demand uncertainty, a key risk in building long-lived infrastructure ahead of the growth in gas consumption.

Moving competition to the lowest capital cost could also encourage greater cost discipline and reduce the risk premiums developers build into bids to account for uncertain transportation volumes.

[TITLE]JERA plans AI data centers beside its power plants with Dell and RHAELM:
[TEXT]
Japan's largest power generator wants to put AI compute next to its turbines, starting with a 400 MW site in Chiba

JERA, Japan’s biggest power generator, wants AI data centers built right next to its power plants. The company reportedly plans to build these facilities for Anthropic and Meta at its generation sites.

The idea is simple. Instead of hauling electricity across a congested grid to a data center, you put the data center where the electricity is made. Getting a power hookup can take years in this industry, so skipping that step could be worth a lot of money.

The Chiba blueprint

The framework took shape on October 1, 2026. On that date, JERA signed a memorandum of understanding with Dell Technologies and UK-based developer RHAELM to develop large-scale AI data centers co-located with power plants.

The first target is JERA’s Chiba Thermal Power Station, an LNG-fired plant with output of approximately 4.38 GW.

The planned data center there would have 400 MW of capacity. That makes it a hyperscale facility, though it would use only a slice of what the plant can produce.

The partners expect total investment for the Chiba project to exceed $15 billion (¥2.3 trillion). Operations are slated to begin in phases starting around 2028, with full capacity targeted for 2029.

Each partner has a defined role:

JERA supplies the land and the power.

RHAELM handles development, construction, operation and financing.

Apollo Global Management is positioned as RHAELM’s financing partner.

Dell Technologies provides standardized rack-scale AI infrastructure.

Why behind-the-meter matters

The key technical feature is a behind-the-meter power setup. The data center draws electricity directly from the plant rather than through the public grid.

For data centers, that queue is the grid connection. Securing a new interconnection can be slow, and AI developers want capacity now. Co-locating with an existing plant lets the partners move faster than they could if they waited for new grid capacity.

JERA’s broader investment plans through 2035 focus on LNG, renewables and hydrogen and ammonia. The data center push now sits alongside those energy plans.

The Chiba facility is meant to be a prototype rather than a one-off. The partners aim to scale AI data centers to multiple gigawatts across various JERA thermal sites in Japan during the 2030s.

What this means

JERA’s model reverses the usual relationship between utilities and tech companies. Normally a utility sells power into the grid and customers buy from it. Here, the utility acts more like a landlord whose property comes with its own power supply.

The fuel source matters too. Chiba runs on LNG, so this AI capacity would be powered by natural gas rather than renewables. JERA’s long-term plans include renewables and hydrogen and ammonia, but the first project is tied to a fossil fuel plant.

Funding is another open question. A project topping $15 billion is a large bet even with Apollo involved. JERA’s openness to options like an IPO suggests the company expects its capital needs to grow fast if the multi-gigawatt plan moves forward.

Execution risk is real. The deal is currently a memorandum of understanding, not a finished facility. Timelines of 2028 and 2029 leave room for delays in construction, equipment delivery or financing.
