[TITLE]Microsoft outage shows that big tech can’t be treated as too big to fail:
[TEXT]
When a massive outage hit Microsoft late last week, much of the digital world ground to a halt.

The outage wiped out key Microsoft services including Outlook, Teams and Copilot, which are used by more than 430 million people around the world. As email and internal communications froze, the operations of governments and businesses went dark alongside them, triggering an economic paralysis that could cost billions of dollars.

While the global economy absorbs the brunt of this paralysis, Microsoft faces both a severe reputational hit and contractual penalties that could reach up to US$800 million per day for services they failed to deliver.

Such a catastrophic outage shows just how precarious our modern digital infrastructure is. It depends on a handful of big tech companies, which have essentially become too big to fail. So how did we end up in this situation? And is there a way out?

Digital dependency

The initial source of Microsoft’s failure was incredibly innocuous. Data centres usually share the load of user demands across systems all over the world. However, last week, a small configuration change caused the company to lose control of some of their computers.

Unable to share the load, these computers dropped user requests onto other systems, overwhelming them and producing more outages. This pattern repeated over and over again until the company lost almost complete control of their network.

Worse, disconnected user devices – phones, email clients and business systems – all automatically tried to reconnect at once, crashing Microsoft’s systems all over again.

Historically, a tech outage might affect a single company. But the reason Microsoft’s outage could impact millions of people comes down to a business revolution that silently took place over 20 years ago.

Software was once something that was sold to companies once for an upfront cost. In this model, purchasers held great power – only buying new versions if the update justified the expense.

Yet, these upfront expenses also scared consumers off. Drawing inspiration from telecommunications, gyms and real estate leasing, the tech industry transitioned to renting access to their products, creating what is now known as “software as a service”.

In theory, everyone profited from this model. Software vendors could bank more reliable cash flow, and customers could transition to regular smaller payments, rather than stumping up for large upfront costs.

Ultimately, this change shifted the balance of power towards tech companies. Instead of purchasing features they needed, consumers handed the keys to their operational kingdoms to the “software as service” providers, and locked themselves into highly expensive long-term contracts in the process.

Too big to fail

The global financial crisis in 2008 introduced the world to the idea of too big to fail. When it hit, the public was left to pick up the pieces of banks that had become too systemically important to be allowed to fail.

Unfortunately, it appears we haven’t really learned those lessons. Instead, we’ve spent the last few years living through a slow-motion version of that with the tech industry. Centralisation, consolidation and unchecked monopolies have left our digital lives, our businesses and our governments in an incredibly precarious place.

On one level or another, almost everything on the internet goes through Google, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon Web Services or CrowdStrike – all of which have had major outages over recent years.

These big tech giants earned our trust by providing unified, seemingly invincible infrastructure. But the convenience that comes with centralisation holds a trap.

Unlike the banking sector in 2008, when these tech titans fail, they don’t need to ask governments to bail them out. Instead, their centrality forces the entire digital economy to bear the costs of their outage.

Developing digital sovereignty

Escaping the reliance on a few tech giants for our national infrastructure, businesses and citizens’ data has spurned the push for so-called digital sovereignty.

Europe is leading the way on this. By actively funding decentralised cloud infrastructure and homegrown software, governments are ensuring that an American tech failure cannot knock out a local hospital.

The world must adopt a similar blueprint. Australia, in particular, is well placed to grasp the opportunities that digital sovereignty brings. We already have the raw engineering talent. Companies such as Atlassian, Apromore, Airwallex and Canva have proven we can build world-class digital tools.

If governments strategically invested in local tech ecosystems, they wouldn’t just be buying digital insurance. They would drive much-needed market competition, dilute the power of big-tech monopolies and spread out systemic risks – ensuring that a single point of failure could never bring the economy to its knees.

This is not to say we should divest ourselves of any connection to big-tech platforms, which do and will continue to provide useful services. But if our entire global economy continues to rely on a handful of too-big-to-fail digital monopolies, we will have no one but ourselves to blame when our screens inevitably go black again.

[TITLE]AI agents could help banks generate nearly 3x returns over three years: McKinsey:
[TEXT]
Representative image

AI productivity gains have yet to translate into significant earnings

Rising costs and security risks pose challenges

Banks adopting artificial intelligence (AI) agents could recover their investments in around 10 months and generate returns of nearly three times the initial outlay over three years, according to a report by McKinsey, which highlighted the technology's potential to reshape the banking sector.An analysis of 52 agentic AI use cases in banking found that the average investment payback period was approximately 10 months, while the estimated return on investment over three years stood at around 2.9 times, the report said.McKinsey said banks' competitive advantage would increasingly depend on how quickly they move beyond pilot projects and integrate AI into their operations, products and customer services.“The economics of banking may shift in quarters rather than years as AI changes what the work costs, how a bank and its customers interact, and how those customers choose,” the report said.Despite the potential returns, McKinsey cautioned that improvements in employee productivity do not automatically lead to higher profits for banks.According to its 2026 State of AI survey, 80 per cent of respondents reported individual productivity gains from using AI.However, only 6 per cent said the technology accounted for a significant share of their earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT).The findings suggest a gap between the productivity benefits reported by employees and the financial gains being realised by organisations.Customer adoption of AI is also increasing. A survey of around 46,000 consumers across 13 countries found that 23 per cent were already using generative AI for banking-related tasks.Among those using AI for such activities, 74 per cent relied on platforms such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, indicating the role of external AI tools in customers' banking-related interactions.The report also flagged the financial and security risks associated with wider AI adoption. Spending on AI tokens, computing and operations could rise to as much as 20 per cent of labour costs, adding to the expenses banks would need to manage while deploying the technology.It also cited around USD 900 million in reported losses from AI-enabled crime recorded by the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) in 2025.McKinsey said banks would need to build what it described as institutional intelligence by bringing together reliable data, customer information, internal policies and the experience of senior employees.Data readiness remains a significant hurdle. As many as 48 per cent of banking executives surveyed identified it as their biggest obstacle to adopting AI.The report said banks would also need to establish clear permissions, continuous monitoring and human oversight to ensure AI agents operate safely.It added that institutions would be better positioned to capture the benefits of the technology if they redesigned entire business processes rather than simply automating individual tasks.

[TITLE]Trump administration plans to name Nvidia an AI national champion:
[TEXT]
The chipmaker's status in Washington looks set to become official, capping a year of medals, pledges and close ties with the White House

The Trump administration plans to recognize Nvidia as an AI national champion. That would put a formal label on a relationship that has been getting cozier for some time.

The designation matters because it marks the government picking a favorite. It signals that Washington sees one chipmaker as central to America’s artificial intelligence ambitions, and plans to say so out loud.

From medal ceremony to mission funding

The groundwork has been visible for months. On October 8, 2026, President Trump awarded Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang the National Medal of Science. The ceremony took place at a summit titled “Science: A New Golden Age.”

The money tells a similar story. Nvidia committed $1 billion to the Genesis Mission, a federal program focused on AI-driven research across 14 agencies. That was the largest single pledge from any industry player.

For comparison, AMD put in $500 million. OpenAI committed $200 million. Total industry commitments to the mission exceed $2.4 billion.

The point of the program is to pour AI computing power into areas like energy, health and space.

Nvidia’s pledge alone accounts for well over a third of the total.

A relationship that runs both ways

The ties between Nvidia and the administration go beyond one research program. That influence has shown up in regulatory decisions and strategic chip export approvals. It has also shown up in US manufacturing pledges amounting to $500 billion over four years, starting in 2025, which have been linked to Huang and Nvidia.

There is also a more personal financial thread. Nvidia has contributed over $26 million to Trump-related projects. That figure includes a $25 million pledge toward the White House ballroom.

Critics argue that kind of spending raises potential conflicts of interest.

The partnership has not been free of friction. As of November 2026, scrutiny has intensified over Huang’s relationships and over foreign intelligence concerns. Trump has publicly defended him.

Why the label matters

A “national champion” is generally understood as a company a government treats as its flag-bearer in a strategic industry. The label usually implies policy support, preferential access and a shared sense of mission.

For rivals, the picture is less rosy. AMD and OpenAI are both contributing to the same federal mission, yet only one company is reportedly on track to receive the champion title.

What this means

Watch three things next. First, whether the champion designation is formalized and what specific benefits come attached to it. Second, how the scrutiny around Huang develops, and whether the White House keeps backing him if it intensifies further. Third, whether competitors push for similar recognition or for limits on how much policy weight a single company carries.

For now, Nvidia holds an unusual position: the biggest single backer of a federal AI program, a recipient of presidential honors and, soon, an officially recognized national champion.
