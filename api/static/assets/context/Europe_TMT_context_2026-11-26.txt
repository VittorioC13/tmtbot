[TITLE]Open source models to become more prevalent going forward: Entire's Dohmke:
[TEXT]
Thomas Dohmke, the former chief executive officer (CEO) of open-source platform GitHub, said there will be more open-source models going forward because of economic pressure and the models themselves becoming better. "They may not be catching up to frontier models, but they are reaching a quality level that makes them a viable substitute for many tasks. So, we're going to see that kind of shift, and then we're going to have software factories—where you can build certain things just by telling a factory of coding and review agents,” he told Business Standard in an interaction. According to Dohmke, open-source models lag in capital investment, pre-training, and data labelling because they lack the team strength and large clusters of graphics processing units (GPUs) needed for training, forcing them to rely on synthetic data. However, their advantages lie in lower costs, customisation for specific use cases, and better support for languages where frontier models fall short.

The debate over frontier and open-source models has been on the rise. Model orchestration has become an important topic in the artificial intelligence (AI) world as enterprises have found out that random use of tokens hardly improves efficiency or productivity. It, on the other hand, only burns the annual AI budget quicker than anticipated. That is leading them to use a variety of models—large, small, or open-source—according to the activity, which is often more effective and helps rein in costs.

Dohmke, who led GitHub for four years, stepped down last year to start his own startup Entire, which is a developer platform focused on agent-to-human collaboration. The company raised $60 million in seed funding earlier this year.

Unlike GitHub, which is designed for human-to-human collaboration, Entire is built for a world where millions of lines of code are written by agents and reviewed by humans in the loop.

“What we have seen in the last six months is that more and more code are coming from agents. Not only the code, but all the summaries, comments, and descriptions are also coming from agents,” Dohmke said.

According to him, code generation is now the responsibility of AI agents rather than human engineers, shifting the software engineer's role to orchestrating these specialised agents. This orchestration framework relies on two core components: a decentralised infrastructure layer that maintains global data residency for geopolitical and scaling needs, and a semantic layer that stores session logs and agent traces alongside the repository code.

That is why Entire launched repositories in the US, Europe, and Australia before putting India on its map. “Now we're excited to be here to also talk with many individual developers to be able to create the repository from the start on Entire in India,” he said.

For that, Entire has tied up with sovereign model company Sarvam. This will make Entire India’s first agent-native Git forge with data residency, storing code locally, while Sarvam serves inference for agent runs.

“With Entire and Sarvam, an engineering team can host its code in India and run its agents on models we train and serve here. For enterprises that need their code to stay in India, that opens up agent-assisted development even for their most sensitive repositories,” Sarvam cofounder Vivek Raghavan said in a statement.

[TITLE]Europe Tightens Smart Glasses Privacy Rules:
[TEXT]
Regulators in Europe are clamping down on smart glasses in response to growing concerns over covert filming, harassment, and breaches of digital privacy law.

AI glasses made by the likes of Meta and Snap are surging in popularity, but built-in cameras and microphones and potentially soon facial recognition, have drawn criticism from human rights and civil liberties advocates.

Charlie Whelton, policy lead on smart glasses at UK-based human rights organisation Liberty says: “Smart glasses facilitate covert filming of people without their consent. This is often focused on women and girls, or the harassment of vulnerable people for reactions on social media ...the current lack of regulation facilitates abuse.”

On 19 November, the Dutch Data Protection Authority issued an unprecedented warning advising consumers against buying smart glasses after its own study found they endangered the privacy and safety of bystanders and users.

Threading the needle between useful wearables and bystander privacy is important because successful smart glasses could be navigational aids for blind and low-vision wearers, help train robots, or act as real-time interpreters.

Europe converges on smart glasses restrictions

Earlier in November, the UK’s Minister for Safeguarding said the government is considering new restrictions on smart glasses in certain settings and limiting sales to certain users over concerns the technology is being used to surreptitiously film women. Several UK venues, including a major pub chain, have already restricted use of the eyewear.

Italy’s data protection watchdog, Garante, has called for a range of safeguards including changes to devices to prevent the automated publishing of captured footage online.

The toughest stance came from the Norwegian government, announcing plans for a temporary ban on smart glasses and other wearables in public spaces, including parks, beaches, museums, shopping malls, and places frequented by children.

National governments are responding to campaign groups, public discomfort, and emerging EU guidance.

The European Commission’s policy advisor, the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), issued a statement in October outlining its stance on the risks of smart glasses, backed up by findings in a report The connected gaze. It said the devices raise “significant concerns” around the protection of personal data and rights to privacy, security, freedom of expression, and freedom of movement.

Under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), a household exemption allows the use of AI glasses in a purely personal capacity, for example sharing photos with family or friends.

However, public use, including posting the videos on social media or using them for official, professional, political, or other non-personal activities makes the user a “data controller”, said EDPB, giving the wearer legal responsibility for compliance with obligations including transparency toward people whose data they are capturing. In practice that means, people recording in public must alert bystanders that they are recording personal data and for what purposes.

But manufacturers’ GDPR obligations cannot be shifted to users, explains Katie Hewson, partner at legal firm Stephenson Harwood: “A device that can record continuously, process biometric data, and transmit information to third-party servers – all without the knowledge of those being recorded – is in conflict with the regulatory framework, which is why regulators are flagging concerns.”

The data protection board called for a combination of new design features on devices to warn bystanders when they are recording, beyond the standard warning light on frames. Proposals include: brighter flashing lights that stop the recording if covered, audible voice commands for activation, beeps while recording, and a Bluetooth beacon that would notify nearby smartphones when the smart glasses are recording.

Smart glasses makers adapt to tighter rules

Device OEMs say they are on board with modifying their hardware and exploring less invasive designs and form factors.

“Some manufacturers are either changing designs or bringing in new designs, aware of an increasing backlash,” says Benjamin Hatton, connected devices research analyst at FDM CCS Insight. “One manufacturer told me that in order to get a footing in Europe they will probably need to offer a product that alleviates some of the concerns around cameras.”

Apple has reportedly delayed the launch of its first smart glasses to late 2027 to address privacy concerns. Meta, which dominates smart glasses with an 84% share of global shipments, updated its second generation Ray-Ban Meta frames software over the summer to prevent covert recording with a modified built-in LED.

The software detects a blocked, or modified, light and disables the camera. If the light is removed altogether, the camera shuts down permanently. Gentle Monster AI glasses, powered by Google, run a similar system.

Camera-free models are gaining traction. Meta has introduced audio-only frames, and China-based Even Realities specializes in camera-free smart glasses. Its founder Will Wang has argued that integrated cameras should wait until companies can put stronger protections in place.

But omitting cameras “completely changes the value proposition,” adds Hatton, making the eyewear “more akin to a note taker with a personal assistant built in.”

In its statement, the EDPB proposed additional technical measures to give bystanders more meaningful control over their personal data. These include limits on continuous recording and live streaming and blurring bystanders’ faces or avoiding processing their identities. Where possible, it said, processing should happen on the device, instead of on remote cloud servers.

“It’s really important that smart glasses technologies have privacy by design and by default built in from the outset, making it the responsibility of manufacturers and not leaving the burden of lawful use to individual users,” says Akvilė Šikšniūtė, policy officer for AI at The European Consumer Organisation (BEUC), which has formally urged EU authorities to investigate smart glasses. “It could mean minimizing the collection of personal data, prioritizing on device processing, or disabling unnecessary recording and storage functionalities by default to better protect the data of both the wearer and bystanders simultaneously.”

Incorporating such features could mean difficult design trade offs. Larger frames could make them less desirable to consumers, even as they allow additional functions. Next-generation chips powerful enough to handle local processing of data normally pushed to the cloud, could also be uncomfortably hot for wearers.

Existing chips already have sufficient processing power to handle safety features such as obscuring faces, says Hatton “if manufacturers chose to implement them.”

Spy-wear or fair to wear?

But not everyone is convinced that modifications to devices or tougher transparency rules will go far enough to protect consumers.

Berlin-based human rights organization HateAid is calling for a ban on sales of Meta AI glasses in Germany. The NGO filed a criminal complaint against Meta, Ray-Ban, Oakley, and several eyewear retailers in August arguing that they violate the Telecommunications, Digital Services, and Data Protection Act.

“Under this law it is prohibited to sell espionage gadgets that are hidden in everyday objects and intended to film people without their knowledge,” says Josephine Ballon, chief executive of HateAid, “This is exactly what smart glasses are designed for.”

A Meta spokesperson responded to the complaint indirectly in a Politico article, saying the smart glasses were approved by Germany’s Federal Network Agency in 2022 and were built with privacy safeguards that exceed those of a smartphone.

Expect the debate over covert filming, bystander consent, and GDPR compliance to gather pace over the coming months as European regulators continue to review the technology and how to police it. Meantime, engineers will be racing to preserve the beneficial uses of smart glasses while ensuring they don’t harm bystanders.

But manufacturers’ GDPR obligations cannot be shifted to users, explains Katie Hewson, partner at legal firm Stephenson Harwood: “A device that can record continuously, process biometric data, and transmit information to third-party servers – all without the knowledge of those being recorded – is in conflict with the regulatory framework, which is why regulators are flagging concerns.”

The data protection board called for a combination of new design features on devices to warn bystanders when they are recording, beyond the standard warning light on frames. Proposals include: brighter flashing lights that stop the recording if covered, audible voice commands for activation, beeps while recording, and a Bluetooth beacon that would notify nearby smartphones when the smart glasses are recording.

Smart glasses makers adapt to tighter rules

Device OEMs say they are on board with modifying their hardware and exploring less invasive designs and form factors.

“Some manufacturers are either changing designs or bringing in new designs, aware of an increasing backlash,” says Benjamin Hatton, connected devices research analyst at FDM CCS Insight. “One manufacturer told me that in order to get a footing in Europe they will probably need to offer a product that alleviates some of the concerns around cameras.”

Apple has reportedly delayed the launch of its first smart glasses to late 2027 to address privacy concerns. Meta, which dominates smart glasses with an 84% share of global shipments, updated its second generation Ray-Ban Meta frames software over the summer to prevent covert recording with a modified built-in LED.

The software detects a blocked, or modified, light and disables the camera. If the light is removed altogether, the camera shuts down permanently. Gentle Monster AI glasses, powered by Google, run a similar system.

Camera-free models are gaining traction. Meta has introduced audio-only frames, and China-based Even Realities specializes in camera-free smart glasses. Its founder Will Wang has argued that integrated cameras should wait until companies can put stronger protections in place.

But omitting cameras “completely changes the value proposition,” adds Hatton, making the eyewear “more akin to a note taker with a personal assistant built in.”

In its statement, the EDPB proposed additional technical measures to give bystanders more meaningful control over their personal data. These include limits on continuous recording and live streaming and blurring bystanders’ faces or avoiding processing their identities. Where possible, it said, processing should happen on the device, instead of on remote cloud servers.

“It’s really important that smart glasses technologies have privacy by design and by default built in from the outset, making it the responsibility of manufacturers and not leaving the burden of lawful use to individual users,” says Akvilė Šikšniūtė, policy officer for AI at The European Consumer Organisation (BEUC), which has formally urged EU authorities to investigate smart glasses. “It could mean minimizing the collection of personal data, prioritizing on device processing, or disabling unnecessary recording and storage functionalities by default to better protect the data of both the wearer and bystanders simultaneously.”

Incorporating such features could mean difficult design trade offs. Larger frames could make them less desirable to consumers, even as they allow additional functions. Next-generation chips powerful enough to handle local processing of data normally pushed to the cloud, could also be uncomfortably hot for wearers.

Existing chips already have sufficient processing power to handle safety features such as obscuring faces, says Hatton “if manufacturers chose to implement them.”

Spy-wear or fair to wear?

But not everyone is convinced that modifications to devices or tougher transparency rules will go far enough to protect consumers.

Berlin-based human rights organization HateAid is calling for a ban on sales of Meta AI glasses in Germany. The NGO filed a criminal complaint against Meta, Ray-Ban, Oakley, and several eyewear retailers in August arguing that they violate the Telecommunications, Digital Services, and Data Protection Act.

“Under this law it is prohibited to sell espionage gadgets that are hidden in everyday objects and intended to film people without their knowledge,” says Josephine Ballon, chief executive of HateAid, “This is exactly what smart glasses are designed for.”

A Meta spokesperson responded to the complaint indirectly in a Politico article, saying the smart glasses were approved by Germany’s Federal Network Agency in 2022 and were built with privacy safeguards that exceed those of a smartphone.

Expect the debate over covert filming, bystander consent, and GDPR compliance to gather pace over the coming months as European regulators continue to review the technology and how to police it. Meantime, engineers will be racing to preserve the beneficial uses of smart glasses while ensuring they don’t harm bystanders.

The Digital Duty of Care Act requires Big Tech to proactively reduce the risk of harm before it occurs, according to an Australian government statement.

Australian online law falls in line with EU and British legislation
