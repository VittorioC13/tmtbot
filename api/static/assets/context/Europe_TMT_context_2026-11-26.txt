[TITLE]Europe Tightens Smart Glasses Privacy Rules:
[TEXT]
Regulators in Europe are clamping down on smart glasses in response to growing concerns over covert filming, harassment, and breaches of digital privacy law.

AI glasses made by the likes of Meta and Snap are surging in popularity, but built-in cameras and microphones and potentially soon facial recognition, have drawn criticism from human rights and civil liberties advocates.

Charlie Whelton, policy lead on smart glasses at UK-based human rights organisation Liberty says: “Smart glasses facilitate covert filming of people without their consent. This is often focused on women and girls, or the harassment of vulnerable people for reactions on social media ...the current lack of regulation facilitates abuse.”

On 19 November, the Dutch Data Protection Authority issued an unprecedented warning advising consumers against buying smart glasses after its own study found they endangered the privacy and safety of bystanders and users.

Threading the needle between useful wearables and bystander privacy is important because successful smart glasses could be navigational aids for blind and low-vision wearers, help train robots, or act as real-time interpreters.

Europe converges on smart glasses restrictions

Earlier in November, the UK’s Minister for Safeguarding said the government is considering new restrictions on smart glasses in certain settings and limiting sales to certain users over concerns the technology is being used to surreptitiously film women. Several UK venues, including a major pub chain, have already restricted use of the eyewear.

Italy’s data protection watchdog, Garante, has called for a range of safeguards including changes to devices to prevent the automated publishing of captured footage online.

The toughest stance came from the Norwegian government, announcing plans for a temporary ban on smart glasses and other wearables in public spaces, including parks, beaches, museums, shopping malls, and places frequented by children.

National governments are responding to campaign groups, public discomfort, and emerging EU guidance.

The European Commission’s policy advisor, the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), issued a statement in October outlining its stance on the risks of smart glasses, backed up by findings in a report The connected gaze. It said the devices raise “significant concerns” around the protection of personal data and rights to privacy, security, freedom of expression, and freedom of movement.

Under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), a household exemption allows the use of AI glasses in a purely personal capacity, for example sharing photos with family or friends.

However, public use, including posting the videos on social media or using them for official, professional, political, or other non-personal activities makes the user a “data controller”, said EDPB, giving the wearer legal responsibility for compliance with obligations including transparency toward people whose data they are capturing. In practice that means, people recording in public must alert bystanders that they are recording personal data and for what purposes.

But manufacturers’ GDPR obligations cannot be shifted to users, explains Katie Hewson, partner at legal firm Stephenson Harwood: “A device that can record continuously, process biometric data, and transmit information to third-party servers – all without the knowledge of those being recorded – is in conflict with the regulatory framework, which is why regulators are flagging concerns.”

The data protection board called for a combination of new design features on devices to warn bystanders when they are recording, beyond the standard warning light on frames. Proposals include: brighter flashing lights that stop the recording if covered, audible voice commands for activation, beeps while recording, and a Bluetooth beacon that would notify nearby smartphones when the smart glasses are recording.

Smart glasses makers adapt to tighter rules

Device OEMs say they are on board with modifying their hardware and exploring less invasive designs and form factors.

“Some manufacturers are either changing designs or bringing in new designs, aware of an increasing backlash,” says Benjamin Hatton, connected devices research analyst at FDM CCS Insight. “One manufacturer told me that in order to get a footing in Europe they will probably need to offer a product that alleviates some of the concerns around cameras.”

Apple has reportedly delayed the launch of its first smart glasses to late 2027 to address privacy concerns. Meta, which dominates smart glasses with an 84% share of global shipments, updated its second generation Ray-Ban Meta frames software over the summer to prevent covert recording with a modified built-in LED.

The software detects a blocked, or modified, light and disables the camera. If the light is removed altogether, the camera shuts down permanently. Gentle Monster AI glasses, powered by Google, run a similar system.

Camera-free models are gaining traction. Meta has introduced audio-only frames, and China-based Even Realities specializes in camera-free smart glasses. Its founder Will Wang has argued that integrated cameras should wait until companies can put stronger protections in place.

But omitting cameras “completely changes the value proposition,” adds Hatton, making the eyewear “more akin to a note taker with a personal assistant built in.”

In its statement, the EDPB proposed additional technical measures to give bystanders more meaningful control over their personal data. These include limits on continuous recording and live streaming and blurring bystanders’ faces or avoiding processing their identities. Where possible, it said, processing should happen on the device, instead of on remote cloud servers.

“It’s really important that smart glasses technologies have privacy by design and by default built in from the outset, making it the responsibility of manufacturers and not leaving the burden of lawful use to individual users,” says Akvilė Šikšniūtė, policy officer for AI at The European Consumer Organisation (BEUC), which has formally urged EU authorities to investigate smart glasses. “It could mean minimizing the collection of personal data, prioritizing on device processing, or disabling unnecessary recording and storage functionalities by default to better protect the data of both the wearer and bystanders simultaneously.”

Incorporating such features could mean difficult design trade offs. Larger frames could make them less desirable to consumers, even as they allow additional functions. Next-generation chips powerful enough to handle local processing of data normally pushed to the cloud, could also be uncomfortably hot for wearers.

Existing chips already have sufficient processing power to handle safety features such as obscuring faces, says Hatton “if manufacturers chose to implement them.”

Spy-wear or fair to wear?

But not everyone is convinced that modifications to devices or tougher transparency rules will go far enough to protect consumers.

Berlin-based human rights organization HateAid is calling for a ban on sales of Meta AI glasses in Germany. The NGO filed a criminal complaint against Meta, Ray-Ban, Oakley, and several eyewear retailers in August arguing that they violate the Telecommunications, Digital Services, and Data Protection Act.

“Under this law it is prohibited to sell espionage gadgets that are hidden in everyday objects and intended to film people without their knowledge,” says Josephine Ballon, chief executive of HateAid, “This is exactly what smart glasses are designed for.”

A Meta spokesperson responded to the complaint indirectly in a Politico article, saying the smart glasses were approved by Germany’s Federal Network Agency in 2022 and were built with privacy safeguards that exceed those of a smartphone.

Expect the debate over covert filming, bystander consent, and GDPR compliance to gather pace over the coming months as European regulators continue to review the technology and how to police it. Meantime, engineers will be racing to preserve the beneficial uses of smart glasses while ensuring they don’t harm bystanders.

[TITLE]FCC Backs AT&T's Plan to Remove Copper Landlines in California:
[TEXT]
The Federal Communications Commission is backing AT&T’s push to override a California regulator so that the carrier can discontinue copper landline service to consumers.

On Friday, the FCC announced that it was preempting "specific requirements in California law that prevent consumers from transitioning off of unreliable, slow copper lines,” even as some residents fear they’ll be saddled with spotty cell service as an alternative.

AT&T argues that California’s "Carrier of the Last Resort” (COLR) rules prevent it from removing the old-school copper network that currently serves only 3% of California households, a number that continues to shrink. In May, the company urged the commission to declare that federal laws preempted California's regulations, thus clearing the way for AT&T to discontinue the service as it prioritizes high-speed fiber and expanded 5G cellular coverage.

Friday’s FCC order partially grants AT&T’s petition, agreeing that under federal law it has “explicit jurisdiction over the discontinuance of interstate telecommunications services.”

Still, the order refrains from “wholly” preempting California’s Carrier of the Last Resort rules, and seems to instead focus on the AT&T situation. “While we preempt those requirements that necessitate that AT&T obtain a variety of CPUC [California Public Utilities Commission] approvals before it can discontinue POTS [plain old telephone service] in accordance with federal authorization, we do not preempt the COLR and other obligations more generally,” the 74-page document says. (Photo by Kevin Dietsch/Getty Images)

The FCC issued the order, even though many locals opposed AT&T’s proposal to discontinue the landline service. “Please do not allow AT&T to eliminate the last reliable communication tool for rural residents. The VOIP and cellular infrastructure is not robust enough to provide...equal access for their communication needs,” one concerned user wrote in public comments.

The CPUC also told the FCC “nothing in California’s COLR rules prevents AT&T from retiring its copper infrastructure," even though it argued against preemption. CPUC’s main concern has been that the company’s replacement, called AT&T Phone - Advanced, won’t perform as well indoors compared with outdoor cell coverage, and it’ll cost more for consumers.

However, the FCC disagreed and was convinced that AT&T Phone - Advanced would be offered at a lower price, including “at a discounted rate of $25 per month to existing Lifeline customers in California,” while providing sufficient signal strength. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr also said: “Importantly, this decision does not come at the expense of consumer safeguards. The FCC’s rules require providers to notify consumers of proposed service discontinuances and provide an opportunity for public comment. We also preserve important protections for service reliability and public safety, including access to 911.”

Despite the FCC’s order, it’s not entirely clear if AT&T will pursue the copper landline phase-out in California. In May, the carrier sued to overturn the local regulations and block the CPUC from enforcing them against AT&T, which noted it spends $1 billion each year to maintain the copper network. That court case remains ongoing.

For now, AT&T EVP Rhonda Johnson praised Chairman Carr for the decision. “California deserves fast, reliable, future-ready technology – and with the right policies in place, we’re delivering it and transitioning away from copper," she said. [TEXT]
The US government was warned four years ago that security weaknesses in national telecommunications networks had created a “perilous” situation and left the country vulnerable to foreign spies, but it fought to keep a report detailing those concerns secret and didn’t address its findings, according to interviews and documents reviewed by Bloomberg News.

A June 2022 report commissioned by the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency concluded that US telecommunications networks contained “major structural vulnerabilities” that had created national security risks. “The fact that US national networks can be readily compromised from anywhere in the world is not yet widely understood,” states the report.

The report, which was disclosed as part of a lawsuit and reviewed by Bloomberg, was produced more than two years before revelations that a suspected Chinese hacking group known as Salt Typhoon had breached at least nine major U.S. telecommunications and broadband providers, including Verizon, AT&T and Lumen Technologies. The intruders targeted the phones of top politicians — including then presidential candidate Donald Trump — in what US officials described as the one of the worst cyber espionage cases in the recent history.

The Salt Typhoon hackers used an obscure telecom protocol called SS7, or Signaling System 7, which was one of the vulnerabilities identified in the report, for some early reconnaissance efforts, said a former US official familiar with the breaches. But they didn’t exploit it to gain access to US telecommunications networks, according to the person, and a second former official familiar with the matter. Both spoke on the condition they not be identified because of the sensitivity of the matter.

CISA withheld the report from public release for four years and resisted congressional pressure to publish it. Still, Senator Ron Wyden, a Democrat from Oregon whose office was briefed on the report’s findings in 2023 and pushed for its release, said it advocated an overhaul that could have helped shore up US networks.

“Had this report been made public when it was first written in 2022, Congress would have had ample time to require mandatory cybersecurity standards for phone companies, in time to prevent the Salt Typhoon hacks,” Wyden said in a statement last year. In a statement to Bloomberg this week, he said, “It is totally inexcusable that CISA stonewalled releasing this report for four years, while adversaries were spying on Americans.”

After fighting against its release, CISA provided a redacted version of the report to Washington-based Electronic Privacy Information Center, which sued to get it under the Freedom of Information Act. The details of the report haven’t been previously reported.

A CISA spokesperson declined to comment. Responding to EPIC’s lawsuit in court, the agency argued that the report contained privileged information and was exempt from disclosure. CISA said it didn’t act to adopt the report’s recommendations because “it makes technical claims without presenting the supporting information necessary to verify the claims.”

CTIA, an industry trade group formerly known as the Cellular Telecommunications Industry Association, said the report contains no new findings affecting US network security. “SS7 is not used in the 4G or 5G networks that Americans rely on daily for connectivity,” the group said in a statement, adding that the vulnerability has already been addressed through best practices and investments in “advanced wireless networks.”

Jeramie Scott, director of EPIC’s surveillance oversight program, accused the US government of doing little to address the problems outlined in the report, even as it “knows these vulnerabilities are actively being exploited by bad actors.” “The government has seemingly spent more effort trying to keep this report from the public than trying to protect the public against these known risks,” he said.

Kevin Briggs, a former senior CISA official involved in commissioning the report, said in an interview that he had repeatedly advocated inside the agency for it to be published and circulated among lawmakers. But he said his efforts were blocked by his superiors. Briggs said they told him that releasing the report would have a negative impact on CISA’s relationship with the telecommunications industry due to its criticisms of the sector’s cybersecurity and recommendations for greater regulation.

“Telecommunication is entwined with all of our infrastructure — it’s business intelligence, it’s national security, it’s financial,” said Briggs, who was laid off from CISA last year amid sweeping federal cuts made by Elon Musk’s Department of Government Efficiency. “If you can get into telecommunication networks and monitor or disrupt, you can potentially get into everything. We have to fix these things.”

The 111-page report, titled “US telecommunications insecurity 2022,” focuses in large part on technical vulnerabilities in SS7, a sort of switchboard for the global telecommunications industry that is used to route communications between phone networks.

First developed in the 1970s, SS7 contains numerous known vulnerabilities that governments and private surveillance companies have targeted to spy on phone calls and text messages, in addition to tracking people’s locations. Bloomberg News has previously reported on instances in which SS7 has been exploited to conduct covert surveillance of people’s mobile phones.

Firewalls and other available technologies can block SS7-based surveillance from occurring, but these aren’t uniformly implemented by all network operators. According to the report, the US is vulnerable to surveillance that exploits flaws in SS7, partly because there are many different telecommunications operators across the country, including small rural carriers, with varying levels of security. That means more “entry points” for bad actors, the report says.

The report says its findings were based on a review of up to 50 billion daily “telecom events,” and it concluded that the US was the most targeted country in the world in terms of “cyber exploits” seeking to exploit telecom protocols such as SS7. It cites outdated regulation and the complexity of the US telecommunications system as reasons for “this perilous and untenable situation for the US and those responsible for its protection.”

Several people in the US were apparently surveilled in espionage operations, according to the report. In March 2022, the report links an unnamed nation-state security agency to a case in which an individual carrying a Cyprus-registered mobile device was singled out for surveillance while apparently in Indianapolis. In another instance, in April 2022, phones linked to three people in the US were targeted in attempts to “covertly acquire the locations of the devices involved,” according to the report.

It also hints at the scope of the potential surveillance. During one week in May 2022, the report identified 9,000 suspicious incidents across US phone networks that it linked to several unnamed “malicious entities.” Some of the surveillance attempts outlined in the report were traced to an unnamed group in Southeast Asia.

The redacted version of the report reviewed by Bloomberg News excludes dozens of company names and other specific details, including recommendations for the telecom industry. But the report includes some unique codes associated with particular telecom companies, which can be used to identify them.

One of them, Texas-based Choice Wireless, was detected transporting “malicious signaling traffic,” a term defined in the report as attempts to conduct surveillance, such as eavesdropping on calls or messages and tracking locations.

Two people familiar with the report’s findings said that Choice had been flagged as a security concern because, according to the report, it had a commercial relationship with a Cypriot-Israeli company called Inno Networks. Inno’s chairman was also a cofounder of a surveillance technology company called Circles, known for selling governments services that enable them to spy on mobile phones. Circles has supplied surveillance services to several governments in authoritarian countries, according to advocacy groups.

Choice and other small US mobile operators had leased network infrastructure to foreign entities, according to the report. The foreign entities then allegedly used the leased networks as a kind of cloak that would hide their true intentions and identities. They connected to other American telecommunications networks while exploiting vulnerabilities in the SS7 protocol to conduct surveillance of people, according to the report, which added that it’s unclear whether Choice Wireless knew its network may have been commandeered for espionage.

Representatives of Choice Wireless and Inno Networks didn’t respond to requests for comment. Circles also didn’t respond to a request for comment.
