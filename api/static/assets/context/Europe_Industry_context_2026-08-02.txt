[TITLE]Keiron raised €20.7M to replace the process responsible for 70% of circuit board defects:
[TEXT]
TL;DR Eindhoven’s Keiron raised €20.7M Series A to scale laser-based solder paste printing that replaces stencils. First-pass yield jumps from ~60% to 95%+. AI hardware with 10,000+ interconnects needs it.

Keiron Printing Technologies, an Eindhoven-based deep-tech startup, raised €20.7 million in Series A funding to scale a laser-based solder paste printing system that replaces the stencil process used in electronics assembly for decades. The round was co-led by Invest-NL, DeepTechXL, and Waves Capital, with participation from Singapore’s ATUM Ventures. Solder paste printing is responsible for over 70% of printed circuit board assembly defects in a market worth more than €600 billion a year.

Keiron’s HF2 printer uses Laser-Induced Forward Transfer to deposit solder paste digitally and without contact, eliminating stencils entirely. The company says first-pass yield jumps from an industry-typical 60% to above 95%, production lead time halves, uptime doubles, and programming is 10 times faster. The technology originated from TNO Holst Centre, a collaboration between Dutch research organisation TNO and Belgian semiconductor institute imec.

The AI hardware connection is what makes this relevant beyond the factory floor. Next-generation AI chip packages carry up to 10,000 interconnects per component. At that density, conventional stencil printing breaks down and yields collapse. The AI boom is already straining every layer of the electronics supply chain, from memory to power to packaging. Solder paste printing is the bottleneck nobody outside manufacturing talks about, but it determines whether the chips that cost millions to design actually work when assembled into systems.

The funding will scale production and expand commercial operations across Europe, North America, and Asia-Pacific. Eindhoven is emerging as a European deep-tech hub, anchored by ASML, NXP, and the Brainport ecosystem. Keiron’s ambition is to become the global standard for electronics assembly. At €20.7 million, it is early. But if AI hardware keeps adding interconnects at the current rate, the stencil method it replaces will not survive the decade.

NEW YORK, July 31, 2026 /PRNewswire/ -- Ares Management Corporation (NYSE: ARES) announced today that Ares Credit funds (collectively "Ares") closed U.S. direct lending commitments of approximately $8.2 billion across 69 transactions during the second quarter of 2026 and approximately $52.3 billion across 347 transactions in the 12 months ended June 30, 2026. AeriTek is a leading international manufacturer of commercial refrigeration and foodservice equipment.

[TITLE]Ex-Anduril founders raise $30mn to build Europe’s synthetic battlefield for defence AI:
[TEXT]
A London startup building the training grounds for Europe’s autonomous weapons has come out of stealth with $30mn. It is one of the region’s largest defence-tech seed rounds this year.

Agon is not building the weapons themselves. It is building the infrastructure that trains them: a synthetic combat arena for AI-powered drones, missiles and uncrewed vehicles. There they can practise, take hits and harden before reaching a real battlefield, Resilience Media reported.

The raise splits into a $7mn pre-seed and a $23mn seed. Lakestar, Lux Capital, Northzone and XYZ Venture Capital are among the backers, with 201 Ventures and D3 in the earlier tranche. Lakestar’s Klaus Hommels has joined the board, tech.eu reported. The news was first reported by The Times.

The data bottleneck

The problem Agon is chasing is a shortage of the right data. Modern defence systems run on AI, from command software to the navigation and decision-making inside drones and missiles. All of it needs training data to improve. In defence, that data is scarce, often classified, and scattered across countries reluctant to share it.

This is one reason so many defence firms gravitate to Ukraine, where live combat generates the battlefield data their models crave. Agon’s pitch is to manufacture a version of that synthetically, in a secure environment. Nations could then train systems on their own confidential data, without depending on anyone else.

“We are in an AI arms race,” chief executive Tristam Constant said. “Agon is developing the infrastructure that will ensure European defence can iterate and orchestrate systems at the pace of the threat.”

Not another Scale AI

There is a wider bet buried in the model. Where firms like Scale AI built businesses labelling data for any and all models, Agon is wagering that defence AI will be far more specialised.

The logic is that a general-purpose data platform will not cut it. Gaming engines are built for games. Defence environments, the thinking goes, should be built for defence, tuned to the specific systems and end users they serve.

“Our launch today reflects the critical need for Europe to establish its own sovereign defence AI capability,” co-founder Junaid Hussain said. He added that the money would let the company scale and iterate its platform for European customers.

The pedigree

The founders are why investors leaned in. Constant led Applied Intuition’s European defence business, was a senior director for Europe at Anduril, and spent 13 years in the British Army before that.

Hussain is a serial entrepreneur who co-founded the defence startup Cambridge Aerospace and the AI gaming company Iconic. The chair is David Helgason, founder of the games-engine giant Unity. It is his first defence chairmanship, after a career of gaming bets.

The team, split between London and Berlin, draws on alumni of AWS, Google, Helsing and Palantir. Agon spent much of this year quietly poaching defence-tech talent from Germany.

A hot, unproven market

Investor appetite for the sector is running hot. Europe’s defence-tech funding hit a record as the war in Ukraine and a jittery geopolitical mood pulled capital in. Anduril and Helsing have soaked up billions.

Agon has not named a single customer. It says it has signed initial partnerships with European defence-autonomy companies. Given the founders’ ties, firms like Anduril and Helsing look like plausible early clients. For now, that remains unproven.

The open question is whether Agon’s specialised training layer becomes essential plumbing, or gets folded into the bigger platforms it hopes to serve. That is the bet its backers just made. The next year will start to answer it.
