[TITLE]Nvidia scandal puts spotlight on US-China AI chip rivalry:
[TEXT]
The controversy over Nvidia's former CEO Jensen Huang and an alleged affair with a suspected spy has exposed the intensifying AI rivalry between the US and China.

China has ambitious plans to become the leader in the global race for artificial intelligence (AI). Beijing is aware that the country needs cutting-edge chips to achieve this goal.

Vice Premier Ding Xuexiang, who chairs the Communist Party's Commission on Science and Technology, has been tasked with strengthening China's capacity for technological innovation, which would enhance its strategic competitiveness against the United States.

The party is currently using AI to monitor citizens, develop advanced unmanned weapons systems, and transform economic sectors through robotics and industrial automation, among other things.

But the US has so far been leading the way in manufacturing technology and chip design. And Washington has taken a slew of measures to retain its dominance in this strategic sector.

Why is the US restricting AI chip imports to China?

As early as 2023, the then Biden administration decided to restrict the export of advanced semiconductors and chip manufacturing equipment to China, citing national security reasons.

Beijing slammed the US restrictions, calling them "unilateral and arbitrary."

US chipmakers, however, have been drawn to China's vast and rapidly growing market.

Jensen Huang has emerged as a key adviser on AI to US President Donald Trump during his second term Image: Jung Yeon-je/AFP

Jensen Huang, who resigned as CEO of the American AI chip manufacturer Nvidia last week, enjoyed something of a rock star status in China. The 63-year-old, who was born in Taiwan, is viewed in China as a successful entrepreneur.

Huang, who has emerged as a key adviser on AI to US President Donald Trump during his second term, has pushed for looser regulations and fewer restrictions on selling AI chips to China.

In December, the Trump administration granted Nvidia permission to ship its H200 chips to China, provided the US got a 25% cut of sales.

Although the H200 is not part of Nvidia's highest-performance series, it is significantly more efficient than competing Chinese products.

Each of these chips carries a hefty $27,000 (€24,000) price tag, and Nvidia estimates demand in China at 2 million units, which corresponds to sales of approximately $5.4 billion from this product alone.

Jensen Huang's resignation and the Nvidia spy scandal

But Huang resigned last week following revelations of extramarital relationships, including one involving a businesswoman suspected by the US of spying for Beijing.

The scandal not only sent shockwaves through the global AI and semiconductor industries, but also fueled highly contentious political debates in China and the US.

Washington, which sees its national security threatened, is said to be planning further restrictions against China to close loopholes that allow Chinese firms to acquire advanced American semiconductors.

China, on the other hand, denied it engaged in an attempt to ensnare Huang, saying the spying allegations were unsubstantiated and a smear campaign against Beijing.

Beijing also stepped up its rhetoric ahead of the meeting between President Xi Jinping and Trump at this week's Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC) summit in the southern Chinese city of Shenzhen.

"China has consistently opposed the misuse of export control measures that jeopardize the stability of global industrial and supply chains, as this is not in the interest of either party," Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson Mao Ning said on November 13.

Some Chinese media outlets have also criticized the reporting on the issue.

"Jensen Huang cheated on his wife. What does that have to do with China?" asked China's state-run Global Times newspaper in an editorial.

It accused the US media of turning the affair into a "Hollywood thriller" but without providing the "hard evidence to back up the central allegations."

Many Chinese have also taken to the microblogging and social media platform Weibo to comment on the controversy.

A user wrote that Huang's downfall was, in essence, "the result of political maneuvering within the United States," while another user asked: "Could a love affair really cause Mr. Huang to resign? Isn't this plot a little too melodramatic?"

Is China buying more US chips?

Ding, the Chinese vice premier, has to decide whether China should continue to rely on US chips or back its domestic industry in the hope that it can achieve the breakthrough needed to surpass American competitors.

Around 600,000 Nvidia H200 chips, for instance, are currently sitting in Hong Kong, awaiting Chinese import approval.

The White House has already given the green light, but Beijing has yet to approve the imports.

For Karson Elmgren, research manager at the Institute for AI Policy and Strategy in Washington, that is puzzling. "To me, this suggests that China does not view short-term advantages as decisive and is confident in its ability to replace US chips over the medium term."

Chinese AI products are also increasingly using "Made in China" chips.

"This effectively mitigates the risks of cyberattacks by foreign actors," wrote Tiantian Wang of the Harbin Institute of Technology in a recent study on China's semiconductor strategy.

This is the case not only to civilian sectors. "Against the backdrop of the current complex geopolitical situation, strengthening autonomy and control over national defense and critical infrastructure has become a core element of the national security strategy," Wang explained.

According to her, for example, the radar systems in China's fifth-generation Chengdu J-20 fighter jet and in the Dongfeng-41 intercontinental ballistic missile are equipped exclusively with Chinese chips.

China is increasingly focused on autonomous AI chip platforms developed domestically, Wang underscored.

"This has created a closed ecosystem that encompasses data, algorithms, computing power, and chips, thereby reducing systemic dependence on foreign chips such as Nvidia," she added.

Chip manufacturing requires lithography systems that expose raw silicon materials to ultraviolet light.

Bowing to pressure from Washington, the Dutch maker of lithography machines, ASML, has stopped exporting its most advanced machines to China.

The ban helps secure the US's technological lead in AI chips, said the Center for Technology & Statecraft, a Washington-based think tank. According to a statement issued by the ministry on Monday, Tijani “advocated for a shared regional protocol for digital infrastructure resilience, including mechanisms for rerouting network traffic and coordinating repairs when subsea cables fail.”

The ministry said the initiative formed part of Nigeria’s efforts to strengthen regional digital infrastructure and improve the reliability and affordability of internet services across West Africa.

It disclosed that Nigeria was collaborating with Chad, Burkina Faso and the Republic of Benin to improve cross-border connectivity, with plans to extend the cooperation to Cameroon.

Nigeria is also working with Togo on plans to establish a regional data park to expand access to computing capacity for researchers and businesses across West Africa.

The ministry said the initiatives would strengthen regional economic integration and reduce vulnerabilities arising from dependence on limited international telecommunications infrastructure.

It noted that submarine cables carry more than 99 per cent of international data traffic, making their protection essential to the functioning of the global digital economy.

[TITLE]Telcos adopt equipment reuse to reduce emissions:
[TEXT]
Telecom operators are increasingly adopting equipment reuse and refurbishment to reduce network investment costs, limit waste and cut emissions as the industry faces pressure to meet its net-zero target by 2050.

The shift is moving operators away from the traditional practice of replacing equipment during network upgrades towards extending the lifespan of existing infrastructure, according to a Research Analyst at GSMA Intelligence, Muskan Gadodia.

In an article published on the GSMA website, Gadodia reported that the world’s 10 largest mobile network operators by subscriber base had introduced or supported more than 500 unconventional sustainability initiatives since 2020, either independently or through partnerships.

She identified equipment reuse, energy management, smarter procurement, redesigned network sites, environmental restoration and community clean-energy partnerships as six areas in which operators were expanding their sustainability efforts beyond conventional measures.

Under the equipment reuse approach, Telefónica’s MAIA platform and Orange’s OSCAR programme are helping to extend the lifespan of network equipment through reuse and refurbishment.

According to Gadodia, the initiatives can reduce waste, lower capital requirements and mitigate supply-chain risks.

The development is also drawing equipment manufacturers into operators’ sustainability programmes. Orange’s OSCAR programme, for instance, has established a formal refurbishment partnership with Nokia, reflecting the growing role of suppliers in efforts to extend the useful life of telecommunications infrastructure.

Gadodia said achieving net-zero emissions by 2050 remained one of the industry’s biggest challenges, despite operators’ investments in network efficiency and renewable energy.

“Achieving net-zero emissions by 2050 remains one of the telecom industry’s biggest challenges. Most mobile network operators are pursuing this goal through traditional measures such as improving network energy efficiency and increasing the use of renewable energy,” the analyst wrote.

Those measures alone might not be sufficient to close the industry’s remaining emissions gap, prompting operators to explore additional initiatives involving infrastructure, energy management and environmental restoration.

The analysis also highlighted efforts to turn telecommunications infrastructure into energy assets. Finland’s Elisa is using backup batteries at base stations in a virtual power plant model to support electricity grid balancing, while Deutsche Telekom is deploying waste heat from network facilities.

Other operators are incorporating environmental considerations into procurement decisions. Telefónica, for example, has introduced internal carbon pricing into its procurement processes, taking into account energy consumption, emissions and lifetime operating costs alongside purchase prices.

In Africa, Airtel Africa’s lean-site model in Nigeria was cited as an example of how operators are redesigning network infrastructure to reduce operational requirements. Japan’s NTT DOCOMO also operates a self-powered hydropower base station.

The sustainability drive extends to environmental monitoring and community energy projects. Orange Business and Tēnaka are collaborating on coral reef monitoring, while Vodafone has an environmental monitoring initiative in Scotland. In Kenya, Safaricom’s partnership with Sistema.bio is helping farmers access biodigesters through mobile-enabled payments and loyalty mechanisms.

The GSMA’s Mobile Net Zero report showed that the mobile industry’s operational emissions fell by eight per cent between 2019 and 2023, even as data traffic quadrupled. However, the industry needs to reduce emissions by 7.5 per cent annually by 2030, more than twice the average annual rate achieved so far, to reach net zero by 2050.

Gadodia said the unconventional initiatives were designed to complement, rather than replace, network modernisation and renewable energy adoption, as operators seek to reduce emissions across their wider operations and supply chains.

[TITLE]US to close AI chip loophole after Nvidia scandal:
[TEXT]
The United States announced tougher export controls on AI chips on Monday after a scandal involving Nvidia co-founder Jensen Huang exposed a loophole allegedly exploited by Chinese companies.

The news came the day before Californian chip titan Nvidia reports its first earnings since Huang’s departure as CEO, with investors questioning if the artificial intelligence boom can hold strong.

Under Washington’s new rules, data centres anywhere in the world will need a US license to provide large volumes of computing power from advanced chips to companies headquartered in China, Macau or other countries under US arms embargoes.

The rules also require licenses to ship advanced chips to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Brazil, countries the US Commerce Department says present a “higher-than-normal risk of diversion” of the chips.

These changes are a response to a loophole that allowed Chinese firms to rent AI computing power from data centres in Southeast Asia and elsewhere that run on US hardware not available for export to China.

While this loophole has been known to authorities, the revelation of Nvidia co-founder Huang’s extramarital affair with a woman suspected of being a Chinese spy has drawn intense scrutiny to the practice.

The woman, Alice Huang, is a former executive of Megaspeed — a Singapore-based cloud computing firm under US criminal investigation for allegedly helping Chinese companies procure advanced Nvidia AI chips in violation of export controls.

Jensen Huang, 63, stepped down last week after at least two extramarital affairs were revealed in media reports.

Facing criticism, the US Commerce Department said Monday it would close a loophole that had drawn concern from US authorities.
