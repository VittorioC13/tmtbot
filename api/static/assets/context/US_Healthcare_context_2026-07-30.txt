[TITLE]Scandal Exposes Limits of China’s Scientific Rise:
[TEXT]
The highlights this week: The death of a Chinese child in a gene-editing trial sparks outrage, COVID-19 cases surge across China, and South Korea’s stock market plummets in response to Chinese chipmaking advances.

Welcome to Foreign Policy’s China Brief.

The highlights this week: The death of a Chinese child in a gene-editing trial sparks outrage, COVID-19 cases surge across China, and South Korea’s stock market plummets in response to Chinese chipmaking advances.

Fatal Gene-Editing Trial Sparks Outrage

A joint investigation by Science and Retraction Watch into the death of a Chinese child during a gene-editing trial—and the subsequent cover-up—has sparked outrage online.

Six-year-old Mei, a pseudonym, had a genetic defect that caused moderate learning difficulties. Her parents, a well-off professional couple, enrolled her in a gene-editing trial led by prominent neuroscientist Zilong Qiu at Xinhua Hospital in Shanghai, which is affiliated with the elite Shanghai Jiao Tong University.

Mei died during the trial from a severe immune reaction after researchers ignored warning signs that had emerged in animal trials. Those involved in the trial concealed Mei’s death, omitting her data when they submitted a paper on the trial to Nature. After being stonewalled by the university and the hospital, Mei’s parents turned to Western media.

The story has received extensive coverage in Chinese media in the last few days, though it has not yet appeared in the most official outlets such as Xinhua or People’s Daily, suggesting that authorities are still deciding what the official response should be.

China is a rising leader in the field of biotechnology, though not yet the dominant power many expected a decade ago. At that time, Western firms and researchers were eager to partner with Chinese scientists, attracted by both generous state funding and what they perceived as a more permissive regulatory environment with fewer bioethical constraints.

That perception changed dramatically in 2018 with the He Jiankui scandal. A well-known scientist, He became the first to edit the genomes of human embryos, altering them in an attempt to generate resistance to HIV infection. He said he received ethics approval, but the institutions involved later denied authorizing the work.

He’s approach reflected a strategy that has often succeeded in China: make a bold, attention-grabbing breakthrough first, then hope the authorities decide to take credit for it after the fact. But that backfired for He—perhaps in part because he made the mistake of breaking the story with The Associated Press, rather than a Chinese outlet.

He was condemned for his actions, including forging documents, sentenced to three years in prison, and fined $429,000. Qiu, the leader of the Shanghai trial, was among more than 100 Chinese scientists who signed an open letter condemning He, writing that he “completely ignored the principles of biomedical ethics, conducting experiments on humans without proving it’s safe.”

Unlike He’s case, Qiu’s experiment received approval from the hospital’s ethics board, which also seems to have been involved in concealing Mei’s death. The cover-up is, unfortunately, unsurprising.

Across China’s bureaucracy, the instinct is to suppress bad news—even when those involved bear little responsibility—out of fear of becoming a scapegoat. In medical contexts, such concealment is particularly dangerous and can have calamitous consequences, as was the case in the early days of the COVID-19 pandemic.

Failure to publish negative or null results is a long-standing problem across the scientific field, but China has a particularly bad reputation for covering up negative trial data and, in some cases, fabricating results. Chinese researchers account for a disproportionate share of retracted academic papers.

The Shanghai trial also bears the hallmarks of China’s pervasive medical corruption. Mei’s parents were asked to pay for the experimental treatment, which is not unusual in cutting-edge clinical research. More troublingly, they were also asked to send money to individual researchers, including more than $130,000 to a single team member, and routinely offered the team expensive gifts.

Altogether, the family spent more than $860,000. (The researcher who received the largest payment returned the money after Mei’s death.)

These practices—though usually involving much smaller sums—are routine in China’s healthcare system, where informal payments are often viewed as necessary to receive quality treatment.
