[TITLE]What tech leaders need to know from Budget 2026:
[TEXT]
It’s all in the title.

Masters of Our Destiny, Ottawa’s 2026 budget tabled on Nov. 5 by Finance Minister François-Philippe Champagne, is being presented as strongly focused on sovereignty and strengthening Canada’s economy at home.

And for the country’s CIOs and digital decision-makers, there are several measures that reach into how tech is funded, developed, bought, and managed.

While some measures are already in motion, others still need legislation, CRA guidance, or detailed rules before organizations know exactly what they will have to implement.

Ottawa is putting money behind its own IT overhaul

Digital Transformation Canada (DTC) launched in September with a mandate to improve how the federal government develops, buys, and uses technology.

The new organization brings together “key digital groups across the public service,” including Shared Services Canada and the Canadian Digital Service.

The budget proposes $1 billion over five years, starting in 2026-27, for a Government Transformation Fund to accelerate federal IT projects, focusing on improving both delivery and productivity of public services.

It also includes $17.6 million over five years for a fellowship program through Build Canada Exchange that would bring technical experts, professionals, and academics into government for priority projects.

Ottawa intends to introduce a Digital Transformation Canada Act covering the delivery, management, and oversight of digital and IT services across government.

The government says DTC will lead adoption of AI and other emerging technologies across the public service, while developing and testing tools for use across departments.

For vendors, the budget does not spell out how procurement will work inside the new structure.

The Council of Canadian Innovators has been calling for such an organization since 2020, says Daniel Perry, director of federal affairs for CCI, in a budget debrief on Nov. 6.

CCI had asked Ottawa before the budget to make federal purchasing decisions account for Canadian intellectual property, domestic economic impact, and long-term capacity.

Right now, he says, their focus is “making sure the structure is right,” and that key details will be included, including the role of procurement, how funding will be allocated, and how Canadian companies and talent will be integrated.

New measures for fraud, data portability, and payments

The budget also moves several long-running files forward, including fraud prevention, data portability, and payments modernization.

Ottawa is introducing a legislated Multi-Sector Anti-Fraud Framework covering financial institutions, telecom providers, and digital platforms.

Organizations will have to prevent, detect, disrupt, and respond to fraud, working across sectors to resolve fraud complaints.

The government says it will consult on draft legislation and amendments before introducing them, with more information on the strategy coming in 2027.

Proposed amendments to the Bank Act will mean consumers have $0 liability for transactions resulting from unauthorized access to a bank account unless gross negligence is involved. It also plans to amend the Telecommunications Act to give the CRTC authority to set fraud-prevention obligations for telecom providers.

The budget also proposes expanding data portability rights to telecom and air travel, so Canadians can more easily switch providers and securely reuse their information with a new service.

Canada’s Real-Time Rail fast-payment system launches today, allowing individuals and businesses to send data-rich payments instantly with immediate access to funds. Ottawa also expects to begin a public consultation on the next phase of payments modernization in early 2027.

Expanding support for R&D and compute

Budget 2026 confirms the Productivity Mega Deduction announced in September. It allows businesses to immediately write off the full cost of a broader range of capital assets, representing more than 65% of investment, on a permanent basis.

Since the deduction applies to new technology, equipment, R&D, software, and more, the government says it “strengthens the business case” for modernizing, and encourages investment and expansion in Canada.

The budget also expanded the Scientific Research and Experimental Development (SR&ED) tax incentive, following reforms that took effect earlier this year.

Some commercialization costs would become eligible, including work needed for initial regulatory approval or certification and some costs to protect and maintain IP developed through SR&ED.

For large-scale R&D and innovation projects in sectors like AI, agri-food, bio-manufacturing, and clean technologies, Ottawa is putting $1 billion into the Strategic Response Fund.

The government also wants to make more compute available to Canadian AI companies. Large tech companies are securing most of the country’s new AI infrastructure because they can afford longer contracts and rising costs, leaving smaller Canadian companies with fewer options.

The government will use financial tools like backstops (which reduce project risk) and offtake agreements (which commit buyers to future capacity) to secure access to 500 MW of sovereign AI compute for Canadian AI companies.

A first step

Budget measures related to Canadian tech and innovation were largely seen as a positive step by the ecosystem, though plenty of questions remain on how these will be implemented or delivered.

On LinkedIn, KPMG’s Head of AI Research Dr. Andrew Forde gave a word of caution.

“We should be careful not to add our own hopes and biases to what actually appeared in today’s Government of Canada’s budget on AI,” he wrote, outlining specific budget items.

“My read is the government has now put money, targets, infrastructure contracts, regulation and public procurement around AI, but the delivery and measurement layer is still incomplete, particularly standards, assurance, health data and evidence of actual productivity gains.”

Build Canada, a non-partisan volunteer organization of entrepreneurs and policy experts, welcomed the budget’s recognition of entrepreneurship as an economic driver, and the government’s adoption of key tax reforms they’ve been calling for.

“A Canadian who sells a company and starts the next one now has more reason to do it here,” said Lucy Hargreaves, co-founder and CEO of Build Canada. “That is how a country creates a strong innovation ecosystem and gets a generation of repeat builders to stay and build here.”

In an initial statement on behalf of CCI members, Perry said the budget was a step in the right direction.

“There is still important work to do to make Canada the best place in the world to scale an innovative company and to govern 21st-century economic realities, including data, strategic standards, financial infrastructure, and intellectual property, but this budget is a significant step in the right direction,” he said.

In the debrief, CCI Vice President of Policy and Advocacy Laurent Carbonneau asked how large Canadian corporations could be part of the country’s wider AI infrastructure build-out.

“How can we take, you know, our big Canadian corporates, including… banks and telecoms, and get them to be better customers of Canadian companies,” he asked.

“I think you have many of our CCI companies who sell to telecoms and banks abroad, and have very little difficulty in doing so, and I think we have a bit of an ‘invented here’ syndrome, where if it’s Canadian, then Canadian companies [have] little interest in it, which I think is precisely the wrong attitude to take, and precisely the wrong bias to have.”

At the end of the debrief, Carbonneau said that public procurement in Canada at about 12-15% of GDP (a stat he’s presented before), and that public policy and strategic procurement is being leveraged to give Canadian companies a fair shot.

“Even if a small amount of that is used really, really well, that’s worth almost more than any boutique innovation program could possibly be.”

Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC) has approved investments of up to $1 billion in two major artificial intelligence companies, Anthropic and OpenAI, as the world’s largest dedicated semiconductor foundry expands its strategic presence in the technology ecosystem.

In an official statement, TSMC said its board approved an investment of up to $500 million in Anthropic to acquire its common shares at the company’s per-share initial public offering price.

The board also approved an investment of up to $500 million in OpenAI to acquire its preferred stock. The two investments together could amount to $1 billion.

It stated, “Approved the investments in (1) Anthropic, PBC at its per-share initial public offering price in an amount not to exceed $500 million to acquire Anthropic’s common shares; and (2) OpenAI Group PBC in an amount not to exceed $500 million to acquire OpenAI’s preferred stock”.

Artificial intelligence company Anthropic has announced the launch of its initial public offering (IPO) roadshow, seeking to raise up to $64.35 billion through the sale of 825 million shares, with an expected price range of $70 to $78 per share.

According to the company’s official announcement, the proposed offering comprises 825,000,000 shares of Class A common stock, including 801,351,354 shares being offered by Anthropic and 23,648,646 shares being sold by certain existing stockholders.

Alongside these investments, TSMC’s board also approved capital appropriations of approximately $40.93 billion ($40,926.70 million) to support its long-term capacity requirements.

The company stated that the planned spending will mainly go towards installing and upgrading advanced technology capacity, expanding advanced packaging and mature or speciality technology capacity, and constructing new fabrication facilities.

The approved capital allocation will also cover 2027 research and development investments, sustaining capital expenditure and capitalised leased assets.

TSMC also approved an investment of up to $500 million to establish an investment company that will make strategic investments in the semiconductor industry and its related ecosystem.

The company’s board approved its third-quarter 2026 financial results. TSMC reported consolidated revenue of $45.50 billion (NT$1,494.24 billion) and net income of $23.23 billion (NT$763.06 billion).

Diluted earnings per share stood at $0.90 (NT$29.43).

The board approved a cash dividend of $0.27 (NT$9.0) per share for the third quarter of 2026. The dividend will be paid on April 8, 2027, with March 17, 2027, set as the record date for eligible common stock shareholders.

Founded in 1987, TSMC is the leading manufacturer of semiconductors. The company is headquartered in Hsinchu, Taiwan

[TITLE]Anthropic: Anthropic expands Claude into spreadsheets, dashboards and collaborative workplace tools:
[TEXT]
Artificial intelligence company Anthropic has expanded its Claude platform with new tools for creating spreadsheets , digital whiteboards and data dashboards, broadening its capabilities in workplace productivity and collaboration.The new features, announced on Tuesday, allow users to create and edit spreadsheets, organise ideas collaboratively and generate visual reports within Claude. The move extends the platform beyond answering queries and generating text towards handling tasks traditionally performed through office productivity applications.The expansion puts Claude in the same workplace productivity space as Microsoft's 365 suite and Google's Workspace, whose AI assistants, Copilot and Gemini, help users create documents, analyse spreadsheets and prepare presentations.Anthropic's Claude Sheets brings spreadsheet capabilities similar to those of Microsoft Excel and Google Sheets into the Claude platform. It allows teams to organise data collaboratively, create and edit formulas, and work across multiple spreadsheet tabs.Claude Whiteboards offers a shared digital space, similar to collaborative whiteboarding tools, where teams can organise ideas during meetings and ask Claude to summarise their discussions.Claude Data, formerly known as Dashboards, extends the platform into data analysis and visualisation, connecting with services such as Snowflake and Salesforce to generate dashboards, visual data stories and short narrated videos using definitions certified by an organisation's data team.While Microsoft and Google offer AI capabilities across their established productivity suites, Anthropic is bringing spreadsheets, whiteboards and dashboards together within Claude, allowing users to carry context between different formats without repeating instructions.The company said Claude can also create customised workplace tools such as account trackers, daily briefings, project status boards and contract review tables, allowing teams to build tools suited to their specific requirements.Anthropic is also introducing interactive connectors that allow partner applications to be embedded directly within Claude. These include integrations with Adobe and Atlassian, while plugins from Salesforce, NetSuite and Workday are designed to help sales, finance and human resources teams perform tasks within the platform."Claude works around you. It knows your team's work through the tools you connect, helps you bring ideas to life in the formats you need, and runs within the controls your admins set," the company said.For businesses, Anthropic is adding administrative controls to manage employee access to applications, limit the actions Claude can perform and set organisational spending limits. Enterprise data is encrypted in transit and at rest, while usage can be monitored through audit logs.The workplace expansion comes as Anthropic prepares to enter public markets. The company launched its IPO roadshow on Tuesday, seeking to raise up to USD 64.35 billion through the sale of 825 million shares at a proposed price range of USD 70-78 per share. It has applied to list on Nasdaq under the ticker symbol "ANT".
