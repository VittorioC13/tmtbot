[TITLE]CBN sets N15bn capital requirement for dollar stablecoins:
[TEXT]
The Central Bank of Nigeria has introduced a minimum paid-up capital requirement of N15bn for companies issuing foreign currency-backed stablecoins, including those pegged to the United States dollar, as part of new regulations governing virtual assets and digital payment services in Nigeria.

The requirement is contained in the Regulations for the Operations of Non-Security Virtual Assets in Nigeria, released by the apex bank on Friday, following a circular dated 5 November, 2026, and signed by the Director of the Payments System Policy Department, Musa Jimoh.

Under Section 15.2 of the regulations, the CBN stated, “A Class 2 Licensed Issuer shall maintain minimum paid-up capital of fifteen billion Naira (N15,000,000,000), or such higher amount as the Bank may prescribe, on entry and at all times thereafter.”

The provision applies to Class 2 stablecoins, which are digital tokens designed to maintain a stable value against foreign currencies, including the US dollar.

The regulations also prescribe a lower capital threshold of N10bn for issuers of naira-referenced stablecoins, classified as Class 1, while companies providing other non-security virtual asset services must maintain at least N1bn in paid-up capital.

The CBN stated, “A Class 1 Licensed Issuer shall maintain paid-up capital of ten billion Naira (N10,000,000,000), or such higher amount as the Bank may prescribe, on entry and at all times thereafter.”

For other virtual asset service providers, the regulations specify that the N1bn requirement must be maintained separately from capital committed to licences issued by the Securities and Exchange Commission or other regulatory authorities.

The new framework establishes different capital requirements for stablecoin issuers and other service providers, reflecting the varying financial and operational risks associated with their activities.

It also empowers the CBN to demand higher capital from operators based on their transaction volumes, market significance, redemption obligations, technology risks and exposure to the broader financial system.

According to the document, “Capital shall be separate from and additional to issuer reserves, the CBN reserve deposit, customer funds and assets and operational liquidity.”

This means operators cannot use the same funds to satisfy multiple regulatory obligations, including capital adequacy and reserves backing stablecoins already in circulation.

The regulations further require eligible capital to be held as cash in designated accounts with the CBN or in approved Federal Government securities subject to the bank’s control.

Operators whose capital falls below the prescribed minimum must notify the CBN immediately and submit a restoration plan within 10 business days. The regulator may restrict new token issuance or customer onboarding until compliance is restored.

Beyond capital requirements, the CBN introduced strict rules on the assets backing stablecoins, requiring issuers to maintain reserves equivalent to at least 100 per cent of outstanding obligations, alongside an additional two per cent buffer against potential losses.

It stated, “No Licensed Issuer shall issue new stablecoin tokens (mint) unless, at the time of minting, equivalent Eligible Reserve Assets have been received and held in the Reserve Pool.”

Stablecoin holders must also be allowed to redeem their tokens at face value, generally no later than the next business day after a valid request, without charges for standard redemptions.

The regulations prohibit algorithmic stablecoins that rely primarily on supply adjustments rather than adequate reserve assets, alongside tokens linked to fiat currencies but backed mainly by other cryptocurrencies.

The CBN also established additional supervisory requirements for stablecoins whose circulating value exceeds N500bn for 30 consecutive days or which are used by more than five million people in Nigeria.

Such instruments will be classified as systemically important, attracting enhanced regulatory scrutiny.

The framework further covers cryptocurrency exchanges, digital wallets, payment gateways, remittances, custody services and platform-facilitated peer-to-peer transactions.

It introduces obligations relating to anti-money laundering, terrorist financing, customer identification, transaction monitoring, cybersecurity, consumer protection and regulatory reporting.

The rules apply to both domestic and foreign operators offering regulated services to Nigerians, regardless of whether they maintain a physical office in the country.

However, the CBN clarified that securities-related virtual assets would remain under the SEC’s jurisdiction, with both regulators coordinating where their responsibilities overlap.

[TITLE]Europe’s chip design platform lines up €525 million for fabless startups:
[TEXT]
The EU-backed EuroCDP aims to give small chip designers tools, funding and a path to manufacturing through 2028

Europe has a new plan to grow its own chip designers, and it comes with a price tag of €525 million.

The European Chips Design Platform, known as EuroCDP, is set to mobilize that sum to help fabless startups, small and mid-sized companies, and research groups design semiconductors without first raising a fortune.

What EuroCDP actually does

EuroCDP sits under the EU Chips Act and is scheduled to run from 2025 to 2028. Belgian research hub imec coordinates the effort, leading a consortium of 12 partners across Europe.

That roster includes some of the heaviest names in European applied research: France’s CEA, Germany’s Fraunhofer and Silicon Austria Labs.

The platform’s core job is to lower the barriers to entry for companies that design chips but do not own factories. These are known as fabless firms. They sketch the blueprints and rely on someone else to do the actual manufacturing.

To help them, EuroCDP offers three main resources. The first is electronic design automation, or EDA, tools. These are the software suites engineers use to design chips. The second is intellectual property libraries. These are pre-built, pre-tested chip components that designers can drop into their own projects instead of reinventing them from scratch. The third is access to pilot line technologies for fabrication and testing. Pilot lines are smaller-scale production setups where a new design can be built and checked before anyone commits to mass manufacturing.

The platform is aimed at strategic areas, with artificial intelligence and high-performance computing, or HPC, singled out.

The money and how it splits

The €525 million figure is the total investment plan. Of that, approximately €220 million is allocated by the EU for startup support through various funding vehicles.

For eligible projects, the funding follows a three-way structure. The EU contributes 35%, national governments provide 35% in matching funds, and the remaining 30% comes from self-funding or venture capital.

Software support is also part of the package. Synopsys, one of the major EDA vendors, is set to provide its design software for AI and HPC applications through EuroCDP, with a start date of November 4, 2026.

The arrangement is meant to cover projects from the early stages all the way through to pre-production.

Why Europe is doing this

The backdrop is a funding mismatch that European policymakers have been staring at for a while. European integrated circuit startups comprise around 30% of the global market, yet they attract only about 6% of available funding.

EuroCDP is also tied to a bigger target. Under the Chips for Europe Initiative, the EU aims to double its share of the semiconductor market to 20% by 2030.

The platform is framed as part of Europe’s push for digital sovereignty, meaning less dependence on chip design and manufacturing ecosystems outside the EU.

What this means for startups, investors and the EU’s 2030 goal

For early-stage chip designers, the most immediate effect is cost relief. Shared access to EDA software, IP libraries and pilot lines could shorten the expensive stretch between founding a company and producing a working sample.

The Synopsys arrangement is worth watching on its own. Access to a major vendor’s tools through a public platform could narrow the gap between a European startup and a well-funded rival elsewhere, at least on the design side. The November 4, 2026 start date also means the full benefit lands in the second half of the platform’s 2025 to 2028 window.

What to watch next: how quickly the approximately €220 million in startup support actually reaches companies, how many national governments step up with their 35% matching share, and whether venture firms show up for the remaining 30%.

[TITLE]Streaming Coalition Urges Squires to Support Enforcement Against Abusive SEP Licensing Practices:
[TEXT]
“Once the standard is widely adopted, [these SEP holders] renege on that promise and demand excessive royalties on threat of an injunction.” – Coalition letter

A coalition of ten streaming, broadcasting, and audiovisual media organizations, including the Motion Picture Association (MPA) and the Streaming Innovation Alliance, sent a letter today to U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) Director John Squires, urging him to “support strong public and private sector enforcement against abusive SEP licensing practices.” The signatories said that abuse of standard essential patents (SEPs) was undercutting the open process of setting standards for the video streaming technologies their members use.

In December 2025, the USPTO established an SEP Working Group that reports directly to Director Squires. The agency said the group would continue its practice of filing court statements supporting SEP injunctions, such as the statement of interest filed in Radian Memory Systems v. Samsung Electronics in June 2025.

The remaining signatories were the Association of Commercial Television and Video on Demand Services in Europe, the Commercial On-Demand and Broadcasting Association, Strima, the Asociación Española de Video on Demand, the European Video On Demand Coalition, Streaming for Australia, Streamingdiensten Nederland and the Television Association of Programmers Latin America. If left unaddressed, the letter said SEP abuse would harm consumers and competition, significantly affect investment in local creative economies, “misappropriate millions” intended for creative content and new offerings, and lower the quality of the viewing experience.

Standardized codec technologies such as AVC and HEVC allow competing streamers to provide dependable, high-quality video on devices from many manufacturers, the signatories explained. Since inclusion in a standard enhances a patent’s market power by requiring every potential competitor to license the technology, the coalition wrote, SEPs must be licensed on fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory (FRAND) terms.

According to the coalition, a growing list of SEP holders, many of which it said invest primarily in lawyers and litigation, had abandoned that principle for video streaming standards. These SEP holders commit to FRAND licensing to convince standards bodies, such as the International Organization for Standardization and the International Telecommunications Union, to incorporate their patented technology into industry standards, and then “once the standard is widely adopted, they renege on that promise and demand excessive royalties on threat of an injunction,” the letter stated. The coalition said those injunctions result from lawsuits filed in a handful of jurisdictions that routinely grant them without prompt review of the asserted patents’ validity or before a final infringement determination.

The signatories described this conduct as a “bait-and-switch patent ‘holdup’” affecting streamers bound to a standard for years, noting that AVC and HEVC were published in 2003 and 2013, respectively. According to the letter, the practice imposes large costs by destabilizing streaming technology standards, raising consumer prices, potentially diminishing commercial offerings and chilling streaming innovation while the industry remains in lengthy litigation.

The coalition stated that “the threat isn’t theoretical,” pointing to advisory FRAND royalty guidelines recently issued by a European court, identified in the letter’s footnotes as the Munich Regional Court. “Without any supporting evidence,” the letter said, the court proposed an annual codec royalty for two leading subscription video on demand (SVOD) services at 16% of one-third of annual subscription revenues, increasing to 25% for premium services. The signatories cited a Statista figure projecting nearly $100 billion in global SVOD revenues for 2026 and said that applying only the lower rate across the industry would drain well over $5 billion from the entertainment sector.

The 7th Civil Chamber of the Munich Regional Court I published the guidelines on August 13, 2026, building on recent decisions including ASUS I, ASUS II, Renault and ZTE v. Samsung. The guidelines reflect only that chamber’s views, and other courts may reach different conclusions.

All signatories support the protection of intellectual property (IP) rights, the coalition stated, adding that “it is precisely because we take IP rights so seriously” that they object to holdup tactics. The signatories said those tactics far exceed the value of the underlying patents, raise competition concerns, and damage the reputation of IP rights. The letter also referenced an amicus curiae brief the MPA submitted to the U.S. District Court in Delaware, which it said explained the conduct in detail.

That brief was filed in Disney Enterprises, Inc. v. InterDigital, Inc., an antitrust lawsuit Disney filed in August 2025 alleging that InterDigital’s licensing practices for video compression patents violated Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act.

According to the letter, these SEP holders’ conduct is “cultivating a new generation of opponents for the IP community,” including car makers, TV manufacturers, healthcare providers and farmers. The signatories said they need to facilitate a FRAND licensing framework that respects IP rights and allows the streaming ecosystem to benefit creators, innovators, consumers, and IP rights holders. The coalition urged Director Squires to support public and private sector enforcement against abusive SEP licensing practices.
