[TITLE]What Would a U.S. Telecommunications Act 2.0 Look Like?:
[TEXT]
In February 1996, U.S. President Bill Clinton signed the Telecommunications Act, which boosted competition and loosened broadcasting requirements. It also set a baseline of telecom service everyone should have and set up funding to pay for it.

Thirty years later, people are still fighting over the Act’s provisions for connecting the hardest-to-reach places to telephone networks and the internet. Especially contentious today are debates over which technologies—fiber-optic lines, wireless broadband, or satellite networks like Starlink—are the best investment for the most number of customers.

A key component of the Telecommunications Act was its Universal Service Fund (USF), paid for with a surcharge on telephone users. Lawmakers wanted the USF to bridge the remaining gaps keeping telecom networks from reaching everyone.

Much has changed in the three decades since the bill became a law. Landlines are a distant memory for the masses, the USF’s funding is increasingly precarious, and the FCC continues to spend around US $8 billion or 9 billion a year trying to close the telecom access gap.

So what would a hypothetical Telecom Act 2.0 do differently today?

It Would Fix the Universal Service Fund

“The contribution base to the USF must be fixed,” says Joey Wender, executive director of the Schools, Hospitals & Libraries Broadband Coalition in Washington, D.C. “Congress should consider assessing both broadband internet-access providers and tech companies.”

The shortfall in USF revenue Wender describes stems from the fact that the original Act taxed a now-outdated technology—landlines. Because so much present-day communication takes place over broadband, which the FCC does not charge the same way, the FCC has had to boost its current USF tax to a staggering 42 percent, compared to 8 percent in 1996.

Industry consultant Carol Mattey, a former FCC official involved in writing the 1996 Act, proposes that the FCC start charging a fee to companies that depend on broadband. This move, she says, would help fund USF and would offset the dwindling fees from conventional voice phone lines.

An industry think tank begs to differ. “The right answer is the broadest possible base, which means general revenues through annual congressional appropriations,” says economist Scott Wallsten of the Washington, D.C.–based Technology Policy Institute. “That is more efficient and less regressive than anything the FCC can do on its own.”

Meanwhile, researcher Adam Thierer of another Washington, D.C.–based think tank, R Street, advocates for means testing for each household. “Like the food stamps model,” Thierer says, adding that a more consumer-oriented USF would create “a powerful incentive for households to shop with their dollars.”

It Would Define Broadband Internet Service Differently

The 1996 Telecommunications Act distinguishes “telecommunications services” from “information services.” That’s practically a meaningless distinction today. “A new Telecom Act should establish a third category: broadband internet-access services,” says engineering-policy researcher Jon M. Peha at Carnegie Mellon University, a former chief technologist at the FCC.

Peha, in other words, sees room for yet another category of telecom services. Wallsten, on the other hand, says Congress should avoid overspecifying what broadband means. “Whatever Congress writes will be obsolete before the ink dries,” he says. Instead, he adds, “The principle should be that minimum specifications follow what consumers actually want to buy, not what a particular technology can deliver.”

Wallsten’s observation reflects a wider tension in the industry. Some want the federal government to pay companies to lay down fiber in places where prevailing market forces wouldn’t take them. Others prefer emerging alternatives, such as fixed wireless access (FWA, which uses 5G or other wireless signals for home broadband) or low Earth orbit (LEO) satellite connections. The fight depends on how the government defines broadband. If broadband is just the fastest available internet connection, then for the foreseeable future USF can only fund fiber.
