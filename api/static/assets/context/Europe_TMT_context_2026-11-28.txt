The Bank of England has sounded the alarm over the impact of the investment boom in artificial intelligence on global financial stability, warning that the explosive growth in borrowing and increasingly complex financing structures in the sector are raising the risk of a crisis spreading across the wider financial system.

In its half-yearly Financial Stability Report, published on Thursday, the UK central bank said that, despite recent heavy stock market losses, valuations of technology companies remain excessively high and depend largely on their ability to deliver the profits expected from AI.

What is causing particular concern, however, is that investment is increasingly being financed through arrangements that are “complex, circular and opaque”, raising the likelihood that pressures in one part of the market could spill over into others.

Rising interest rates and mounting government debt worldwide are adding to the strain, increasing the risk of multiple financial problems emerging simultaneously.

The $4 trillion financing web

According to the report, the financial links between so-called hyperscalers and the financial system amount to approximately $4 trillion.

The figure does not relate solely to existing borrowing, but also encompasses broader financing and contractual obligations.

Bond issuance by hyperscalers alone is expected to exceed $305 billion in 2026, accounting for approximately 8% of global investment-grade corporate bond issuance.

When the rest of the AI ecosystem is included, the sector’s debt issuance now exceeds the annual borrowing of most governments, including the UK.

The trend is expected to intensify. According to estimates by JPMorgan, investment-grade bond issuance by companies linked to AI is projected to reach $450 billion in 2027, up from $368 billion this year.

Circular deals and opaque debt

At the heart of the Bank of England’s concerns is the practice of circular financing, through which companies in the sector simultaneously act as investors, suppliers and customers.

A typical example involves a technology giant financing an AI model developer, which then uses the funds to purchase computing power from the same investor.

Such transactions make it harder to assess actual demand for AI services and identify where credit risk ultimately lies.

Meanwhile, long-term infrastructure lease agreements, guarantees, future purchasing commitments and off-balance-sheet financing further complicate the assessment of risks.

The growing involvement of private credit funds is also particularly significant. The UK central bank cites Morgan Stanley estimates suggesting that around $700 billion in data centre investments between 2026 and 2028 could be financed through this market.

Banks seek to transfer risk

The moves by major US banks offer an indication of the concerns prevailing across the financial sector.

According to reports, Citi and JPMorgan are exploring the sale of loan portfolios linked to artificial intelligence, either to reduce their exposure or to free up capacity for new lending.

Some transactions are being carried out through synthetic risk transfers, which allow banks to retain loans on their balance sheets while transferring part of the risk of potential losses to third-party investors.

Ensuring the financial system remains resilient in the event of an AI valuation collapse is a top priority for the Bank of England, its Deputy Governor Catherine Braddick said, stressing the need to prevent market pressures from spreading to the banking system.

Shared concerns at the ECB and Fed – Memories of 2008

The Bank of England’s warnings add to similar concerns raised in recent weeks by the European Central Bank (ECB) and the Federal Reserve, with both pointing to the technology industry’s growing reliance on borrowing.

The ECB has highlighted that the huge capital expenditure by technology companies is beginning to exceed their capacity to finance it internally, while the Fed has recorded more than a fivefold increase in debt issuance for advanced computing infrastructure compared with 2023.

The ECB has also identified similarities with both the dotcom stock market bubble and certain features of the subprime mortgage market, which was at the heart of the 2008 global financial crisis.

The central banks’ shared concern is that a sudden reversal in expectations for AI profitability could trigger a chain reaction through credit losses, forced liquidations and a contraction in financing.

So far, however, the Bank of England has found that the spillover of recent stock market turbulence into other markets has remained limited.

[TITLE]Nvidia, Micron, Meta and AI: India's global investing obsession decoded:
[TEXT]
Indian investors are discovering the world beyond Dalal Street. But if their overseas portfolios are passports to global wealth, many seem to be travelling with the same itinerary: US stocks, artificial intelligence and a handful of mega-cap technology companies.

That is one of the striking contrasts in Vested’s How India Invests Globally 2026 report. The platform said 51% more investors transacted this year than last, while one in four new investors made an exchange-traded fund (ETF) their first global investment. Yet AI stocks accounted for 40% of assets held by investors on the platform, suggesting that global diversification is, in practice, often concentrated around a powerful investment theme.

The report, based on aggregated and anonymised Vested platform data alongside public sources, offers a snapshot of how its customers are investing overseas. It is not a census of all Indian investors, but it provides a glimpse into the habits forming as cross-border investing becomes easier.

Here are the key highlights:

51% more investors transacted on Vested in 2026 than in 2025

One in four new investors made an ETF their first global investment.

Most traded US stocks in 2026: Micron, Nvidia, SanDisk, Meta and Microsoft

AI was the largest investment theme, 2026’s trading value in AI stocks was 87% higher than in all

of 2025

Bengaluru has the most investors, but nearly three in four live outside the top 10 cities

Six Gulf countries account for 71% of NRI deposits

The AI trade has become the neighbourhood

AI is no longer just the stock investors buy to test the waters. It has become a sizeable part of their portfolios.

According to Vested, 31% of new investors made an AI stock their first investment in 2026, compared with 33% in 2025. More than half of investors with a portfolio held at least one AI stock, and AI companies represented 40% of assets held. Trading value in AI stocks rose 87% between January and October 2026 compared with all of 2025.

The irony is that investors may be diversifying geographically while remaining exposed to a similar set of market drivers. A portfolio of several US technology stocks can still depend heavily on the same AI spending cycle, chip demand and growth expectations.

"As portfolios grow and spread across more products and countries, investing through a secure and regulated ecosystem matters more, which is why we now hold three licences in GIFT City alongside our US registrations. Our belief that this decade will be when portfolios in India become multi-country and multi-currency only continues to grow stronger. Progress means more Indian families considering opportunities around the world when planning their financial future," said Viram Shah, Founder and CEO, Vested. The report’s sector data offers a reminder of how quickly the market’s favourite can change. In 2022, energy stocks rose 66% while the S&P 500 fell almost 20%. Technology led in four of the 10 years between 2016 and 2025, while energy led in three. No sector remained the top performer for more than two consecutive years.

The report also looked at who invests overseas and where they live. Investors under 35 accounted for 41%, while women represented 20% share. Investors in India’s Silicon Valley - Bengaluru - held the largest overseas portfolios on average at $17,900.

Among non-resident Indians (NRIs), the six Gulf Cooperation Council countries accounted for 71% of deposits by value. The UAE contributed 37%, Qatar 19% and the US 7%. NRI portfolios averaged $19,500, about 2.3 times the resident Indian average of $8,600. The gap was wider for deposits, which averaged $7,100 for NRIs and $1,900 for residents.

Across funded accounts, seven in ten investors deposited money more than once. Of those who returned within their first year, 76% made at least one deposit larger than their first. In 2026, repeat deposits were 2.5 times the size of first deposits. Some investors used systematic investment plans (SIPs).

The new investor is starting with a basket, not a stock

One of the more interesting changes is how investors begin.

A quarter of new Vested investors started with an ETF rather than an individual stock. The report also says 29% of investors who began with stocks later bought an ETF.

The habit of adding money also appears to strengthen after the first transaction. New investors made an average of three deposits in their first 12 months. A repeat deposit was around 2.5 times the size of the first, and 76% of repeat depositors made at least one deposit larger than their initial contribution during that year.

DIY investors want a little help — but not the steering wheel

Vested’s data suggests investors remain keen to manage their own portfolios.

About 62% preferred an entirely do-it-yourself (DIY) approach, choosing their own stocks and ETFs. Another 32% combined DIY investing with managed portfolios or global funds, while 6% used managed options exclusively.

The hybrid investor is particularly interesting. Among those who combine DIY and managed investing, 72% held a managed portfolio alongside their own stocks, while 65% held global funds alongside their self-selected investments.

Bengaluru leads, but global investing is not just a metro hobby

Bengaluru accounted for 8% of investors on the platform, around 1.5 times Mumbai’s share. Vested points to the city’s concentration of engineers, product managers and senior executives working for US-listed companies, many of whom may receive restricted stock units (RSUs) as part of their compensation.

The more surprising statistic is that the top 10 cities accounted for just 27% of investors. Nearly three in four investors lived elsewhere.

The South accounted for 37% of investors, followed by the North at 26% and the West at 23%.

The largest average portfolios were not limited to the biggest financial centres either. Vested reported an average portfolio of $17,900 in Bengaluru, $17,400 in Noida, $16,600 in Gurugram and $16,300 in Mumbai.

The rupee is making the case for global assets — one way or another

The report tracks the rupee’s movement from ₹66.33 per dollar at the end of 2015 to ₹92.97 on November 20, 2026 — a depreciation of about 40% over the period. Over 10 years, the rupee weakened at an annual rate of 3.4%; over 20 years, 3.7%; and over 50 years, 4.8%, according to the report’s calculations.

That currency movement can change the rupee value of an overseas investment, even when the underlying asset’s dollar price is unchanged.

Vested illustrates this with a ₹1 lakh investment made at the end of 2015. By November 20, 2026, the Nifty 50 price return would have taken it to ₹3.25 lakh, while the S&P 500 price return converted into rupees would have taken it to ₹4.74 lakh. The report attributes ₹1.36 lakh of the S&P 500’s rupee value to currency depreciation. The comparison excludes dividends.

Global investing is still a small slice of India’s mutual fund pie

Despite the growth in platform activity, overseas investing remains a small part of the broader Indian mutual fund market.

The report said schemes investing overseas account for less than 1% of Indian mutual fund assets. It contrasts this with the US, where around 22% of equity mutual fund and ETF assets are in funds investing mainly outside the country.

This gap indicates room for overseas investing to become a larger part of Indian financial planning, particularly for households whose future expenses may be linked to foreign currencies.

But the next phase may be less about buying another US technology stock and more about broadening the menu.

Vested’s report points to European industrial and luxury companies, Japanese manufacturers and Asian semiconductor businesses as potential sources of diversification. It also highlights UCITS funds, which can provide access to different markets and investment themes through funds domiciled outside the US.

[TITLE]Elon Musk's SpaceX share giveaway could be worth $18 billion: What will US children get?:
[TEXT]
Elon Musk plans to give every child in the United States two shares of SpaceX, in a giveaway estimated to be worth nearly $18 billion, Bloomberg reported.

The announcement, made around the Thanksgiving holiday, involves one newly issued share from SpaceX and another contributed personally by Musk. The world's richest man has also indicated that he intends to continue the initiative by gifting an additional SpaceX share to every American child each year.

According to Reuters, Musk announced the plan on Wednesday, shortly after SpaceX received approval from US regulators to deploy a constellation of up to one million satellites for data centres in space.

"Every child in America will receive two SpaceX shares, one from the company and one from me personally," Musk wrote on X.

What will US children receive? Under the proposed arrangement, each eligible child will receive two shares of SpaceX -- one contributed by the company and the other by Musk personally.

SpaceX said it would issue one new Class A common share for every currently active Trump Account, a government-backed programme designed to help Americans under 18 build wealth through investments.

Musk has pledged to match the company's contribution with one of his own shares.

However, the two contributions will not necessarily be transferred at the same time. According to Reuters, Musk said SpaceX's shares would be transferred next month, while his personal contribution would follow after his shareholding lock-up expires in June next year.

Musk also indicated that the programme would continue annually.

"I will be donating another share of SpaceX to all kids in America every year," he wrote in another post on X on Thursday.

How much is Musk's SpaceX giveaway worth? The combined contribution of two shares per child is estimated to be worth approximately $18 billion, based on SpaceX's share price of $127.44 at Wednesday's close, according to Bloomberg.

With around 72 million children in the US, according to estimates from the US Census Bureau, the scale of the proposed giveaway is substantial.

Bloomberg's estimate of $18.4 billion is based on the number of children and the prevailing share valuation.

Musk, whose net worth is estimated at around $820 billion, holds a significant stake in SpaceX. According to the Bloomberg Billionaires Index, his SpaceX holdings account for approximately $650 billion of his fortune, with additional wealth linked to Tesla, Boring Co. and Neuralink.

SpaceX president Gwynne Shotwell has also announced a separate contribution of more than two million SpaceX shares. However, the company has not disclosed how those shares will be distributed.

Why is Musk giving away SpaceX shares? The announcement comes as Musk seeks to expand SpaceX's presence in the artificial intelligence industry, particularly through plans to develop data centres in space.

The US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) approved SpaceX's proposal to deploy up to one million satellites intended to support growing demand for AI computing infrastructure.

However, the expansion has also attracted criticism. Astronomers have raised concerns about the potential impact of a massive satellite constellation on astronomical observations and the ability to study the night sky.

The American Astronomical Society expressed disappointment over the FCC's decision, warning that the proposed network could undermine years of investment in astronomical research facilities.

Reuters reported that Musk's share giveaway could also help build public support for SpaceX at a time when the technology sector faces growing scrutiny over its influence, environmental footprint and expanding role in the economy.

[TITLE]Elon Musk has approval to launch 1 million satellites. An astronomer explains how this would impact the sky:
[TEXT]
This week, on the eve the Thanksgiving holiday, the United States Federal Communications Commission approved SpaceX’s plan to launch one million “Starmind” artificial intelligence (AI) data centre satellites. This decision reflects the convergence of a satellite boom, an AI bubble and the Trump administration’s disregard for environmental regulation.

If Starmind proceeds in full, the number of operational satellites will be multiplied by a factor of 50. Space and our night skies will become vastly busier places.

SpaceX’s pitch is to take the impact of data centres away from Earth, using a million solar-powered satellites to do AI data crunching in space and beam the results to Earth.

But what is the impact of one million satellites, both in space and on Earth? Why has the Federal Communications Commission made this decision? And will a million satellites actually end up in orbit?

Ups and downs

We are currently in a satellite boom, and constellations of hundreds or thousands of satellites are a key feature.

The Starlink internet satellites (another SpaceX project) is the best known example of such a constellation. Orbital rocket launches have increased to almost one per day, while the number of operational satellites is creeping towards 20,000.

But Starmind represents something else. A million satellites.

To get so many satellites into orbit would be a vast undertaking. If the satellites have a lifetime of five years (similar to Starlink satellites), then roughly 500 satellites would need to be launched every day.

This also implies that roughly 500 satellites would eventually need to be disposed of every day. For satellites less than 2,000 kilometres above Earth, disposal usually means reentry and burning up in the atmosphere. And this has consequences.

When satellites burn up in the atmosphere, their materials react with and are deposited into the upper atmosphere. We now have satellites depositing far more aluminium, titanium, copper and lead into the atmosphere than the natural quantities from meteors.

Aluminium is of particular concern, as alumina can lead to the destruction of ozone in the upper atmosphere. This could increase ultraviolet light on Earth and the risk of skin cancers.

It’s getting busy

Increasing the number of satellites by a factor of 50 is going to change our view of the sky.

If you look up during the early evening, you will see the stars your ancestors saw. But within a minute or two you will see a satellite. A point of light silently moving from horizon to horizon.

Our view of the night skies could fundamentally change.

Each satellite would be 75 metres wide and in the early evening they will be brighter than any star. And with a million satellites in orbit, there will be hundreds of Starmind satellites above the horizon irrespective of where you are.

The orbital clutter comes with real risks.

Satellites travel at speeds of roughly seven kilometres per second, and collisions produce clouds of debris that can produce further collisions. A nightmare scenario is the Kessler Syndrome – a runaway cascade of debris producing collisions that render low Earth orbit unusable.

Of course, SpaceX is aware of the risks of orbital collisions, and it already mitigates this risk with Starlink by manoeuvring satellites away from potential collisions. However, such manoeuvres require operational satellites and it is inevitable some satellites will fail.

Those uncontrolled satellites will pose a real danger.

Regulator or promoter?

The Federal Communications Commission has been a key regulator for American satellites. This has included regulating the radio frequencies that satellites use for communications to and from Earth, the disposal of satellites at the end of their useful lives and environmental impacts.

However, under the Trump administration, the commission is taking a looser approach to regulating satellites. This includes no consideration of environmental impacts, which is reflected in the commission’s decision on Starmind. It says the commission “will not require further environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act […] for SpaceX’s Starmind system”.

There are also some statements from the commission on Starmind that are, quite frankly, bizarre. For example, it claims Starmind “promises to bring the human race closer to a Kardashev II civilization – one that can harness the full power of the sun”.

This is sci-fi silliness. A Kardashev II civilisation would need many trillions of square kilometres of technology in orbit around the Sun. Starmind is not a stepping stone to a vast “Dyson sphere” – a hypothetical megastrucutre that would surround the Sun and capture its energy.

But can it work?

One reason data centres are so unpopular on Earth is their use of precious resources, including water for cooling hot computer processors. But cooling in space is harder still. When stuck in a vacuum, one can only radiate heat away. And if you are familiar with vacuum flasks, you will know this can be a slow process.

Of course, it is possible to regulate heat in space, and satellites do it all the time. But they typically aren’t pouring out heat like data centres do.

Google recently launched an AI demonstrator satellite into orbit and tasked it with undertaking AI calculations. But it had to have downtime between calculations to dispose of excess heat.

And apart from the physics there’s the finance.

Right now we are in an AI boom, with the inflow of investment being vastly higher than revenues. And the cost of deploying AI in orbit will be vastly higher than equivalent AI on Earth.

If the AI boom is a bubble, then the burst can bring Starmind back to Earth.

[TITLE]Japan’s FSA Explores New Rules for Decentralized Finance:
[TEXT]
Japan’s Financial Services Agency (FSA) is weighing potential new regulations for decentralized finance (DeFi) and self-custodial wallet providers as part of a broader effort to address risks associated with onchain finance.

In a discussion paper dated Thursday, the regulator raised the possibility of discouraging users from accessing DeFi services that lack adequate Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorist Financing controls.

The FSA also asked whether regulated businesses that interact with DeFi, including stablecoin issuers, on- and off-ramp providers and financial institutions, should play a role in mitigating illicit finance risks when no party responsible for regulatory compliance can be identified.

The regulator questioned how to identify parties responsible for DeFi services that claim to be decentralized but retain centralized elements, and whether front-end providers that give users access to decentralized exchanges should be subject to financial regulation.

The question of who should be held responsible for DeFi activities remains unresolved in other major markets. The European Union excludes fully decentralized crypto services without intermediaries from its Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) regulation, while US regulators have largely focused on the nature of financial activities and the degree of control exercised by developers or operators. The stalled US CLARITY Act seeks to clarify which DeFi participants should be subject to financial regulation.

Regulators in Europe are clamping down on smart glasses in response to growing concerns over covert filming, harassment, and breaches of digital privacy law.

AI glasses made by the likes of Meta and Snap are surging in popularity, but built-in cameras and microphones and potentially soon facial recognition, have drawn criticism from human rights and civil liberties advocates.

Charlie Whelton, policy lead on smart glasses at UK-based human rights organisation Liberty says: “Smart glasses facilitate covert filming of people without their consent. This is often focused on women and girls, or the harassment of vulnerable people for reactions on social media ...the current lack of regulation facilitates abuse.”

On 19 November, the Dutch Data Protection Authority issued an unprecedented warning advising consumers against buying smart glasses after its own study found they endangered the privacy and safety of bystanders and users.

Threading the needle between useful wearables and bystander privacy is important because successful smart glasses could be navigational aids for blind and low-vision wearers, help train robots, or act as real-time interpreters.

Europe converges on smart glasses restrictions

Earlier in November, the UK’s Minister for Safeguarding said the government is considering new restrictions on smart glasses in certain settings and limiting sales to certain users over concerns the technology is being used to surreptitiously film women. Several UK venues, including a major pub chain, have already restricted use of the eyewear.

Italy’s data protection watchdog, Garante, has called for a range of safeguards including changes to devices to prevent the automated publishing of captured footage online.

The toughest stance came from the Norwegian government, announcing plans for a temporary ban on smart glasses and other wearables in public spaces, including parks, beaches, museums, shopping malls, and places frequented by children.

National governments are responding to campaign groups, public discomfort, and emerging EU guidance.

The European Commission’s policy advisor, the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), issued a statement in October outlining its stance on the risks of smart glasses, backed up by findings in a report The connected gaze. It said the devices raise “significant concerns” around the protection of personal data and rights to privacy, security, freedom of expression, and freedom of movement.

Under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), a household exemption allows the use of AI glasses in a purely personal capacity, for example sharing photos with family or friends.

However, public use, including posting the videos on social media or using them for official, professional, political, or other non-personal activities makes the user a “data controller”, said EDPB, giving the wearer legal responsibility for compliance with obligations including transparency toward people whose data they are capturing. In practice that means, people recording in public must alert bystanders that they are recording personal data and for what purposes.

But manufacturers’ GDPR obligations cannot be shifted to users, explains Katie Hewson, partner at legal firm Stephenson Harwood: “A device that can record continuously, process biometric data, and transmit information to third-party servers – all without the knowledge of those being recorded – is in conflict with the regulatory framework, which is why regulators are flagging concerns.”

The data protection board called for a combination of new design features on devices to warn bystanders when they are recording, beyond the standard warning light on frames. Proposals include: brighter flashing lights that stop the recording if covered, audible voice commands for activation, beeps while recording, and a Bluetooth beacon that would notify nearby smartphones when the smart glasses are recording.

Smart glasses makers adapt to tighter rules

Device OEMs say they are on board with modifying their hardware and exploring less invasive designs and form factors.

“Some manufacturers are either changing designs or bringing in new designs, aware of an increasing backlash,” says Benjamin Hatton, connected devices research analyst at FDM CCS Insight. “One manufacturer told me that in order to get a footing in Europe they will probably need to offer a product that alleviates some of the concerns around cameras.”

Apple has reportedly delayed the launch of its first smart glasses to late 2027 to address privacy concerns. Meta, which dominates smart glasses with an 84% share of global shipments, updated its second generation Ray-Ban Meta frames software over the summer to prevent covert recording with a modified built-in LED.

The software detects a blocked, or modified, light and disables the camera. If the light is removed altogether, the camera shuts down permanently. Gentle Monster AI glasses, powered by Google, run a similar system.

Camera-free models are gaining traction. Meta has introduced audio-only frames, and China-based Even Realities specializes in camera-free smart glasses. Its founder Will Wang has argued that integrated cameras should wait until companies can put stronger protections in place.

But omitting cameras “completely changes the value proposition,” adds Hatton, making the eyewear “more akin to a note taker with a personal assistant built in.”

In its statement, the EDPB proposed additional technical measures to give bystanders more meaningful control over their personal data. These include limits on continuous recording and live streaming and blurring bystanders’ faces or avoiding processing their identities. Where possible, it said, processing should happen on the device, instead of on remote cloud servers.

“It’s really important that smart glasses technologies have privacy by design and by default built in from the outset, making it the responsibility of manufacturers and not leaving the burden of lawful use to individual users,” says Akvilė Šikšniūtė, policy officer for AI at The European Consumer Organisation (BEUC), which has formally urged EU authorities to investigate smart glasses. “It could mean minimizing the collection of personal data, prioritizing on device processing, or disabling unnecessary recording and storage functionalities by default to better protect the data of both the wearer and bystanders simultaneously.”

Incorporating such features could mean difficult design trade offs. Larger frames could make them less desirable to consumers, even as they allow additional functions. Next-generation chips powerful enough to handle local processing of data normally pushed to the cloud, could also be uncomfortably hot for wearers.

Existing chips already have sufficient processing power to handle safety features such as obscuring faces, says Hatton “if manufacturers chose to implement them.”

Spy-wear or fair to wear?

But not everyone is convinced that modifications to devices or tougher transparency rules will go far enough to protect consumers.

Berlin-based human rights organization HateAid is calling for a ban on sales of Meta AI glasses in Germany. The NGO filed a criminal complaint against Meta, Ray-Ban, Oakley, and several eyewear retailers in August arguing that they violate the Telecommunications, Digital Services, and Data Protection Act.

“Under this law it is prohibited to sell espionage gadgets that are hidden in everyday objects and intended to film people without their knowledge,” says Josephine Ballon, chief executive of HateAid, “This is exactly what smart glasses are designed for.”

A Meta spokesperson responded to the complaint indirectly in a Politico article, saying the smart glasses were approved by Germany’s Federal Network Agency in 2022 and were built with privacy safeguards that exceed those of a smartphone.

Expect the debate over covert filming, bystander consent, and GDPR compliance to gather pace over the coming months as European regulators continue to review the technology and how to police it. Meantime, engineers will be racing to preserve the beneficial uses of smart glasses while ensuring they don’t harm bystanders.

SpaceX’s pitch is to take the impact of data centres away from Earth, using a million solar-powered satellites to do AI data crunching in space and beam the results to Earth.

But what is the impact of one million satellites, both in space and on Earth? Why has the Federal Communications Commission made this decision? And will a million satellites actually end up in orbit?

Ups and downs

We are currently in a satellite boom, and constellations of hundreds or thousands of satellites are a key feature.

The Starlink internet satellites (another SpaceX project) is the best known example of such a constellation. Orbital rocket launches have increased to almost one per day, while the number of operational satellites is creeping towards 20,000.

But Starmind represents something else. A million satellites.

To get so many satellites into orbit would be a vast undertaking. If the satellites have a lifetime of five years (similar to Starlink satellites), then roughly 500 satellites would need to be launched every day.

This also implies that roughly 500 satellites would eventually need to be disposed of every day. For satellites less than 2,000 kilometres above Earth, disposal usually means reentry and burning up in the atmosphere. And this has consequences.

When satellites burn up in the atmosphere, their materials react with and are deposited into the upper atmosphere. We now have satellites depositing far more aluminium, titanium, copper and lead into the atmosphere than the natural quantities from meteors.

Aluminium is of particular concern, as alumina can lead to the destruction of ozone in the upper atmosphere. This could increase ultraviolet light on Earth and the risk of skin cancers.

It’s getting busy

Increasing the number of satellites by a factor of 50 is going to change our view of the sky.

If you look up during the early evening, you will see the stars your ancestors saw. But within a minute or two you will see a satellite. A point of light silently moving from horizon to horizon.

Our view of the night skies could fundamentally change.

Each satellite would be 75 metres wide and in the early evening they will be brighter than any star. And with a million satellites in orbit, there will be hundreds of Starmind satellites above the horizon irrespective of where you are.

The orbital clutter comes with real risks.

Satellites travel at speeds of roughly seven kilometres per second, and collisions produce clouds of debris that can produce further collisions. A nightmare scenario is the Kessler Syndrome – a runaway cascade of debris producing collisions that render low Earth orbit unusable.

Of course, SpaceX is aware of the risks of orbital collisions, and it already mitigates this risk with Starlink by manoeuvring satellites away from potential collisions. However, such manoeuvres require operational satellites and it is inevitable some satellites will fail.

Those uncontrolled satellites will pose a real danger.

Regulator or promoter?

The Federal Communications Commission has been a key regulator for American satellites. This has included regulating the radio frequencies that satellites use for communications to and from Earth, the disposal of satellites at the end of their useful lives and environmental impacts.

However, under the Trump administration, the commission is taking a looser approach to regulating satellites. This includes no consideration of environmental impacts, which is reflected in the commission’s decision on Starmind. It says the commission “will not require further environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act […] for SpaceX’s Starmind system”.

There are also some statements from the commission on Starmind that are, quite frankly, bizarre. For example, it claims Starmind “promises to bring the human race closer to a Kardashev II civilization – one that can harness the full power of the sun”.

This is sci-fi silliness. A Kardashev II civilisation would need many trillions of square kilometres of technology in orbit around the Sun. Starmind is not a stepping stone to a vast “Dyson sphere” – a hypothetical megastrucutre that would surround the Sun and capture its energy.

But can it work?

One reason data centres are so unpopular on Earth is their use of precious resources, including water for cooling hot computer processors. But cooling in space is harder still. When stuck in a vacuum, one can only radiate heat away. And if you are familiar with vacuum flasks, you will know this can be a slow process.

Of course, it is possible to regulate heat in space, and satellites do it all the time. But they typically aren’t pouring out heat like data centres do.

Google recently launched an AI demonstrator satellite into orbit and tasked it with undertaking AI calculations. But it had to have downtime between calculations to dispose of excess heat.

And apart from the physics there’s the finance.

Right now we are in an AI boom, with the inflow of investment being vastly higher than revenues. And the cost of deploying AI in orbit will be vastly higher than equivalent AI on Earth.

If the AI boom is a bubble, then the burst can bring Starmind back to Earth.

[TITLE]What’s up with… SpaceX, BT, Colt:

In today’s industry news roundup: FCC gives SpaceX the green light to deploy up to 1 million datacentre satellites; BT tackles SMB cybersecurity threats with a new nationwide initiative; Colt accelerates the launch of its 800G wavelength services to meet the demands of AI workloads; and much more!

It’s always sunny in space! That’s one of the factors influencing SpaceX’s plan to build a constellation of low-earth orbit (LEO) datacentre satellites that would perform compute and storage functions in orbit – a plan that has now been given the green light by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC). The US regulator has authorised Elon Musk’s SpaceX to deploy 1 million “orbital compute modules” to process massive amounts of data. FCC chairman Brendan Carr stated: “This action opens a bold new frontier for the computing infrastructure needed to power America’s leadership and growth. Compute campuses or datacentres are essential to sustaining America’s leadership in SI [super intelligence]. By moving compute capacity beyond Earth, SpaceX and the many competitors working on their own orbital datacentres are meeting the moment. This is the kind of American ingenuity that can unite our strengths in space and advanced technology, reinforce our economic leadership and expand prosperity here at home.” SpaceX has already designed its mini satellite datacentre constellation, dubbed Starmind, which comprises hundreds of thousands of “AI satellites”, with the first iteration known as AI1. Each satellite will carry up to 250 kilowatts of compute payload (the systems are AI chip agnostic), be powered by solar energy and use liquid-cooling techniques that radiate the heat produced “into the vacuum of space”, according to SpaceX. The Starmind satellites will receive and transfer data via high-bandwidth wireless optical connections to the Starlink broadband constellation which, in turn, connect to the earth via ground stations and customer dishes. SpaceX executives noted during the company’s recent earnings call that the first Starmind satellites are due to be in orbit in 2027.

With UK government data showing that malicious cyber activity costs UK organisations an estimated £14.7bn every year, BT has unveiled Cyber Prepared UK, a nationwide programme of in-person and online sessions designed to “increase the nation’s resilience by transforming the way Britain’s small businesses respond to growing cyberattacks.” According to the UK telco, AI has helped to drive a 500%-plus increase in “malicious cyber scans – where criminals probe businesses for weaknesses in their defences” over the past year. BT is also now detecting approximately 28,000 active malicious scanners across its networks, an increase of one-third since 2025. The sessions will start early next year and, by the end of 2028, BT aims to deliver 100,000 hours of cyber resilience training, “providing big-business security expertise to the small businesses that power the economy, helping to establish the UK as a global leader in national cyber preparedness”. The programme is backed by partners such as the National Cyber Security Centre, Microsoft, Anthropic, CrowdStrike, Samsung, Google, TrustLayer, Xero, Immersive and Fortinet. Jon James, CEO at BT Business, stated: “UK businesses are on the cyber frontline every day, facing ever more challenging threats. BT protects more businesses than any other network, and is stepping up to help every British small business to be cyber prepared, with a national programme offering a combination of realistic cyber-simulations and training.”

Still with BT… Following successful trials in North London earlier this year, BT’s EE division has upgraded its mobile network infrastructure across all of the UK’s capital with Ericsson’s AI-native Scheduler for Link Adaptation technology. According to the operator, the move has “increased average download speeds by 26%” and improved the spectral efficiency of EE’s network by more than 13%, enabling it to carry more data over existing airwaves.” Ericsson’s technology “works by using advanced artificial intelligence to optimise how data is transmitted across the mobile network. By continuously adapting to changing network conditions, the technology helps improve speeds and capacity, supporting a more consistent experience for customers using data-intensive applications, such as streaming, gaming and video calling,” noted BT. Reza Rahnama, MD of mobile networks at BT, said: “By harnessing the power of AI, we’re delivering faster speeds and a smoother mobile experience for people across London, without the need for new hardware. This is another great example of how we’re using cutting-edge innovation to get more from our network, building on EE’s position as the UK’s leading mobile network and ensuring our customers continue to benefit from the very best connectivity.”

International operator Colt has brought forward the launch of new 800G wavelength services from 2027 to now to “help businesses meet the huge demand for capacity generated by AI workloads.” Spanning key routes across Europe, Asia and the US, including vital transatlantic subsea connections, the 800G services “bring ultra-high-capacity connectivity to some of the world’s most important digital corridors,” the operator noted in this announcement. Joe Scattareggia, Colt’s executive VP of infrastructure sales, noted: “AI is driving massive demand for capacity, and hyperscalers and neoscalers need to scale at incredible speed. GPU-intensive AI workloads require huge amounts of bandwidth, low latency and consistent performance, and there’s no time to wait for capacity to catch up. We’ve invested in 800G across our global infrastructure and now customers can order that capacity when they need it, helping them move fast and scale as AI demand grows.”

Industry body Connect Europe (formerly the European Telecommunications Network Operators’ Association, or ETNO) has welcomed the European Commission’s proposed Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA), unveiled earlier this year, as a “major opportunity to strengthen Europe's technological sovereignty and build a competitive cloud and AI ecosystem,” that supports the EC’s “proposed four-level sovereignty framework,” which defines cloud and AI sovereignty comprising four assurance levels. At the same time, Connect Europe has also proposed three priorities for the EC as related to CADA, namely: To turn sovereignty into “real market opportunities” for the providers of sovereign European cloud and AI solutions and so help European providers scale and compete; to support European cloud providers of all sizes, with smaller datacentres (including edge facilities run by telcos) also able to benefit from faster permitting and investment incentives; and to “match ambition with investment, affordable energy and sustainability. Europe’s cloud and AI ecosystem needs sustained funding, simpler rules, faster grid connections and affordable low-carbon energy.” Alessandro Gropelli, director general of Connect Europe, noted: “Telecom operators are ready to help build and scale European cloud. Sovereignty needs customers and investment anchored in public procurement, not new mandates for private companies.” All of these topics will be discussed in depth at the forthcoming Digital Sovereignty Forum event in London on 2 December, which will kick off with a keynote on-stage interview with Natalie Black, the group director for infrastructure and connectivity at UK regulator Ofcom.

Singtel and the Government Technology Agency of Singapore (GovTech Singapore) are to deploy the Open Digital Platform (ODP) and “explore AI-enabled smart-building applications” at the telco’s new corporate headquarters, One Comcentre, which is scheduled for completion by the end of 2028, the telco announced. The collaboration will bring together Singtel’s advanced 5G+ (5G standalone) connectivity, network intelligence and AI capabilities with ODP, “a domain-agnostic integration platform co-developed by GovTech Singapore and JTC Corporation that enables diverse systems and applications, such as robots and building systems to connect, exchange data and interoperate through common interfaces,” noted the telco. It added: “A key focus of this initiative will be enabling trusted and secure interactions between autonomous robots, AI systems and smart building infrastructure… Envisioned as a flagship AI-native intelligent building, One Comcentre will be the first building to deploy the telco-enhanced ODP, demonstrating how intelligent technologies can improve operational efficiency, security, sustainability and occupant experiences, with the potential for wider adoption across Singapore’s buildings and smart districts.”
