[TITLE]Streaming Coalition Urges Squires to Support Enforcement Against Abusive SEP Licensing Practices:
[TEXT]
“Once the standard is widely adopted, [these SEP holders] renege on that promise and demand excessive royalties on threat of an injunction.” – Coalition letter

A coalition of ten streaming, broadcasting, and audiovisual media organizations, including the Motion Picture Association (MPA) and the Streaming Innovation Alliance, sent a letter today to U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) Director John Squires, urging him to “support strong public and private sector enforcement against abusive SEP licensing practices.” The signatories said that abuse of standard essential patents (SEPs) was undercutting the open process of setting standards for the video streaming technologies their members use.

In December 2025, the USPTO established an SEP Working Group that reports directly to Director Squires. The agency said the group would continue its practice of filing court statements supporting SEP injunctions, such as the statement of interest filed in Radian Memory Systems v. Samsung Electronics in June 2025.

The remaining signatories were the Association of Commercial Television and Video on Demand Services in Europe, the Commercial On-Demand and Broadcasting Association, Strima, the Asociación Española de Video on Demand, the European Video On Demand Coalition, Streaming for Australia, Streamingdiensten Nederland and the Television Association of Programmers Latin America. If left unaddressed, the letter said SEP abuse would harm consumers and competition, significantly affect investment in local creative economies, “misappropriate millions” intended for creative content and new offerings, and lower the quality of the viewing experience.

Standardized codec technologies such as AVC and HEVC allow competing streamers to provide dependable, high-quality video on devices from many manufacturers, the signatories explained. Since inclusion in a standard enhances a patent’s market power by requiring every potential competitor to license the technology, the coalition wrote, SEPs must be licensed on fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory (FRAND) terms.

According to the coalition, a growing list of SEP holders, many of which it said invest primarily in lawyers and litigation, had abandoned that principle for video streaming standards. These SEP holders commit to FRAND licensing to convince standards bodies, such as the International Organization for Standardization and the International Telecommunications Union, to incorporate their patented technology into industry standards, and then “once the standard is widely adopted, they renege on that promise and demand excessive royalties on threat of an injunction,” the letter stated. The coalition said those injunctions result from lawsuits filed in a handful of jurisdictions that routinely grant them without prompt review of the asserted patents’ validity or before a final infringement determination.

The signatories described this conduct as a “bait-and-switch patent ‘holdup’” affecting streamers bound to a standard for years, noting that AVC and HEVC were published in 2003 and 2013, respectively. According to the letter, the practice imposes large costs by destabilizing streaming technology standards, raising consumer prices, potentially diminishing commercial offerings and chilling streaming innovation while the industry remains in lengthy litigation.

The coalition stated that “the threat isn’t theoretical,” pointing to advisory FRAND royalty guidelines recently issued by a European court, identified in the letter’s footnotes as the Munich Regional Court. “Without any supporting evidence,” the letter said, the court proposed an annual codec royalty for two leading subscription video on demand (SVOD) services at 16% of one-third of annual subscription revenues, increasing to 25% for premium services. The signatories cited a Statista figure projecting nearly $100 billion in global SVOD revenues for 2026 and said that applying only the lower rate across the industry would drain well over $5 billion from the entertainment sector.

The 7th Civil Chamber of the Munich Regional Court I published the guidelines on August 13, 2026, building on recent decisions including ASUS I, ASUS II, Renault and ZTE v. Samsung. The guidelines reflect only that chamber’s views, and other courts may reach different conclusions.

All signatories support the protection of intellectual property (IP) rights, the coalition stated, adding that “it is precisely because we take IP rights so seriously” that they object to holdup tactics. The signatories said those tactics far exceed the value of the underlying patents, raise competition concerns, and damage the reputation of IP rights. The letter also referenced an amicus curiae brief the MPA submitted to the U.S. District Court in Delaware, which it said explained the conduct in detail.

That brief was filed in Disney Enterprises, Inc. v. InterDigital, Inc., an antitrust lawsuit Disney filed in August 2025 alleging that InterDigital’s licensing practices for video compression patents violated Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act.

According to the letter, these SEP holders’ conduct is “cultivating a new generation of opponents for the IP community,” including car makers, TV manufacturers, healthcare providers and farmers. The signatories said they need to facilitate a FRAND licensing framework that respects IP rights and allows the streaming ecosystem to benefit creators, innovators, consumers, and IP rights holders. The coalition urged Director Squires to support public and private sector enforcement against abusive SEP licensing practices.

[TITLE]Google will block search access for schools that ban AI use:
[TEXT]
Google said on Tuesday that it will turn off artificial intelligence in its educational apps for K-12 student accounts unless school administrators expressly enable it.

The Alphabet-owned search giant said that after surveying school administrators and teachers, it wants educational institutions to control students' access to various AI capabilities.

Access to Google's AI features — Gemini, Gemini Notebook and Gemini in Classroom — as well as Google search will be automatically turned off for K-12 student accounts until "school administrators explicitly turn them on," Chris Phillips, Google's vice president and general manager of education, said in a blog post.

Schools that ban AI "will not have access to search, and they'll see a message from their school that indicates it's not available," Phillips told The New York Times.

While Gemini, Gemini Notebook and Search will be turned off by default, schools can choose to turn on one, two or all three services, Google explained in a post on X Tuesday.

"We believe schools should have control over AI in their classrooms and in providing administrators with controls to help them manage their approach," Phillips wrote in the post.

New policy prompts outcry

Google's new policy immediately prompted criticism that it is seeking to pressure schools to use its AI apps. In a statement posted on social media, New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani accused the tech giant of "trying to use its monopoly power to force AI into our public schools."

"No corporation should have the power to dictate how we teach our children. Our public schools exist to serve students, not to grow the profit margins of Big Tech," he added.

A Google spokesperson declined to respond to Mamdani's remarks.

Roughly 150 million students and teachers use Google Classroom, and many students use school-provided Chromebooks, according to Google. Google drew backlash in August when it activated its Gemini chatbot for many K-12 students. Until August, Google had only allowed Gemini to be turned on for students 18 or older.

In September, New York City schools said that AI would be banned until students reach high school and implemented a one-year delay on the use of generative AI for children in 2-K through eighth grade.

Lina Khan, who served as chair of the Federal Trade Commission from 2021 to 2025, said in a statement posted on X that Google is seeking to exploit its dominance in internet search.

"This highlights the danger of allowing an unregulated monopolist to control an essential service," said Khan, who now chairs the board of the New York City Economic Development Corporation, a nonprofit group that promotes economic growth and investment in New York.

"Multiple courts have found Google liable for violating antitrust laws, and this latest move shows it remains unchastened," she added. "As the public increasingly pushes back against the use of AI in schools, Google is making clear it will punish those who don't fall in line."

Josh Golin, executive director of children's advocacy group Fairplay, said Google's attempt to "strong-arm schools into using its AI products is likely to backfire." He said Google's technology in schools is not about learning but about "developing lifetime brand loyalty" for its products.

Randi Weingarten, president of the American Federation of Teachers, accused Google of putting its business interests ahead of education. "Instead of working with teachers and parents to uphold privacy and ensure their tools are safe and useful, it throws the baby out with the bathwater and bans access to the tools altogether," she said in a statement.
