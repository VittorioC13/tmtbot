[TITLE]Kalshi Adds 36 More Stock Perpetual Futures, From TSMC and ASML to Goldman Sachs:
[TEXT]
Kalshi has added 36 more single-stock perpetual futures to its U.S. exchange, and this batch reaches deep into the chip supply chain. Taiwan Semiconductor, ASML, SK hynix, Arm, KLA and Lam Research are all on the list.

In a certification letter filed with the Commodity Futures Trading Commission dated Nov. 17, KalshiEX LLC said it intends to start listing the contracts on Nov. 18, on a continuous basis. The products are perpetual security futures, meaning they never expire.

The filing also lays out how fast the product line has grown. With this batch, Kalshi’s single-stock perpetual lineup reaches 148 contracts in just over two weeks.

Three Batches in 16 Days

According to the letter, the CFTC approved Kalshi’s first 58 perpetual security futures products on Nov. 2, after the exchange submitted them for review on Sept. 18. We covered that decision when the CFTC cleared stock perps earlier this month.

ADVERTISEMENT

Kalshi then self-certified 54 more on Nov. 4. The Nov. 17 filing adds 36, using the faster self-certification route under CFTC Regulations 40.2(a) and 41.23(a). Under that route, an exchange certifies that a product complies with the law rather than waiting for agency approval.

The letter says the new contracts have the same terms as the earlier ones in every respect except the underlying stock. Kalshi’s New Product Committee determined that each stock meets the listing standards in Chapter 14 of its rulebook, which has been filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission.

That dual oversight is part of the design. Kalshi is notice-registered with the SEC as a national securities exchange for security futures, so both regulators have a hand in these products.

Which Stocks Made the List

The schedule includes 33 common stocks and three American depositary receipts. The ADRs are Arm Holdings, SK hynix and Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing.

Semiconductors and chip equipment stand out. Besides the three ADRs, the batch covers ASML, Analog Devices, KLA, Lam Research, Amphenol, Arista Networks and Dell.

Wall Street names are well represented too. Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Citigroup, Wells Fargo, BlackRock, Charles Schwab and American Express are all included.

By our count, 24 of the underlying shares have their primary listing on the NYSE and 12 on Nasdaq.

How the Contracts Work

Each contract covers 100 shares of its underlying stock, with a minimum tick of $0.005 and a tick value of $0.50. Traders do not need to buy a full contract. The minimum order is one five-thousandth of a contract, which equals 0.02 of a share.

In practice, the smallest position is worth about 2% of one share’s price. That is the same minimum unit Kalshi used for the 54 contracts it certified on Nov. 4.

Instead of an expiry date, the contracts use a once-daily funding payment to stay close to the stock price. Kalshi measures the gap between the contract and an underlying price index minute by minute during the regular U.S. stock session. If the contract trades at a premium, longs pay shorts. If it trades at a discount, shorts pay longs.

Trading runs from 6 p.m. ET on Sunday to 5 p.m. ET on Friday, with a one-hour daily maintenance break from 5 p.m. to 6 p.m. ET. That means the contracts can trade overnight on weekdays, when the stock market itself is closed. Settlement is in cash, and no shares change hands.

A Built-In Leverage Cap

The most important number in the filing may be 15.50%. That is the minimum margin Kalshi requires on each position, measured against its current market value. The exchange can set it higher.

Federal security futures rules already require at least 15% customer margin. Kalshi’s floor sits slightly above that, which caps leverage at roughly 6.45 times. Leverage is not user-selectable, according to the filing.

Perpetual futures were invented in crypto, and for years Bitcoin traders were their main users. Kalshi’s filing even cites academic work on hedging with leverage on Bitcoin perpetuals.

Now the same structure is being applied to chipmakers and Wall Street banks inside a regulated, centrally cleared market. We see that as a strong endorsement of a design that the Bitcoin market tested at scale first.

It also connects back to Bitcoin holders. As we reported, Kalshi Prime now lets institutions margin Kalshi perpetual futures with Bitcoin held at Anchorage. Every new contract on the exchange widens the menu that Bitcoin-backed collateral can support.

The race is not limited to Kalshi. The CFTC recently cleared Backpack’s blockchain exchange for U.S. perpetual futures, and rival Polymarket US has certified new oil and gas contracts.

What to Watch

The filing notes that any stock not on the schedule must be submitted to the CFTC before listing. Kalshi has filed three batches in 16 days, so more additions would not be a surprise.

The bigger test is volume. Offshore crypto-native venues already have a head start in stock perps. Kalshi is betting that tighter margin, central clearing and oversight from both the CFTC and SEC will draw traders who want the nearly round-the-clock perpetual format inside U.S. rules.

If it works, the trading tool that Bitcoin popularized will have become a standard part of the U.S. stock market.

[TITLE]FCC Backs AT&T's Plan to Remove Copper Landlines in California:
[TEXT]
The Federal Communications Commission is backing AT&T’s push to override a California regulator so that the carrier can discontinue copper landline service to consumers.

On Friday, the FCC announced that it was preempting "specific requirements in California law that prevent consumers from transitioning off of unreliable, slow copper lines,” even as some residents fear they’ll be saddled with spotty cell service as an alternative.

AT&T argues that California’s "Carrier of the Last Resort” (COLR) rules prevent it from removing the old-school copper network that currently serves only 3% of California households, a number that continues to shrink. In May, the company urged the commission to declare that federal laws preempted California's regulations, thus clearing the way for AT&T to discontinue the service as it prioritizes high-speed fiber and expanded 5G cellular coverage.

Friday’s FCC order partially grants AT&T’s petition, agreeing that under federal law it has “explicit jurisdiction over the discontinuance of interstate telecommunications services.”

Still, the order refrains from “wholly” preempting California’s Carrier of the Last Resort rules, and seems to instead focus on the AT&T situation. “While we preempt those requirements that necessitate that AT&T obtain a variety of CPUC [California Public Utilities Commission] approvals before it can discontinue POTS [plain old telephone service] in accordance with federal authorization, we do not preempt the COLR and other obligations more generally,” the 74-page document says. (Photo by Kevin Dietsch/Getty Images)

The FCC issued the order, even though many locals opposed AT&T’s proposal to discontinue the landline service. “Please do not allow AT&T to eliminate the last reliable communication tool for rural residents. The VOIP and cellular infrastructure is not robust enough to provide...equal access for their communication needs,” one concerned user wrote in public comments.

The CPUC also told the FCC “nothing in California’s COLR rules prevents AT&T from retiring its copper infrastructure," even though it argued against preemption. CPUC’s main concern has been that the company’s replacement, called AT&T Phone - Advanced, won’t perform as well indoors compared with outdoor cell coverage, and it’ll cost more for consumers.

However, the FCC disagreed and was convinced that AT&T Phone - Advanced would be offered at a lower price, including “at a discounted rate of $25 per month to existing Lifeline customers in California,” while providing sufficient signal strength. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr also said: “Importantly, this decision does not come at the expense of consumer safeguards. The FCC’s rules require providers to notify consumers of proposed service discontinuances and provide an opportunity for public comment. We also preserve important protections for service reliability and public safety, including access to 911.”

Despite the FCC’s order, it’s not entirely clear if AT&T will pursue the copper landline phase-out in California. In May, the carrier sued to overturn the local regulations and block the CPUC from enforcing them against AT&T, which noted it spends $1 billion each year to maintain the copper network. That court case remains ongoing.

For now, AT&T EVP Rhonda Johnson praised Chairman Carr for the decision. “California deserves fast, reliable, future-ready technology – and with the right policies in place, we’re delivering it and transitioning away from copper," she said. "No customer will be left without access to voice or 911 service, and we’re committed to working with our customers every step of the way during this transition.”

However, Democratic FCC Commissioner Anna Gomez opposed the order, saying: “Californians deserve to know that when they raise a concern, someone in their state with the authority to act on it is listening. I cannot support an item that moves that authority further away from them. I respectfully dissent.”

In a statement, CPUC also said the FCC's decision "without adequately considering the concerns of California is deeply concerning, particularly for customers in rural communities, high-fire-threat areas, and communities where reliable communications are essential to public safety." The regulator also hinted it'll explore legal options.

"Customers deserve confidence that their communications service they pay for will work when they need it most, CPUC said. "California will continue to ensure that confidence is borne out in practice, and will continue to defend its responsibility to protect consumers, preserve essential communications services, and promote public safety. We will carefully evaluate the FCC’s order and all available legal and regulatory options."

According to the regulator, AT&T has proposed discontinuing the legacy telephone service in the state on or after June 1, 2027. About 184,000 residential customers and 15,000 business customers will be affected.

The US government was warned four years ago that security weaknesses in national telecommunications networks had created a “perilous” situation and left the country vulnerable to foreign spies, but it fought to keep a report detailing those concerns secret and didn’t address its findings, according to interviews and documents reviewed by Bloomberg News.

A June 2022 report commissioned by the US Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency concluded that US telecommunications networks contained “major structural vulnerabilities” that had created national security risks. “The fact that US national networks can be readily compromised from anywhere in the world is not yet widely understood,” states the report.

[TEXT]

Neuralink has made science fiction a reality for the approximately 26 people using the device today in the product’s testing phase. BCIs stand to become increasingly interwoven in our everyday lives, spawning new businesses and demanding new norms to govern their use.

At stake for the companies pioneering these devices is a potentially massive market, both for the devices and the vast troves of data they’ll gather. Neuralink is one in a growing field of U.S. companies developing BCIs, each sprinting to win Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for public sale. Those include Paradromics, which is focused on restoring speech, and Precision Neuroscience, which is pursuing a brain interface for patients with paralysis.

“It is definitely a race,” says Chad Bouton, CEO of the BCI company Neuvotion, which makes a commercially available, AI-guided wearable that can help restore movement in stroke and spinal cord injury patients. “I think in the next three or four years we might see an FDA clearance.”

Neuralink, with $1.3 billion in funding, more than double the next-best-funded brain implant company, might seem a shoo-in to come out ahead, but a rival BCI company, called Synchron, is neck and neck in the FDA process. Synchron’s device—which is less invasive and doesn’t require a skull-breaking craniotomy—may well be first to market.

Thomas Oxley, founder and CEO of Synchron, revealed to Fortune that his company has implanted its Stentrode device in 15 people, adding five more since last year. In that same period, Neuralink has gone from seven patients to 26. “They’re going fast,” said Oxley, who is himself a neurosurgeon.

The race had been underway for years before AI became a household term with the debut of ChatGPT in 2022. But rapidly advancing AI stands to dramatically enhance the power of brain implants by allowing them to better decipher our thoughts and intentions.

One day, AI-enhanced BCIs could enable us to control humanoid robots or AI agents on our behalf, taking instructions directly from our thoughts, says Matt Angle, CEO of Paradromics. As the research goes “cognitively upstream from language,” he says, the implants could know our desires before we are even aware of them. If a human is hungry, for example, “then the BCI would already understand what we are hungry for, and it would deduce the next reasonable thing,” Angle said—perhaps ordering food on a delivery app. “It won’t be mainstream right away,” he explained, but “it’ll come sooner than a lot of people are expecting.”

For workers, brain implants could usher in a new wave of employee surveillance in the name of productivity. Connecting a BCI to a work computer could help us type and navigate our computers faster, while also recording biological signals that reveal whether we are focused on work, or letting our minds wander. Employers could even nudge us back to work if BCIs develop two-way communication, Farahany writes in her book. She suspects punitive brain surveillance is unlikely to be tolerated by the public—but even today we accept new forms of workplace tracking, from how many times a day we badge-in to the office to transcriptions of what we say in meetings and monitoring of which applications we open on our computer.

“What you’re thinking and feeling is your last space of true privacy.” NITA FARAHANY, AUTHOR AND DUKE UNIVERSITY PROFESSOR

Some argue that the “cyborgization” of humanity has already begun, even if the devices and algorithms that we’re joined with are external to the body. In a 2017 blog post titled “The Merge,” OpenAI CEO Sam Altman wrote, “Our phones control us and tell us what to do and when; social media feeds determine how we feel; search engines decide what we think.” By 2025, an OpenAI analysis of 1.5 million ChatGPT conversations found that roughly three-quarters concerned practical guidance, information seeking, or writing—suggesting that people are increasingly turning to AI to help decide what to do and what to say.

Altman cofounded the BCI company Merge Labs in 2025, and in early 2026, OpenAI invested in it. The company’s goal is to create a more “seamless” relationship with AI—presumably ChatGPT, or one of the models behind it. A Merge Labs spokesperson wasn’t ready to reveal its product yet in our conversation, but expects to have more to share in “a couple months.”

The portal to this new world will open when the FDA approves the first brain implant. Getting there is a long road for aspirants: Companies in the running must go through various stages, beginning with animal testing. Once they clear that hurdle, companies can perform their first human implant and begin what’s called “early feasibility studies.”

Then comes the pivotal study. Companies that reach this stage can expand to more patients, while also focusing on manufacturing. That means having the “whole manufacturing line established and frozen, [and] that’s a huge investment,” Oxley said.

It also means experimentation is largely over—device design is locked, and the final trials will assess the benefits for a specific, target user. The pivotal study concludes with FDA approval to sell the products to patients with eligible, clearly defined medical conditions, with insurance likely helping to cover the cost.

In 20 years, the BCI capabilities of today will likely seem rudimentary. But the technology is already capable of incredible things—no less than modern magic.

Neuralink’s first patient, Noland Arbaugh, who became paralyzed after a diving accident, has used the implant he had installed in January 2024 to play video games such as Civilization and is now working on a book in partnership with a writer.

His implant has enabled him to travel the world for speaking engagements and facilitated being in a relationship. Even though “being paralyzed still sucks,” he posted a few months ago, he’s “constantly blown away” by the advances of his implant. When I interviewed Arbaugh in 2025, he told me, “It has absolutely changed my outlook on life and what I’m able to accomplish and what I think I can do in the future. So it has given me a lot of hope for myself.”

Synchron patients can also manipulate a computer, and the company was the first to integrate with Apple’s newly created standardized, Bluetooth-powered BCI connection in 2025. But Synchron’s device, the Stentrode, is less powerful than Neuralink’s. That’s a downside of its being significantly less invasive, perhaps making it more palatable to patients who aren’t interested in the skull-drilling that Neuralink’s N1 requires.
