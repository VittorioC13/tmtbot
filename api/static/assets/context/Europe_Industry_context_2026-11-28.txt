[TEXT]
No-one is disputing the transformative power of AI but the commercialisation of it is a different matter

By the end of this year the seven biggest artificial intelligence (AI) companies – Amazon, Alphabet, Broadcom, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Oracle – will have debt relating to AI investment of $800 billion (€729 billion) sitting on their balance sheets.

Big, yes, but not big enough to call a bubble, and not big enough to trigger a 2008-style crash.

It is the off-balance-sheet debt – now estimated at $3 trillion – that looms like a Manhattan skyscraper over the global financial system.

The Federal Reserve’s latest financial stability report included a section entitled “Leverage in the Data Center Buildout.”

This is the first time the Fed has taken a close look at how the data-centre buildout is being financed.

It noted that while debt balances relating to AI investment have grown (that’s the $800 billion bit), “other obligations” have grown even more rapidly.

It’s these “other obligations” that are making markets jittery.

The Fed estimates that there is more than $1 trillion in leases signed but not yet commenced, plus more than $2 trillion in what it calls “take-or-pay contracts” and “residual value guarantees” at Amazon, Alphabet, Broadcom, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Oracle.

These arrangements let companies with strong credit positions support weaker-credit firms to finance projects “without having an immediate, direct effect on the balance sheets of the stronger-credit firms”.

So, for example, the big firm might agree a long-term lease for data-centre capacity with a third-party data-centre developer which the latter then uses to secure financing to build the data centre. It’s not unlike how a lot of apartment schemes in Ireland are financed.

“However, it also suggests that these firms’ economic exposure to such a downturn may be substantially larger than suggested by their on-balance-sheet liabilities,” it says.

All of which raises the question of who takes the hit if AI demand fails to live up to the hype.

When it came to commercialising the new technology, many firms couldn’t do it or had invested money that didn’t match the potential returns and the investment bubble burst.

The cloud computing, the payment systems, the real value elements of the internet came only later.

The technology at the centre of the dot.com bubble – the internet – went on untrammeled by the boom and bust surrounding it. Many of the companies that built the railways went bust despite the transformative power of rail transport.

[TITLE]Washington Is Buying Its Way Out of China's Tungsten Grip:
[TEXT]
Washington has signed tungsten deals worth up to $2.45 billion this year to build supply that does not depend on China, ahead of a Pentagon ban on Chinese-origin tungsten that takes effect on January 1. From that date, defense contractors can no longer deliver tungsten that was mined, refined, or separated in China, Russia, Iran, or North Korea, which closes the loophole that let them clear the old restrictions by finishing production elsewhere. The United States has not mined tungsten commercially since 2015. The government has signed a defense stockpile contract with a $2 billion ceiling, taken a $450 million position in a U.S. processor, and ordered all U.S. tungsten scrap sales to go to domestic buyers. China produced 79% of the 85,000 tons of tungsten mined worldwide last year, according to the U.S. Geological Survey, and controls most of the refining. In February 2025, Beijing placed tungsten under export controls in response to U.S. tariffs, and then limited legal exports to 15 licensed firms for 2026 and 2027. Chinese exports of ammonium paratungstate (APT), the industry's benchmark intermediate, fell to zero in January and February, and total tungsten exports dropped 22% in the first half of the year to 5,521 tons. APT rose more than 200% in the first four months of 2026 to above $3,000 per metric ton unit (mtu) in Rotterdam, Reuters reported. Tungsten concentrate, which started the year at $750 to $850/mtu, has held between $2,500 and $2,800/mtu since late May, according to Fastmarkets data.

China reportedly barred dual-use tungsten exports to Japanese military-linked buyers in early 2026, after Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi's remarks on Taiwan in November 2025. Shipments of tungsten powder and carbide to Japan fell to zero between February and April, according to Quest Metals. Sumitomo Electric's president said in May that procurement from China "has completely stopped," and Mitsubishi Materials tripled prices on some carbide products from June.
