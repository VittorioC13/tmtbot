[TITLE]No employees and prototypes within days: AI agents supercharge start-ups:
[TEXT]
After entrepreneurs Claudio Reiter and Leon Busch built their first two fully automated biotech labs, the Helsinki-based pair decided to go one better: they instructed an AI agent to complete another on its own.

“We thought it was a bit arrogant to assume humans know best how to build the lab that will teach AI wet-lab science [where scientists work with physical chemicals]. Why not let AI build its own? So we captured what we learned and now an agent can manage a lab build itself,” says Reiter.

The agent is co-ordinating a budget of roughly $500,000 (€455,000), handling everything from procuring instruments to instructing contractors. Other agents are running simple undergraduate-level experiments for their human masters, who plan to ultimately base the company between San Francisco and Zurich.

Reiter believes that within months “the complexity of the experiments they can do will rise until they can meaningfully contribute to scientific research and make discoveries that humans never could”.

Such arrangements are becoming more common and offer a glimpse of how AI is upending start-up culture, traditionally characterised by large teams working long hours and financed by venture capital. Today, a company can be started without either capital or employees. The innovation cycle in start-ups, always rapid, now looks faster than ever – especially for companies whose own products are AI-related. Founders can use AI agents to write code and handle almost any role, from registering a company to designing the product and making marketing videos.

“It’s never been easier to start a company,” says Ziv Reichert, a partner at London-based venture capital firm Phoenix Court.

OpenAI chief executive Sam Altman remarked in 2024 that in a “group chat with my tech CEO friends there’s this betting pool for the first year that there is a one-person billion-dollar company.”

Founders say the game-changing moment for start-ups happened late last year, when new tools such as Anthropic’s Claude Opus 4.5 and its Claude Code made coding cheaper, faster and easier than ever before.

Toby Mather, co-founder of AI start-up Rig, which helps groups integrate company data into reports, apps and AI tasks, wrote in a February newsletter titled “Opus Babies”: “Opus 4.5 appeared on November 24 [2025]. We have since completed 18 months’ of ‘old world’ development in three months.”

Helena Gagern, who cofounded a tech hub and runs a VC fund for European start-ups called The Embassy in San Francisco, was stunned when she recently caught up with an entrepreneur whose new start-up already had a drug in animal testing – but not a single employee.

“He left his company with the insight, ‘I think I can rebuild every single thing that humans are doing in my company right now with AI’,” she says. “Everything – in terms of disease to work on, the research, the trial design – was driven by AI agents. That was for me the eye-opening moment in terms of how far you can push building a company with practically zero humans working on it.”

She adds that while many start-ups in San Francisco are still raising large sums from venture capitalists, “we’ve seen companies make tens of millions within the first year of operating without raising VC money”.

Vincent Huguet, who in 2013 cofounded Malt, a European platform on which freelance AI engineers and data scientists advertise for work, says that just as computer hardware stopped being a barrier to entry when the cloud arrived 20 years ago, now software is not a barrier either. “It can be made by anyone who is a bit geeky,” he notes.

Anthropic, the artificial intelligence giant behind Claude, has just pulled off the biggest initial public offering (IPO) in history. The company raised US$81 billion by listing on the tech-heavy Nasdaq stock market, beating the previous all-time record of US$75 billion set by SpaceX earlier this year.

Initially set at $US83, Anthropic’s share price soared to US$111 after its debut, briefly nudging the company’s value past US$2 trillion, before falling again as investors took profits.

The blockbuster stock market listing comes at a nervous moment in the AI sector. Investors are increasingly questioning whether the huge sums of money being poured into the AI boom are justified, and many are reducing their exposure to technology stocks.

Adding to this shakiness has been the shock departure of chipmaker Nvidia’s chief executive, Jensen Huang, following an affair scandal.

Anthropic’s debut certainly sets a benchmark for rival OpenAI, which still plans to go public.

But we shouldn’t mistake investor enthusiasm in one company for a broader vote of confidence in the global AI industry.

What does Anthropic’s performance tell us?

One thing we can learn from this week’s result is that there is a huge amount of demand for Anthropic itself.

Strong revenue was one factor. At the end of October, Anthropic’s annualised revenue run rate (an estimate of yearly revenue based on the previous month) was US$81 billion.

But the company’s popularity likely also played a role. Claude is one of the most widely used AI tools around the globe. “Familiarity bias” is a well known phenomenon in investing.
