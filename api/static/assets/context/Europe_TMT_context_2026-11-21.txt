Anthropic hits the market. Was it enough to distract investors from a bruised AI sector and Nvidia's scandal?

Anthropic (NASDAQ: ANT) just officially pulled off the biggest initial public offering (IPO) in US history.

This comes at a pivotal time for the artificial intelligence industry.

Sentiment has been souring over the past few months, amid concerns over the magnitude of AI spending.

On the ASX, it was just a short few weeks ago that Firmus failed to list after promising to be the second-largest ASX IPO in history.

With the Democrats winning the US election after (among other things) campaigning to crack down on data centres and regulate the industry, the near future was beginning to look a little bleak.

Then came a black swan event. A governance shock for Nvidia Corp (NASDAQ: NVDA) and the AI industry.

For many investors, this one hurt the most. The poster child for AI suddenly became the problem child.

Nvidia shares have now fallen nearly 40% from their peak. The magnitude of this decline far exceeds any pullbacks investors had faced in the past.

With AI investors becoming increasingly disheartened, the Anthropic IPO gave them something to lift their spirits. Would this be the catalyst that kicks off the next leg of the rally?

Image source: Getty Images

Did Anthropic bring the AI trade back to life?

After much speculation over whether Anthropic's IPO would go ahead this week as planned, it officially listed on the Nasdaq last night.

Anthropic sold 980 million shares at US$83 per share, raising US$81.3 billion. That topped SpaceX's record US$75 billion raise earlier this year.

After the open, Anthropic shares popped 34% before closing 17% higher.

Anthropic became the tenth-largest publicly traded US technology company currently valued at $1 trillion or more.

Its record-breaking debut was a triumph for the company and its investors.

However, it failed its bigger unofficial test. The market had hoped it would reignite investor enthusiasm for the AI trade.

Rather than lifting the sector, the debut coincided with a broad sell-off that sent both the Nasdaq Composite Index (NASDAQ: .IXIC) and the S&P 500 Index (SP: .INX) down 2%.

Nvidia investors who had hoped the Anthropic IPO would boost their shares were left disappointed. Nvidia shares trailed the market, closing 3% lower at $145.

By all market expectations, the day was supposed to go differently.

What comes next?

With Anthropic now listed, investors' attention is likely to turn to the next big listing.

That is, the other kingmaker of the AI industry (and chief rival), OpenAI.

ChatGPT maker OpenAI confidentially filed pre-listing documents with the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) in June 2026.

After ruling out a 2026 debut back in September, CEO Sam Altman confirmed at Wednesday's DealBook Summit that OpenAI definitely plans to go public "at some point".

Anthropic's success is a win for OpenAI. It also incentivises it to list sooner rather than later, while investor enthusiasm remains high.

As noted by The Wall Street Journal, Anthropic's success this week puts OpenAI in the strange position of rooting for its success. Anthropic's stock price and valuation will be used to help price OpenAI's listing.

[TITLE]Kalshi Adds 36 More Stock Perpetual Futures, From TSMC and ASML to Goldman Sachs:
[TEXT]
Kalshi has added 36 more single-stock perpetual futures to its U.S. exchange, and this batch reaches deep into the chip supply chain. Taiwan Semiconductor, ASML, SK hynix, Arm, KLA and Lam Research are all on the list.

In a certification letter filed with the Commodity Futures Trading Commission dated Nov. 17, KalshiEX LLC said it intends to start listing the contracts on Nov. 18, on a continuous basis. The products are perpetual security futures, meaning they never expire.

The filing also lays out how fast the product line has grown. With this batch, Kalshi’s single-stock perpetual lineup reaches 148 contracts in just over two weeks.

Three Batches in 16 Days

According to the letter, the CFTC approved Kalshi’s first 58 perpetual security futures products on Nov. 2, after the exchange submitted them for review on Sept. 18. We covered that decision when the CFTC cleared stock perps earlier this month.

ADVERTISEMENT

Kalshi then self-certified 54 more on Nov. 4. The Nov. 17 filing adds 36, using the faster self-certification route under CFTC Regulations 40.2(a) and 41.23(a). Under that route, an exchange certifies that a product complies with the law rather than waiting for agency approval.

Single-stock perpetual security futures products approved or certified for KalshiEX by date. Running total calculated by DCForecasts. Data: KalshiEX LLC certification letter to the CFTC, Nov. 17, 2026. Retrieved Nov. 18, 2026.

The letter says the new contracts have the same terms as the earlier ones in every respect except the underlying stock. Kalshi’s New Product Committee determined that each stock meets the listing standards in Chapter 14 of its rulebook, which has been filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission.

That dual oversight is part of the design. Kalshi is notice-registered with the SEC as a national securities exchange for security futures, so both regulators have a hand in these products.

Which Stocks Made the List

The schedule includes 33 common stocks and three American depositary receipts. The ADRs are Arm Holdings, SK hynix and Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing.

Semiconductors and chip equipment stand out. Besides the three ADRs, the batch covers ASML, Analog Devices, KLA, Lam Research, Amphenol, Arista Networks and Dell.

ADVERTISEMENT

Wall Street names are well represented too. Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Citigroup, Wells Fargo, BlackRock, Charles Schwab and American Express are all included.

The rest span health care (Abbott, Danaher, Thermo Fisher, Intuitive Surgical, Gilead), industrials (GE Aerospace, GE Vernova, Deere, RTX, Union Pacific) and other sectors, including Accenture, CrowdStrike, Shopify, Linde, NextEra Energy, Philip Morris, TJX, T-Mobile US and Welltower.

By our count, 24 of the underlying shares have their primary listing on the NYSE and 12 on Nasdaq.

How the Contracts Work

Each contract covers 100 shares of its underlying stock, with a minimum tick of $0.005 and a tick value of $0.50. Traders do not need to buy a full contract. The minimum order is one five-thousandth of a contract, which equals 0.02 of a share.

In practice, the smallest position is worth about 2% of one share’s price. That is the same minimum unit Kalshi used for the 54 contracts it certified on Nov. 4.

Instead of an expiry date, the contracts use a once-daily funding payment to stay close to the stock price. Kalshi measures the gap between the contract and an underlying price index minute by minute during the regular U.S. stock session. If the contract trades at a premium, longs pay shorts. If it trades at a discount, shorts pay longs.

Trading runs from 6 p.m. ET on Sunday to 5 p.m. ET on Friday, with a one-hour daily maintenance break from 5 p.m. to 6 p.m. ET. That means the contracts can trade overnight on weekdays, when the stock market itself is closed. Settlement is in cash, and no shares change hands.

A Built-In Leverage Cap

The most important number in the filing may be 15.50%. That is the minimum margin Kalshi requires on each position, measured against its current market value. The exchange can set it higher.

Federal security futures rules already require at least 15% customer margin. Kalshi’s floor sits slightly above that, which caps leverage at roughly 6.45 times. Leverage is not user-selectable, according to the filing.

Maximum leverage implied by minimum margin (1 divided by the margin ratio), compared with the up-to-20x figure for offshore HIP-3 stock perps cited in the filing. Calculated by DCForecasts. Data: KalshiEX LLC certification letter to the CFTC, Nov. 17, 2026. Retrieved Nov. 18, 2026.

Kalshi contrasts this with venues outside U.S. rules. Its filing notes that independent builders on Hyperliquid deploy single-stock perpetuals under the HIP-3 standard, introduced in November 2025, with leverage of up to 20 times.

Citing a comment letter from the Hyperliquid Policy Center, the filing says HIP-3 markets recorded more than $480 billion in notional volume in their first ten months, with about $4 billion in open interest.

Crypto’s Product Goes Mainstream

Perpetual futures were invented in crypto, and for years Bitcoin traders were their main users. Kalshi’s filing even cites academic work on hedging with leverage on Bitcoin perpetuals.

Now the same structure is being applied to chipmakers and Wall Street banks inside a regulated, centrally cleared market. We see that as a strong endorsement of a design that the Bitcoin market tested at scale first.

It also connects back to Bitcoin holders. As we reported, Kalshi Prime now lets institutions margin Kalshi perpetual futures with Bitcoin held at Anchorage. Every new contract on the exchange widens the menu that Bitcoin-backed collateral can support.

The race is not limited to Kalshi. The CFTC recently cleared Backpack’s blockchain exchange for U.S. perpetual futures, and rival Polymarket US has certified new oil and gas contracts.

What to Watch

The filing notes that any stock not on the schedule must be submitted to the CFTC before listing. Kalshi has filed three batches in 16 days, so more additions would not be a surprise.

The bigger test is volume. Offshore crypto-native venues already have a head start in stock perps. Kalshi is betting that tighter margin, central clearing and oversight from both the CFTC and SEC will draw traders who want the nearly round-the-clock perpetual format inside U.S. rules.

If it works, the trading tool that Bitcoin popularized will have become a standard part of the U.S. stock market.

In 1919 America had the world’s biggest economy, but the international political system was still multipolar, with other great powers in both Europe and Asia. Although Woodrow Wilson tried to persuade the victors, and his own people, of the wisdom of lowering tariff barriers and creating an open global trading system, both at home and abroad he lacked the ability to compel others to accept his ideas. Once rebuffed, America drew back into isolationism and protectionism. With no nation willing and able to hold it together, in the 1930s the global economy split apart.

By 1945 the world had become bipolar, with the United States and the Soviet Union standing above all other nations—and America alone briefly accounting for as much as half of global GDP. American planners at first entertained hopes of reviving Wilson’s vision, but the onset of the Cold War soon forced them to abandon the idea. Instead of cooperating to create a single unified system, each superpower proceeded to build its own largely separate bloc. Reiter believes that within months “the complexity of the experiments they can do will rise until they can meaningfully contribute to scientific research and make discoveries that humans never could”.

Such arrangements are becoming more common and offer a glimpse of how AI is upending start-up culture, traditionally characterised by large teams working long hours and financed by venture capital. Today, a company can be started without either capital or employees. The innovation cycle in start-ups, always rapid, now looks faster than ever – especially for companies whose own products are AI-related.
