[TITLE]Global stock markets rise as investors continue to bet on AI:
[TEXT]
Asian markets make gains, following strong showing for US tech stocks.

Global stock markets have climbed as investors continue to bet on bright prospects for the tech giants driving the AI boom.

In the United States, all three major stock indexes rose on Tuesday, led by the tech-heavy Nasdaq Composite, which gained 1.18 percent.

The benchmark S&P 500 rose 0.6 percent, while the narrower Dow Jones Industrial Average edged up 0.03 percent.

Five of the “Magnificent Seven” tech stocks rose, led by Tesla, up 4.04 percent.

Amazon gained 1.61 percent, while Apple and Alphabet gained 0.93 percent and 0.9 percent, respectively.

Nvidia, the world’s most valuable public company, rose almost 1 percent after announcing a $3bn investment in Meta’s equity offering.

Meta fell 1.64 percent, while Microsoft fell 0.24 percent.

Other big movers in tech included CoreWeave, Western Digital and Advanced Micro Devices, which gained 7.94 percent, 6.95 percent and 5.45 percent, respectively.

Asia Pacific markets rally

Asian stock markets followed Wall Street’s gains on Wednesday.

South Korea’s KOSPI led the pack, with the benchmark index closing up 6.63 percent.

[TITLE]Sinking profits turn China's $3.5 trillion stock rout into a deeper trap:
[TEXT]
By Jeanny Yu

Pessimism has gripped Chinese stocks, with no end in sight for a deepening selloff as investors confront the realities of an economy that’s weak enough to erode corporate profits but not to trigger forceful new stimulus.

The world’s second-largest equities market has lost $3.5 trillion in value from a peak in May, data compiled by Bloomberg show. The rout intensified last week, pushing the onshore benchmark CSI 300 Index into a bear market and a technology sector gauge in Hong Kong toward erasing all its gains since Beijing’s sweeping growth support package two years ago.

At the heart of investor gloom is an unsettling realization that a protracted consumption slump and the race-to-the-bottom mentality among businesses is taking a heavier toll on earnings across industries from banks to electric car makers and AI model developers. Further darkening the outlook is the growing perception that Chinese leaders are in no rush to jump-start an economy that is slowing but at a tolerable speed.

“It is too early to conclude that the worst is over,” said Ulrich Urbahn, head of multi-asset strategy and research at Berenberg. “The biggest concern remains profitability: China’s strength in production and exports is not translating consistently into stronger domestic demand, pricing power and corporate earnings.”

The third-quarter reporting season got off a disappointing start, with battery giant Contemporary Amperex Technology Co. Ltd., or CATL, suffering a profit miss that was symptomatic of both weak demand and fierce competition.

The consensus 2026 earnings forecasts for the MSCI China Index members has declined by over 4% since the end of March, according to data compiled by Bloomberg. The Hang Seng Tech Index, dominated by Chinese Internet firms mired in e-commerce price wars, has seen profit projections plunge more than 13% during the period.

Yet even against the lowered expectations, the MSCI China’s constituents that have released results have missed consensus forecasts by an average of 9%, set for the second worst quarterly miss in two years, according to Bloomberg calculations.

Among them, AI infrastructure builder China Mobile Ltd.’s January-September net profit tumbled 8% on year amid cost pressures, while major materials firm Ganfeng Lithium Group Co. also missed estimates on falling prices.

“CATL is the best poster child of what’s going on: great innovative company that’s doing great things but a victim of cut-throat competition and margin squeeze,” said Jason Lemire, chief investment officer at Bold Wealth Partners. “Competition is encouraged and generally brutal in China, not shareholder friendly.”

Profit Squeeze

The earnings pressure has left companies, particularly China’s Internet behemoths that are in a tense race to fund costly AI infrastructure build-outs, increasingly out of cash. That has forced some of them to tap the equities market to raise funds, such as Alibaba Group Holding Ltd.’s $10 billion follow-on stock sale, which further diluted share value.

“I’d always weigh Japan, Korea and Taiwanese firms over China because they have better earnings,” said Stanley Tang, senior portfolio manager at Sumitomo Mitsui DS Asset Management. “If you ask me what China can do, hard to think of any - even further localization of tech may not help to boost share prices.”

Weakening investor confidence is also showing up via other measures. Turnover in China’s onshore stock market is hovering near the lowest level since July last year, while the outstanding amount of margin trades — bets funded by borrowed cash — fell to the least in a year.

To be sure, the slumping market has already prompted authorities to mobilize state funds known as the national team to prevent stock prices from spiraling out of control.

Meanwhile, the recent rout has boosted the appeal of Chinese equities to some investors. Bridgewater Associates’ China arm, for one, said it has turned “prudently optimistic” on local stocks, saying the declines in the third quarter were largely driven by liquidity while valuations aren’t high. It also argued that policy favors long-term capital formation and more money may flow into equities given low bond yields.

Still, skepticism abounds that while the national team’s rescue efforts may be a temporary fix, the market’s long-term prospect remains dim given policymakers’ reluctance to adopt big-bang stimulus measures to accelerate economic growth.

China’s third-quarter gross domestic product grew 4.5% from a year ago, nudging expansion in the first nine months back into the government’s annual goal of 4.5%-5%. That target-meeting performance has given authorities more reason to stick to a drip-feed style of growth support, without compromising fiscal discipline or further reducing historically low borrowing costs.

“Policymakers are comfortable with the nature of economic growth,” said William Bratton, head of cash equity research for Asia Pacific at BNP Paribas. “As a result, either because they’re consciously keeping it in reserve or because philosophically they don’t believe in it, there’s no urgency to use fiscal measures to promote large scale consumption.”
