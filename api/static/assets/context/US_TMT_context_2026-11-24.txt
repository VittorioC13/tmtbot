[TITLE]Startups lead Technology Development Board's second funding cohort:
[TEXT]
According to sources, new-age firms, including Perceptyne, SigTuple Technologies, CynLr, and 5C Network, are among the 11 startups shortlisted to receive funding.

The cohort comprises 13 companies in total and is yet to be formally announced. An email sent to the Department of Science and Technology, the fund’s nodal ministry, did not elicit a response at the time of going to press.

The development comes after TDB closed its call for new proposals on September 30 this year, citing administrative reasons. A source at DST credited the move to a mismatch between growing demand for funding from startups and the capital available with TDB. By April, TDB had already received 124 project proposals.

In September this year, TDB Secretary Rajesh Kumar Pathak told Business Standard that the board was continuing to evaluate applications for its second cohort but was awaiting additional capital before entering into funding agreements with selected companies. “We cannot enter into an agreement with a company if we don’t have funds, so we are waiting for further funds. But our second cohort is already in process. Once we get the funds, we will announce that. So, we have not stopped our evaluation process,” Pathak said.

Subsequently, TDB received an additional allocation of Rs 1,000 crore under the RDI fund, taking its total sanctioned allocation to Rs 3,000 crore from the initial Rs 2,000 crore. The additional capital is expected to support the second cohort.

In line with the fund’s investment thesis, the second cohort reflects a focus on strategic deeptech capabilities, with companies working across industrial robotics, artificial intelligence (AI)-led healthcare, and automation.

For instance, Hyderabad-based Perceptyne is developing intelligent robots that combine advanced hardware with adaptive software for real-world industrial automation.

Bengaluru-based SigTuple uses AI, robotics, and microfluidics to automate the analysis of biological samples for human and veterinary diagnostics.

Another Bengaluru-based startup CynLr is developing visual intelligence technology that enables industrial robotic arms to identify and handle objects in unstructured environments.

Meanwhile, 5C Network is using AI to automate radiology workflows, processing more than 20,000 scans daily across over 2,000 hospitals, while leaving radiologists in control of diagnosis.

The second cohort marks a shift towards more application-oriented technologies. By comparison, the initial beneficiaries included spacetech firms such as AgniKul Cosmos, Dhruva Space, and GalaxEye Space Solutions; electric mobility players such as Ather Energy and The ePlane Company; and drone maker ideaForge, alongside companies working in quantum computing, semiconductors, and biotechnology.

The first cohort comprised 22 firms, for which the government had approved financial support of Rs 2,192 crore.

[TITLE]Irish-founded software start-up Hoplite raises $5.5m in funding:
[TEXT]
Irish-founded software start-up Hoplite has raised $5.5 million (€5 million) to develop its platform that provides an easier way for developers to run artificial intelligence coding agents in the cloud.

The company, which was founded by former University of Limerick (UL) students Ryan Morrissey and Bence Redmond earlier this year, gives companies of all sizes the ability to use AI agents to build and test software. Agents can work around the clock in the cloud, but setting it up can be time consuming and need specific staff resources dedicated to managing it.

Hoplite helps to eliminate some of that complexity, allowing developers to connect their code and use their existing set-up to run agents as needed.

“The models are good enough. What’s missing is the infrastructure around them – the set-up, the machine,” said Morrissey, co-founder and chief executive of Hoplite. “The most interesting software in the world is increasingly built by very small teams and their agents. We now give them the same leverage large companies have. We built the version that just works.”

The funding round was backed by Y Combinator, Stripe co-founders John and Patrick Collison, Fin co-founder Des Traynor, Relational Capital, Upside Ventures and Theo Browne of T3 Chat. The money raised will be used to grow Hoplite’s engineering team and bring the product to more users. Hoplite’s agents have already processed 824 billion tokens from 18,000 users.

“AI is completely changing how software development is happening, and Ryan and Bence are at the forefront of that change,” said John Collison.

Hoplite is based in San Francisco and Dublin. Morrisey and Bence met while studying the Stripe-backed immersive software engineering course at UL, but left the course to take part in Y Combinator’s summer 2026 programme.

[TITLE]Canada looks to strengthen its computer chip supply chain:
[TEXT]
Computer chips have become strategic resources. Artificial intelligence, telecommunications, electric vehicles, defence systems, medical technology and cloud computing all depend upon semiconductor supply chains that are extraordinarily complex and geographically dispersed. Canada is now attempting to secure a larger place within this technological network. Rather than trying simply to reproduce the vast fabrication capacity developed in East Asia, the emerging Canadian model connects critical minerals, semiconductor research, specialist fabrication, photonics and advanced chip packaging.

The approach is particularly important as governments reconsider the resilience of technology supply chains following pandemic-era shortages, geopolitical tensions and rapidly rising demand for computing power.

Semiconductor manufacturing is often presented as the production of silicon wafers. The actual supply chain is significantly more complicated. Canada’s government describes three broad stages: chip design; fabrication; and assembly, testing and packaging (ATP). Manufacturing also depends upon sophisticated machinery, chemicals and exceptionally pure materials. According to Natural Resources Canada, a semiconductor fabrication process can involve as many as 1,400 individual steps and around 300 material inputs. This creates numerous points where shortages or geopolitical restrictions can interrupt supply.

Canada’s opportunity stems partly from possessing some of these upstream materials. Silicon remains fundamental to conventional semiconductors, but increasingly important compound semiconductors combine different elements to deliver particular electrical or optical properties. These include materials involving gallium, germanium, indium and antimony. Such compounds can offer advantages in areas including speed, power handling and durability. Canada’s Critical Minerals Strategy consequently identifies semiconductors as a priority value chain. This connects Canada’s long-established mining and processing industries with the considerably newer worlds of AI, quantum computing and advanced telecommunications.

Turning minerals into technology

A significant development came in July 2026 when the Canadian government launched the Canada Critical Minerals Accelerator and announced an agreement supporting expansion at Teck Resources’ Trail Operations in British Columbia. Under the arrangement, Canada Growth Fund can make an equity-like investment of up to C$400 million, as part of a potential C$850 million investment by Teck in the facility. The proposal could double Trail’s existing capacity for germanium and antimony and potentially establish new gallium production. These are not obscure metals without technological consequence. Germanium is used in fibre optics, infrared systems and semiconductor technologies, while gallium is important to high-performance semiconductors used in telecommunications, radar and advanced electronics.

[TITLE]The AI data center boom is driving billions into private infrastructure:
[TEXT]
Infrastructure is emerging as a standout investment trend heading into 2027, as spending on energy security and artificial intelligence fuels demand. Preqin data shows infrastructure fundraising rebounded sharply in the third quarter of 2026, with $62 billion raised across 21 funds, bringing this year's total to $101 billion across 65 funds. Rene Lubianski, managing partner, infrastructure at Brookfield Asset Management, said infrastructure is a "cornerstone" of the private markets giant's business during the group's third-quarter earnings call Nov. 6. The firm has raised roughly $50 billion in capital commitments across its infrastructure strategies over the past year. Earlier this month, Brookfield said the latest vintage of its flagship infrastructure strategy had received $26 billion in commitments, with $13 billion coming after the end of the quarter. "This puts us well on track to exceed our $30 billion target in early 2027," Lubianski said. "At that size, the fund would surpass the $28 billion raised for its predecessor to become the largest closed-end infrastructure fund ever raised. "If the current momentum continues, it could also become the largest closed-end private fund ever raised in any sector."

'A challenge and an opportunity'

The fundraising surge is unfolding even as investors wait longer to exit existing infrastructure holdings. Portfolio companies owned by the 50 largest infrastructure general partners had an average holding period of 7.6 years in 2025, up from 6.1 years in 2021, according to Boston Consulting Group. Exit activity improved from the prior two years, although many transactions involved continuation vehicles and open-ended infrastructure funds, according to a BCG analysis published earlier this year. Looking ahead, institutional investors appear to have room to bulk up their infrastructure allocations. Goldman Sachs' latest annual 2026 Global Alternatives Survey — which polled some 225 institutional investors, including pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, insurance investors, family offices and other wealth managers between June and August — found that 43% of respondents have a more favorable outlook on infrastructure investment compared with a year ago, with 7% who were less favorable. Nearly half of investors, 49%, with a target allocation to infrastructure said they were under-allocated, compared with just 5% who were over-allocated, according to the study, published Oct. 21. "Geopolitical tensions and their effects on inflation, interest rates, and growth prospects continue to impact valuations and investors' ability to return capital. Those constraints coincide with extraordinary demand for private capital to finance the infrastructure supporting AI expansion — creating both a challenge and an opportunity," said Jeff Fine, global co-head of alternatives capital formation, Goldman Sachs Alternatives.

The sector has evolved over the past three years, Gimber noted, with data centers, communications networks, renewables and energy storage accounting for a growing share of investment alongside traditional assets such as transport and utilities, helping support returns. Power generation has been a standout, returning almost 40% a year over that period and 60% in the past year, according to JPMAM's 2027 Investment Outlook, published on Nov. 16.
