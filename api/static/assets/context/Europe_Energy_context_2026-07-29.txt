[TEXT]
Europe is facing an imminent energy crisis with gas stocks plummeting to historical lows as a result of the war in Iran.

Energy consultancy Wood Mackenzie has warned that storage sites across the continent are just above 50 per cent full, which is a very low level for this time of year.

Previously, storage sites were at 90 per cent capacity, but now, Europe is looking at entering the winter months with only 75 per cent capacity.

Wood Mackenzie's vice president of gas research, Massimo Di Odoardo, cautioned that low storage levels and heightened competition for liquefied natural gas (LNG) would result in higher energy prices in the near future.

He said: 'Low European inventories, strong Asian demand and limited new LNG supply growth almost guarantee elevated prices through this winter and into 2027.'

LNG imports into Europe have slowed sharply since the outbreak of the Iran war.

Imports are on track to total just 6.3 million metric tons in July, the lowest since September 2024, according to Kpler.

Part of the reason is that Asia has returned to the market with force. LNG demand across the region has climbed in recent months, attracting a record four million tons of US supply in June and July.

But the renewed blockade of the strait in recent weeks amid escalating US-Iran tensions has dashed any hopes of that.

[TITLE]Canada bets on fuel cells as the hydrogen economy gathers momentum:
[TEXT]
For more than three decades, Canada has been one of the world’s leading centres for fuel cell innovation. While many clean-energy technologies have experienced cycles of enthusiasm and disappointment, Canada’s commitment to fuel-cell research and hydrogen technologies has remained remarkably consistent. Today, as governments and industries seek practical pathways to decarbonization, that long-term investment is beginning to yield commercial opportunities.

Fuel cells occupy a unique position in the clean-energy landscape. Unlike batteries, which store electricity, fuel cells generate electricity continuously through an electrochemical reaction, typically using hydrogen as a fuel source. The result is electricity with water and heat as the principal by-products, making the technology attractive for sectors where battery-electric solutions face operational limitations.

Canada believes fuel cells could play a significant role in helping achieve net-zero emissions while simultaneously creating high-value jobs, export opportunities, and technological leadership. The country’s Hydrogen Strategy projects a substantial future role for low-carbon hydrogen, supported by industry, government, and research institutions. Approximately 80 low-carbon hydrogen projects have been announced nationwide, representing more than $100 billion in potential investment.

A history of fuel cell leadership

Canada’s fuel cell sector has deep roots, particularly in British Columbia. The most prominent example is Ballard Power Systems, headquartered in Burnaby, which has spent more than four decades advancing proton exchange membrane (PEM) fuel cell technology. The company reports investing over $1.5 billion in research and development and has become one of the world’s best-known fuel-cell manufacturers.

Ballard’s technology is now deployed across multiple sectors, including buses, trucks, trains, marine vessels, stationary power generation, and backup power systems. The company has also developed partnerships across North America, Europe, and Asia, positioning Canada as an international supplier of fuel-cell technology.

The research ecosystem extends beyond industry. Simon Fraser University, for example, operates a dedicated Fuel Cell Research Laboratory that conducts work in PEM fuel cells, hydrogen storage systems, modelling, transportation applications, and advanced fuel-cell materials. Such academic programmes help sustain Canada’s pipeline of engineering and scientific talent.

Why fuel cells matter

Much attention in clean transportation has focused on batteries. However, fuel cells offer advantages in applications where long operating ranges, high energy demand, rapid refuelling, or heavy payloads are required. This is particularly relevant for Canada, with its vast geography, cold climate, extensive freight networks, and remote industrial operations.

The Canadian government sees hydrogen as complementary to electrification rather than a competitor. According to Natural Resources Canada, hydrogen could play an important role in sectors that are difficult to decarbonize through batteries alone, including heavy industry, freight transportation, and certain energy storage applications.

Research alone does not create markets. Recognizing this, Canada has increasingly shifted from supporting laboratory development toward funding commercialization and infrastructure deployment. Recent policy initiatives include hydrogen investment tax credits, strategic financing programmes, and infrastructure funding designed to accelerate hydrogen production and deployment. Federal support has also targeted manufacturing capacity, hydrogen refuelling networks, and clean-technology supply chains.

The Hydrogen Strategy for Canada, originally released in 2020 and subsequently updated through a 2024 progress report, positions Canada as a major producer, user, and exporter of low-carbon hydrogen technologies. The strategy also seeks to enhance Canada’s competitiveness in rapidly growing international hydrogen markets.

Hydrogen hubs have emerged in several regions, including Edmonton, Vancouver, and Southern Ontario. These clusters bring together producers, technology developers, transportation companies, utilities, and researchers to accelerate innovation and create local hydrogen ecosystems.

One sector attracting increasing attention is rail transportation. In late 2024, Ballard Power Systems announced an agreement with Canadian Pacific Kansas City (CPKC) to supply 98 fuel-cell engines for hydrogen-powered locomotives. The project builds on an earlier collaboration that tested hydrogen locomotives in Alberta and demonstrates growing commercial confidence in fuel-cell technology for freight transportation.

Railway applications are particularly attractive because they demand high power output, extended operating periods, and reliable performance under harsh environmental conditions. Fuel cells could provide a lower-carbon alternative to diesel without requiring the extensive charging infrastructure associated with battery-electric locomotives.

From research to manufacturing

One of the challenges facing any emerging technology is moving from successful prototypes to large-scale production. Canada’s hydrogen sector increasingly recognizes that manufacturing capability is just as important as scientific discovery. Recent investments have therefore focused on scaling production of PEM fuel cells and related technologies. Industry analysts note a shift from research and pilot projects toward commercial manufacturing facilities capable of supporting broader adoption across transportation and stationary power markets.

This transition is critical because global competition is intensifying. Europe, the United States, Japan, South Korea, and China are all investing heavily in hydrogen technologies. Canada’s competitive advantage lies in its established expertise, abundant clean-energy resources, strong research base, and early commercial experience in fuel-cell development.

Despite optimism, significant hurdles persist. Fuel-cell systems remain more expensive than conventional technologies in many applications. Hydrogen production, storage, transportation, and distribution infrastructure is still developing. Market demand can also fluctuate based on policy incentives, energy prices, and investment conditions.

Ballard itself acknowledged industry challenges in 2024, citing policy uncertainty and delayed hydrogen-project deployment despite continuing commercial progress and strong order intake. Questions also remain regarding the most effective applications for hydrogen. In some sectors, batteries may ultimately dominate. In others, fuel cells could emerge as the preferred solution.

The most likely outcome is not a single technology winner but a diverse energy ecosystem where batteries, fuel cells, renewable electricity, and low-carbon fuels each serve different needs.

What distinguishes Canada is the consistency of its investment in fuel-cell technology. Long before hydrogen became fashionable in energy-policy discussions, Canadian researchers and companies were developing core technologies that are now attracting global interest.

Today, those investments are being reinforced by national strategy, infrastructure development, and industrial partnerships. Combined with a growing global focus on decarbonization, this creates an opportunity for Canada to strengthen its position as a leader in the hydrogen economy.

[TITLE]Canada bets on fuel cells as the hydrogen economy gathers momentum:
[TEXT]
For more than three decades, Canada has been one of the world’s leading centres for fuel cell innovation. While many clean-energy technologies have experienced cycles of enthusiasm and disappointment, Canada’s commitment to fuel-cell research and hydrogen technologies has remained remarkably consistent. Today, as governments and industries seek practical pathways to decarbonization, that long-term investment is beginning to yield commercial opportunities.

Fuel cells occupy a unique position in the clean-energy landscape. Unlike batteries, which store electricity, fuel cells generate electricity continuously through an electrochemical reaction, typically using hydrogen as a fuel source. The result is electricity with water and heat as the principal by-products, making the technology attractive for sectors where battery-electric solutions face operational limitations.

Canada believes fuel cells could play a significant role in helping achieve net-zero emissions while simultaneously creating high-value jobs, export opportunities, and technological leadership. The country’s Hydrogen Strategy projects a substantial future role for low-carbon hydrogen, supported by industry, government, and research institutions. Approximately 80 low-carbon hydrogen projects have been announced nationwide, representing more than $100 billion in potential investment.

A history of fuel cell leadership

Canada’s fuel cell sector has deep roots, particularly in British Columbia. The most prominent example is Ballard Power Systems, headquartered in Burnaby, which has spent more than four decades advancing proton exchange membrane (PEM) fuel cell technology. The company reports investing over $1.5 billion in research and development and has become one of the world’s best-known fuel-cell manufacturers.

Ballard’s technology is now deployed across multiple sectors, including buses, trucks, trains, marine vessels, stationary power generation, and backup power systems. The company has also developed partnerships across North America, Europe, and Asia, positioning Canada as an international supplier of fuel-cell technology.

The research ecosystem extends beyond industry. Simon Fraser University, for example, operates a dedicated Fuel Cell Research Laboratory that conducts work in PEM fuel cells, hydrogen storage systems, modelling, transportation applications, and advanced fuel-cell materials. Such academic programmes help sustain Canada’s pipeline of engineering and scientific talent.

Why fuel cells matter

Much attention in clean transportation has focused on batteries. However, fuel cells offer advantages in applications where long operating ranges, high energy demand, rapid refuelling, or heavy payloads are required. This is particularly relevant for Canada, with its vast geography, cold climate, extensive freight networks, and remote industrial operations.

The Canadian government sees hydrogen as complementary to electrification rather than a competitor. According to Natural Resources Canada, hydrogen could play an important role in sectors that are difficult to decarbonize through batteries alone, including heavy industry, freight transportation, and certain energy storage applications.

Research alone does not create markets. Recognizing this, Canada has increasingly shifted from supporting laboratory development toward funding commercialization and infrastructure deployment. Recent policy initiatives include hydrogen investment tax credits, strategic financing programmes, and infrastructure funding designed to accelerate hydrogen production and deployment. Federal support has also targeted manufacturing capacity, hydrogen refuelling networks, and clean-technology supply chains.

The Hydrogen Strategy for Canada, originally released in 2020 and subsequently updated through a 2024 progress report, positions Canada as a major producer, user, and exporter of low-carbon hydrogen technologies. The strategy also seeks to enhance Canada’s competitiveness in rapidly growing international hydrogen markets.

Hydrogen hubs have emerged in several regions, including Edmonton, Vancouver, and Southern Ontario. These clusters bring together producers, technology developers, transportation companies, utilities, and researchers to accelerate innovation and create local hydrogen ecosystems.

One sector attracting increasing attention is rail transportation. In late 2024, Ballard Power Systems announced an agreement with Canadian Pacific Kansas City (CPKC) to supply 98 fuel-cell engines for hydrogen-powered locomotives. The project builds on an earlier collaboration that tested hydrogen locomotives in Alberta and demonstrates growing commercial confidence in fuel-cell technology for freight transportation.

Railway applications are particularly attractive because they demand high power output, extended operating periods, and reliable performance under harsh environmental conditions. Fuel cells could provide a lower-carbon alternative to diesel without requiring the extensive charging infrastructure associated with battery-electric locomotives.

From research to manufacturing

One of the challenges facing any emerging technology is moving from successful prototypes to large-scale production. Canada’s hydrogen sector increasingly recognizes that manufacturing capability is just as important as scientific discovery. Recent investments have therefore focused on scaling production of PEM fuel cells and related technologies. Industry analysts note a shift from research and pilot projects toward commercial manufacturing facilities capable of supporting broader adoption across transportation and stationary power markets.

This transition is critical because global competition is intensifying. Europe, the United States, Japan, South Korea, and China are all investing heavily in hydrogen technologies. Canada’s competitive advantage lies in its established expertise, abundant clean-energy resources, strong research base, and early commercial experience in fuel-cell development.

Despite optimism, significant hurdles persist. Fuel-cell systems remain more expensive than conventional technologies in many applications. Hydrogen production, storage, transportation, and distribution infrastructure is still developing. Market demand can also fluctuate based on policy incentives, energy prices, and investment conditions.

Ballard itself acknowledged industry challenges in 2024, citing policy uncertainty and delayed hydrogen-project deployment despite continuing commercial progress and strong order intake. Questions also remain regarding the most effective applications for hydrogen. In some sectors, batteries may ultimately dominate. In others, fuel cells could emerge as the preferred solution.

The most likely outcome is not a single technology winner but a diverse energy ecosystem where batteries, fuel cells, renewable electricity, and low-carbon fuels each serve different needs.

What distinguishes Canada is the consistency of its investment in fuel-cell technology. Long before hydrogen became fashionable in energy-policy discussions, Canadian researchers and companies were developing core technologies that are now attracting global interest.

Today, those investments are being reinforced by national strategy, infrastructure development, and industrial partnerships. Combined with a growing global focus on decarbonization, this creates an opportunity for Canada to strengthen its position as a leader in the hydrogen economy.

Germany Maintains Strong Momentum

India Emerges as a High-Growth Market

Spain Records Stable Growth
