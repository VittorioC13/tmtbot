[TITLE]US extends chip sale restrictions to more Asian countries to reduce risk of ‘diversion’ to China:
[TEXT]
The US Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) has decided to ban remote access to certain GPUs and servers running in datacenters located in Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, and Brazil.

The agency has also introduced a requirement to secure a license before selling what it describes as “advanced super intelligence (SI) chips and servers” that are sufficiently powerful to “be used for training or running of advanced SI models” into the five nations.

The interim final rule [PDF] containing the new regulations says they’re needed “to prevent certain foreign actors from leveraging advanced computing power based on US technology to threaten US national security and foreign policy interests.”

REG AD

Similar language can be found in previous US bans on sales of GPUs and other advanced tech to China, which Washington fears will use advanced tech to improve its military capabilities.

REG AD

US authorities understood that export bans wouldn’t prevent Chinese entities from renting GPUs housed elsewhere. So from 2021 to 2024, Washington introduced a series of regulations that required US-based clouds to make sure they didn’t serve Chinese customers.

No such obligation existed for cloud operators elsewhere, which created a market for resources that Chinese customers could access from afar. It is widely felt that datacenter building booms in Malaysia and Thailand, both of which are generally on good terms with China, aimed to cater to Middle Kingdom customers who wanted to rent GPUs they could not import.

Megapseed – whose former employee Alice Huang had an intimate relationship with former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang and may have been a Chinese intelligence asset – built datacenters in Malaysia and was suspected of catering largely to offshore customers.

Neil Shah, vice president of research at analyst firm Counterpoint, said the new rules shift the regulatory burden of complying with export controls from hardware manufacturers to cloud operators.

“Data center operators in hubs such as Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia will now face massive ‘Know Your Customer's Workload’ requirements,” he told The Register by email, likening it to the KYC restrictions imposed on financial services firms. “They must prove to US regulators that their compute is not being funneled back to embargoed nations, or risk losing their own access to US silicon.”

He feels the new rules represent “a severe short-term compute bottleneck for Chinese tech giants” and “a massive long-term catalyst for decoupling.”

“Washington is forcing domestic capital and engineering talent to double down on full-stack sovereign alternatives, accelerating ecosystems like Huawei's Ascend.”

Shah’s observation came days after Alibaba decided to spin out its T-Head chip design unit, which it described as “a leading provider of full-stack data center chips which enable system-level compute solutions in China.” That description suggests Alibaba doesn’t think T-Head has strong prospects of selling its wares outside China.

REG AD

It remains to be seen if the new BIS rules will slow datacenter development across Asia and Brazil. All five nations the US has regulated are all enjoying the economic impact of the AI boom. They’re also trying to manage its impact. Singapore limits datacenter construction, as befits a tiny and resource-constrained island nation. Malaysia is very much open for business but has introduced increasingly strict building codes and requirements to minimize energy and water consumption. Thailand recently introduced new regulations that new builds of large datacenters must take place in designated industrial zones. Indonesia also has strong regulations but is not currently a major datacenter hub.

Brazil requires datacenter operators to reserve at least 10 percent of capacity for local users, suggesting some operators are more interested in offshore customers.

Interestingly, the new BIS rules don’t impact infrastructure used for “commercial recommender systems, such as those used by online retailers or social media companies, as these functions do not pose the same national security threat.”

Chinese e-tailers operate at vast scale and have already attracted regulatory attention in Europe and the USA. Asian and Brazilian datacenter operators therefore probably do not see the likes of Temu and Alibaba as a lifeline, but might see a role as regional hosts for outfits like Amazon or Meta. ®

[TITLE]Nvidia scandal puts spotlight on US-China AI chip rivalry:
[TEXT]
The controversy over Nvidia's former CEO Jensen Huang and an alleged affair with a suspected spy has exposed the intensifying AI rivalry between the US and China.

China has ambitious plans to become the leader in the global race for artificial intelligence (AI). Beijing is aware that the country needs cutting-edge chips to achieve this goal.

Vice Premier Ding Xuexiang, who chairs the Communist Party's Commission on Science and Technology, has been tasked with strengthening China's capacity for technological innovation, which would enhance its strategic competitiveness against the United States.

The party is currently using AI to monitor citizens, develop advanced unmanned weapons systems, and transform economic sectors through robotics and industrial automation, among other things.

But the US has so far been leading the way in manufacturing technology and chip design. And Washington has taken a slew of measures to retain its dominance in this strategic sector.

Why is the US restricting AI chip imports to China?

As early as 2023, the then Biden administration decided to restrict the export of advanced semiconductors and chip manufacturing equipment to China, citing national security reasons.

Beijing slammed the US restrictions, calling them "unilateral and arbitrary."

US chipmakers, however, have been drawn to China's vast and rapidly growing market.

Jensen Huang has emerged as a key adviser on AI to US President Donald Trump during his second term Image: Jung Yeon-je/AFP

Jensen Huang, who resigned as CEO of the American AI chip manufacturer Nvidia last week, enjoyed something of a rock star status in China. The 63-year-old, who was born in Taiwan, is viewed in China as a successful entrepreneur.

Huang, who has emerged as a key adviser on AI to US President Donald Trump during his second term, has pushed for looser regulations and fewer restrictions on selling AI chips to China.

In December, the Trump administration granted Nvidia permission to ship its H200 chips to China, provided the US got a 25% cut of sales.

Although the H200 is not part of Nvidia's highest-performance series, it is significantly more efficient than competing Chinese products.

Each of these chips carries a hefty $27,000 (€24,000) price tag, and Nvidia estimates demand in China at 2 million units, which corresponds to sales of approximately $5.4 billion from this product alone.

Jensen Huang's resignation and the Nvidia spy scandal

But Huang resigned last week following revelations of extramarital relationships, including one involving a businesswoman suspected by the US of spying for Beijing.

[TITLE]FG pushes regional deal to prevent internet disruptions:
[TEXT]
Add us to your Preferred Sources

File: The Minister of Communications, Innovation and Digital Economy, Dr Bosun Tijani.

The Federal Government is seeking a regional agreement among West African countries to protect submarine telecommunications cables, coordinate repairs, and prevent prolonged internet disruptions that could affect businesses and digital services.

The proposed agreement would establish common procedures for rerouting internet traffic during cable failures, strengthen cross-border telecommunications infrastructure and reduce dependence on limited international connectivity routes.

The Minister of Communications, Innovation and Digital Economy, Bosun Tijani, advanced the proposal during engagements with member states of the International Telecommunication Union at its 2026 Plenipotentiary Conference in Doha, Qatar.

According to a statement issued by the ministry on Monday, Tijani “advocated for a shared regional protocol for digital infrastructure resilience, including mechanisms for rerouting network traffic and coordinating repairs when subsea cables fail.”

The ministry said the initiative formed part of Nigeria’s efforts to strengthen regional digital infrastructure and improve the reliability and affordability of internet services across West Africa.

It disclosed that Nigeria was collaborating with Chad, Burkina Faso and the Republic of Benin to improve cross-border connectivity, with plans to extend the cooperation to Cameroon.

Nigeria is also working with Togo on plans to establish a regional data park to expand access to computing capacity for researchers and businesses across West Africa.

The ministry said the initiatives would strengthen regional economic integration and reduce vulnerabilities arising from dependence on limited international telecommunications infrastructure.

It noted that submarine cables carry more than 99 per cent of international data traffic, making their protection essential to the functioning of the global digital economy.

Nigeria’s involvement in submarine cable protection gained prominence in 2024 when Tijani was appointed Co-Chair of the ITU International Advisory Body for Submarine Cable Resilience, established in partnership with the International Cable Protection Committee.

The country subsequently hosted the International Submarine Cable Resilience Summit in Abuja in February 2025, bringing together government officials, industry leaders and technical experts to address vulnerabilities affecting critical subsea infrastructure.

The advisory body’s work culminated in the adoption of recommendations in July 2026, following further international engagements, including a summit in Porto, Portugal.

The recommendations addressed cable protection, risk mitigation, infrastructure redundancy, timely deployment and repairs, and diversification of submarine cable routes.

Meanwhile, the ministry announced that Nigeria had secured re-election to the ITU Council for the 2027–2030 term.

Nigeria emerged among 13 African countries elected to the 48-member council, extending its participation in international telecommunications governance, which dates back to 1973.

The statement read, “The Federal Republic of Nigeria has secured re-election to the Council of the International Telecommunication Union for the 2027–2030 term, reaffirming the country’s growing influence in global telecommunications governance and its commitment to advancing inclusive digital development.

“The re-election was secured at the ITU Plenipotentiary Conference 2026 (PP-26), holding in Doha, Qatar, where Nigeria emerged among the 13 African countries elected to serve on the 48-member Council.

“The victory represents another important milestone in Nigeria’s longstanding engagement with the ITU and strengthens its voice in shaping international policies and decisions that will influence the future of global connectivity and digital development.”

Reacting to the development, Tijani said, “Nigeria’s re-election to the ITU Council is a strong affirmation of the confidence the international community has in our country’s leadership and contribution to the global digital agenda.”

He added, “Under the visionary leadership of President Bola Ahmed Tinubu, we are positioning Nigeria to play an increasingly influential role in shaping the policies, partnerships and innovations that will define the future of telecommunications.”

“We will use this renewed mandate to advance meaningful connectivity, strengthen Africa’s voice and ensure that emerging technologies create opportunities for our people.”

The minister was elected Vice-Chair of the ITU Council in June 2025 and subsequently chaired its 2026 session in Geneva. During his chairmanship, the council considered emerging technologies, stakeholder engagement, sustainable funding arrangements and institutional reforms.

It also endorsed a revised draft strategic plan for consideration at the Doha conference and approved the union’s operational plan for 2027. Beyond telecommunications infrastructure, the ministry highlighted Nigeria’s participation in international efforts to strengthen child online protection.

It disclosed that Stella Chubiyo Erebor served as the African regional Vice-Chair of the ITU Council Working Group on Child Online Protection, contributing to discussions on online safety policies and regulatory practices.

The ministry linked these efforts to domestic initiatives, including the Nigerian Communications Commission’s establishment of a multi-stakeholder technical working group and the development of the Nigerian Child Online Protection Policy in 2014.

Tijani also called for inclusive access to artificial intelligence, arguing that people across different countries and communities should be able to develop technologies that reflect their languages and address local challenges.

The ministry credited the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, led by Bianca Odumegwu-Ojukwu, and Nigerian diplomatic representatives in Qatar and Geneva with supporting the country’s re-election campaign.

Other participating institutions included the Nigerian Communications Commission, Nigerian Communications Satellite Limited, National Space Research and Development Agency, Defence Space Administration and National Emergency Management Agency.

The Federal Government said Nigeria would use its renewed ITU mandate to strengthen international cooperation on affordable connectivity, cybersecurity, digital skills, technology transfer and responsible innovation.
