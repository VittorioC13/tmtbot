[TITLE]AI platforms accelerating Uganda’s digital transformation:
[TEXT]
From farmers using mobile applications to monitor weather patterns and market prices to entrepreneurs generating content with artificial intelligence (AI)-powered tools and health workers using intelligent systems to improve disease diagnosis, AI is increasingly becoming part of everyday life in Uganda.

The growing adoption of platforms developed by American technology companies such as Google, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon Web Services (AWS), OpenAI, Adobe and Anthropic is accelerating the country’s digital transformation while helping build a new generation of digitally skilled workers.

The trend comes as Uganda pursues an ambitious target of creating one million AI-centred jobs and equipping three million people with AI skills by 2030.

Industry experts believe the target is attainable if the country strategically leverages global technology partnerships while investing in local innovation, infrastructure and talent development.

Nesta Paul Katende, founder and Group CEO of Otic Group, describes AI as one of the greatest economic opportunities Uganda has seen in decades.

“The targets Uganda has set for itself are ambitious, but they are achievable if we think big and work together,” Katende says.

“Artificial Intelligence is quickly becoming a basic workplace skill, much like computer literacy became twenty years ago. The second opportunity is innovation. The third opportunity is jobs. AI is creating entirely new categories of work, and I believe AI is not just a technology opportunity for Uganda. It is an economic opportunity.”

AI in everyday life

Across Uganda, AI-powered technologies are reshaping how people work, learn, communicate and access essential services. Students are using Google Search, YouTube and AI-assisted learning platforms to access educational materials that were previously difficult to obtain, while teachers are increasingly relying on digital tools to prepare lessons and create learning content more efficiently.

Businesses, meanwhile, are adopting AI to automate repetitive tasks, improve customer engagement and boost productivity. According to digital transformation expert Faizal Muhumuza, American technology companies have become deeply embedded in Uganda’s digital ecosystem.

“American technology companies have pushed Uganda forward in a big way,” Muhumuza says.

“Today, a student in Mbarara uses Google for research, a trader in Kikuubo sells through Facebook and WhatsApp, a school or bank in Wakiso uses Microsoft Teams for collaborative learning and working, and a young filmmaker in Kampala studies Netflix productions to understand global standards.”

He says these platforms have lowered barriers to information, communication and business growth.

Google’s ecosystem, for example, has expanded access to education, entrepreneurship and digital inclusion. Schools use Google Classroom to facilitate learning, small businesses improve their visibility through Google Maps, while young people acquire practical digital skills through YouTube tutorials.

“A school in Masaka can use Google Classroom to help learners and teachers study better. A small business in Ntungamo can appear on Google Maps. A youth in Gulu can learn coding from YouTube,” Muhumuza explains.

Transforming healthcare

One of AI’s most significant impacts is being felt in the healthcare sector, where intelligent technologies are improving disease diagnosis, surveillance and patient management.

U.S.-based company MinXray is deploying AI-assisted diagnostic systems that help health workers detect tuberculosis faster and more accurately, particularly in rural communities with limited access to specialist healthcare.

AI is also strengthening disease surveillance. More than 100 Ugandan scientists have been trained to use advanced AI-powered disease monitoring systems developed through international partnerships.

Healthcare experts say such innovations can improve health outcomes through earlier diagnosis, reduced treatment delays and more data-driven decision-making.

Muhumuza says AI can also help health facilities manage patient records, predict medicine shortages and improve follow-up care.

“Healthcare facilities can utilise AI to optimise patient record management, anticipate pharmaceutical shortages and enhance diagnostic accuracy,” he says.

Boosting agriculture

Agriculture, Uganda’s largest employer, is also benefiting from AI technologies. Through AI-powered applications, farmers can monitor pest infestations, receive weather forecasts, track commodity prices and make more informed planting decisions, helping them improve productivity and reduce losses.

Creating new jobs

Beyond improving productivity, AI is creating entirely new employment opportunities. Business Process Outsourcing (BPO), digital content creation, data annotation, AI training, software development, cloud computing, cybersecurity and digital marketing are among the emerging sectors expected to generate thousands of jobs.

Katende believes Uganda has the potential to become a leading destination for AI-enabled services in Africa.

“I believe BPO could become one of Uganda’s biggest job creation opportunities over the next decade,” he says.

“AI is changing the industry, and the jobs of tomorrow will combine human talent and AI capability. If Uganda invests in the right skills and partnerships, we can become a leading destination for AI-enabled services in Africa.”

The growing popularity of platforms such as YouTube, Spotify, TikTok and Facebook is also creating new income streams for content creators, musicians, influencers and digital entrepreneurs.

Young Ugandans are increasingly building businesses around digital content production, online education and marketing using AI-powered tools.

Investing in skills

Experts agree that Uganda’s ability to fully benefit from AI will largely depend on investment in skills development. Katende argues that AI education should extend beyond engineers and computer scientists.

“One lesson stands out above all others: invest in people before you need them,” he says.

“Technology is only as powerful as the people using it. AI education should not be reserved for engineers and computer scientists. The future will belong to people who know how to work alongside AI effectively.”

Muhumuza shares a similar view, arguing that technology alone cannot transform institutions.

“A laptop does not transform an office if the mindset is still manual,” he says.

He says Uganda must invest more in training, awareness, change management and technical support to maximise the benefits of digital technologies.

Building Uganda’s AI ecosystem

While both experts acknowledge the contribution of global technology companies, they emphasise that Uganda must also build its own AI ecosystem. Muhumuza cautions against becoming merely a consumer of foreign technologies.

“We should not remain a supermarket for foreign technology,” he says. “We should become a workshop where Ugandan products are built, tested, improved and exported.”

He argues that Uganda should invest in local AI models, local-language datasets, research institutions, startup financing and affordable internet connectivity. Katende echoes the call for greater collaboration among universities, startups, researchers, government agencies and technology companies.

“Africa must move from being a user of technology to becoming a creator of technology,” he says.

“Our universities, startups and researchers should build solutions grounded in African realities.”

He adds:

“My hope is that Uganda does not simply participate in the AI revolution. It helps lead it.”

AI regulation and governance

As AI adoption accelerates, Uganda is also strengthening its regulatory framework to promote responsible deployment of the technology.

The Uganda Communications Commission (UCC) has established an AI Taskforce to develop guidelines on AI ethics, data governance, algorithmic transparency and risk management. Although Uganda is yet to enact standalone AI legislation, existing consumer protection, broadcasting and data privacy laws are being applied to AI-powered services.

Meanwhile, the ministry of ICT and National Guidance is finalising a National AI Strategy that is expected to introduce risk-based regulation, ethical safeguards and human oversight, laying the foundation for a secure and trusted AI ecosystem that supports innovation, job creation and the country’s broader digital transformation.

TL;DR Samsung signs MOU with Broadcom worth more than $200 billion for AI memory, foundry and packaging through 2030

Samsung Electronics and Broadcom have signed a memorandum of understanding worth more than $200 billion spanning memory chips, foundry manufacturing and advanced packaging for artificial intelligence semiconductors. The deal, announced on Friday at an AI summit in San Francisco, runs for five years through 2030 and covers some of the most critical layers of the AI chip supply chain. It is one of the largest semiconductor partnerships ever disclosed, though as an MOU it represents a statement of intent rather than a binding contract.

The collaboration also extends to advanced packaging that integrates memory and logic more tightly, a technique increasingly central to squeezing more performance out of AI silicon.

Broadcom designs custom AI chips for hyperscalers including Google, Meta and others that want silicon tailored to their specific workloads rather than buying off-the-shelf GPUs from Nvidia. The company extended its AI chip partnership with Meta through 2029 in April, and CEO Hock Tan recently said surging AI revenue has made organic growth more attractive than acquisitions. By diversifying manufacturing to Samsung, Broadcom gains supply chain resilience beyond its existing reliance on TSMC, which controls more than 90 percent of leading-edge chip production.

[TITLE]Trust Stamp joins EU semiconductor initiative to build biometric identity into chips:
[TEXT]
The Nasdaq-listed biometric firm will develop identity-binding technology for trusted semiconductors as part of Europe's €3 billion chip sovereignty push.

Trust Stamp, the AI-powered biometric identity company trading on Nasdaq under ticker IDAI, has been selected as a direct participant in the European Union’s Important Project of Common European Interest on Advanced Semiconductor Technologies, known as IPCEI AST. The initiative, coordinated by Germany, has over €3 billion in committed funding and represents one of Europe’s most ambitious plays for semiconductor sovereignty.

Trust Stamp’s Malta subsidiary secured the spot with support from Malta Enterprise. Its job: develop biometric identity binding that links verified human identities directly to semiconductor devices.

Advertisement

What Trust Stamp is actually building

The company’s contribution to IPCEI AST centers on what it calls biometric identity binding for trusted chips. In English: creating a system where a semiconductor device can cryptographically prove it’s associated with a specific, verified human being.

This goes beyond the standard “enter your password” approach to authentication. Trust Stamp is exploring hardware-rooted security, meaning the identity verification happens at the chip level rather than in software that can be spoofed or hacked. The company is also reportedly looking into authentication methods for brain-computer interfaces, which sounds like science fiction until you remember that multiple companies are already implanting neural devices in human subjects.

Trust Stamp positions itself as a privacy-first AI biometric identity verification provider. It operates across finance and government sectors, two industries where proving someone is who they claim to be isn’t just convenient, it’s regulatory table stakes.

What investors should watch

Trust Stamp is a small-cap company competing in a space dominated by much larger players. Its inclusion in a €3 billion EU initiative signals meaningful institutional validation, but execution risk is real. Developing chip-level biometric systems is technically complex, and the timeline from research participation to revenue-generating products can stretch for years.

The funding dynamics are potentially significant. IPCEI participants typically gain access to state aid that would otherwise violate EU competition rules. That means Trust Stamp could receive direct financial support for its R&D efforts, reducing the cash burn that small tech companies typically face during long development cycles.

[TITLE]African nations adopt plan for cheaper internet, smartphones:
[TEXT]
African countries have adopted a new declaration committing to cheaper internet access, affordable smartphones and technology-neutral regulations as part of a continent-wide strategy to expand meaningful digital connectivity and accelerate economic growth.

The New Mexico Public Regulation Commission opened a rulemaking proceeding to launch a statewide broadband affordability program that could lower monthly bills for roughly 27,000 low-income households in its first year. The effort, led by the state Office of Broadband Access and Expansion (OBAE), stems from Senate Bill 152.

The Low-Income Telecommunications Assistance Program (LITAP) is scheduled to begin December 1. Eligible customers will be able to receive monthly broadband discounts of up to $30, and households on Tribal lands up to $70, according to the Commission. The OBAE said the program is intended to help residents stay connected to education, telehealth, workforce training, and public safety services.

The broadband affordability program’s funding will come from New Mexico’s State Rural Universal Service Fund, which supports telecommunications and broadband service in rural, insular, and high-cost areas of the state. The program is backed by $10 million in its first year, and the Commission may authorize up to $45 million annually in later years. State officials said more than 100,000 households could eventually benefit.

Under the proposed rules, broadband providers that hold eligible telecommunications carrier status would notify customers that the discounts are available and verify income eligibility through the Federal Communications Commission’s National Verifier database. Providers would then apply the savings directly to customer bills and seek reimbursement from the fund.

OBAE Director Jeff Lopez said the broadband affordability program “will help address affordability challenges faced by New Mexico families,” adding that qualifying households would gain a new resource to remain connected once enrollment opens. Commissioner Patrick O’Connell said the proceeding turns the access and affordability provisions of Senate Bill 152 into working policy.

The Commission set a public comment window for the proposed broadband affordability program rules, with written initial comments due September 11 and responsive comments due September 21. The OBAE said it plans to file its own recommendations ahead of the deadlines. Under the enabling legislation, the Commission must finalize the rules by January 1, 2027.

New Mexico is the first state to establish its own replacement for the discontinued federal Affordable Connectivity Program, the OBAE said. That program helped more than 180,000 New Mexico residents afford service before its funding lapsed in 2024. The broadband affordability program was introduced by Senate Majority Whip Michael Padilla and cosponsored by Representatives Pamelya Herndon and Debra Sariñana. [TITLE]AI platforms accelerating Uganda’s digital transformation:
[TEXT]
From farmers using mobile applications to monitor weather patterns and market prices to entrepreneurs generating content with artificial intelligence (AI)-powered tools and health workers using intelligent systems to improve disease diagnosis, AI is increasingly becoming part of everyday life in Uganda.

The growing adoption of platforms developed by American technology companies such as Google, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon Web Services (AWS), OpenAI, Adobe and Anthropic is accelerating the country’s digital transformation while helping build a new generation of digitally skilled workers.

The trend comes as Uganda pursues an ambitious target of creating one million AI-centred jobs and equipping three million people with AI skills by 2030.

Industry experts believe the target is attainable if the country strategically leverages global technology partnerships while investing in local innovation, infrastructure and talent development.

Nesta Paul Katende, founder and Group CEO of Otic Group, describes AI as one of the greatest economic opportunities Uganda has seen in decades.

“The targets Uganda has set for itself are ambitious, but they are achievable if we think big and work together,” Katende says.

“Artificial Intelligence is quickly becoming a basic workplace skill, much like computer literacy became twenty years ago. The second opportunity is innovation. The third opportunity is jobs. AI is creating entirely new categories of work, and I believe AI is not just a technology opportunity for Uganda. It is an economic opportunity.”

AI in everyday life

Across Uganda, AI-powered technologies are reshaping how people work, learn, communicate and access essential services. Students are using Google Search, YouTube and AI-assisted learning platforms to access educational materials that were previously difficult to obtain, while teachers are increasingly relying on digital tools to prepare lessons and create learning content more efficiently.

Businesses, meanwhile, are adopting AI to automate repetitive tasks, improve customer engagement and boost productivity. According to digital transformation expert Faizal Muhumuza, American technology companies have become deeply embedded in Uganda’s digital ecosystem.

“American technology companies have pushed Uganda forward in a big way,” Muhumuza says.

“Today, a student in Mbarara uses Google for research, a trader in Kikuubo sells through Facebook and WhatsApp, a school or bank in Wakiso uses Microsoft Teams for collaborative learning and working, and a young filmmaker in Kampala studies Netflix productions to understand global standards.”

He says these platforms have lowered barriers to information, communication and business growth.

Google’s ecosystem, for example, has expanded access to education, entrepreneurship and digital inclusion. Schools use Google Classroom to facilitate learning, small businesses improve their visibility through Google Maps, while young people acquire practical digital skills through YouTube tutorials.

“A school in Masaka can use Google Classroom to help learners and teachers study better. A small business in Ntungamo can appear on Google Maps. A youth in Gulu can learn coding from YouTube,” Muhumuza explains.

Transforming healthcare

One of AI’s most significant impacts is being felt in the healthcare sector, where intelligent technologies are improving disease diagnosis, surveillance and patient management.

U.S.-based company MinXray is deploying AI-assisted diagnostic systems that help health workers detect tuberculosis faster and more accurately, particularly in rural communities with limited access to specialist healthcare.

AI is also strengthening disease surveillance. More than 100 Ugandan scientists have been trained to use advanced AI-powered disease monitoring systems developed through international partnerships.

Healthcare experts say such innovations can improve health outcomes through earlier diagnosis, reduced treatment delays and more data-driven decision-making.

Muhumuza says AI can also help health facilities manage patient records, predict medicine shortages and improve follow-up care.

“Healthcare facilities can utilise AI to optimise patient record management, anticipate pharmaceutical shortages and enhance diagnostic accuracy,” he says.

Boosting agriculture

Agriculture, Uganda’s largest employer, is also benefiting from AI technologies. Through AI-powered applications, farmers can monitor pest infestations, receive weather forecasts, track commodity prices and make more informed planting decisions, helping them improve productivity and reduce losses.

Creating new jobs

Beyond improving productivity, AI is creating entirely new employment opportunities. Business Process Outsourcing (BPO), digital content creation, data annotation, AI training, software development, cloud computing, cybersecurity and digital marketing are among the emerging sectors expected to generate thousands of jobs.

Katende believes Uganda has the potential to become a leading destination for AI-enabled services in Africa.

“I believe BPO could become one of Uganda’s biggest job creation opportunities over the next decade,” he says.

“AI is changing the industry, and the jobs of tomorrow will combine human talent and AI capability. If Uganda invests in the right skills and partnerships, we can become a leading destination for AI-enabled services in Africa.”

The growing popularity of platforms such as YouTube, Spotify, TikTok and Facebook is also creating new income streams for content creators, musicians, influencers and digital entrepreneurs.

Young Ugandans are increasingly building businesses around digital content production, online education and marketing using AI-powered tools.

Investing in skills

Experts agree that Uganda’s ability to fully benefit from AI will largely depend on investment in skills development. Katende argues that AI education should extend beyond engineers and computer scientists.

“One lesson stands out above all others: invest in people before you need them,” he says.

“Technology is only as powerful as the people using it. AI education should not be reserved for engineers and computer scientists. The future will belong to people who know how to work alongside AI effectively.”

Muhumuza shares a similar view, arguing that technology alone cannot transform institutions.

“A laptop does not transform an office if the mindset is still manual,” he says.

He says Uganda must invest more in training, awareness, change management and technical support to maximise the benefits of digital technologies.

Building Uganda’s AI ecosystem

While both experts acknowledge the contribution of global technology companies, they emphasise that Uganda must also build its own AI ecosystem. Muhumuza cautions against becoming merely a consumer of foreign technologies.

“We should not remain a supermarket for foreign technology,” he says. “We should become a workshop where Ugandan products are built, tested, improved and exported.”

He argues that Uganda should invest in local AI models, local-language datasets, research institutions, startup financing and affordable internet connectivity. Katende echoes the call for greater collaboration among universities, startups, researchers, government agencies and technology companies.

“Africa must move from being a user of technology to becoming a creator of technology,” he says.

“Our universities, startups and researchers should build solutions grounded in African realities.”

He adds:

“My hope is that Uganda does not simply participate in the AI revolution. It helps lead it.”

AI regulation and governance

As AI adoption accelerates, Uganda is also strengthening its regulatory framework to promote responsible deployment of the technology.

The Uganda Communications Commission (UCC) has established an AI Taskforce to develop guidelines on AI ethics, data governance, algorithmic transparency and risk management. Although Uganda is yet to enact standalone AI legislation, existing consumer protection, broadcasting and data privacy laws are being applied to AI-powered services.

Meanwhile, the ministry of ICT and National Guidance is finalising a National AI Strategy that is expected to introduce risk-based regulation, ethical safeguards and human oversight, laying the foundation for a secure and trusted AI ecosystem that supports innovation, job creation and the country’s broader digital transformation.
