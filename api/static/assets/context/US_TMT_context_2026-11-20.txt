In 2012, Smith co-founded what is now STQRY (pronounced “story”), a mobile app that visitors to the likes of the Wellington Zoo, Kelly Tarlton’s Aquarium or the Waiōuru Army Museum could use to scan a QR code and interact with an exhibit for self-guided tours.

Seattle born and raised Chris Smith moved to Wellington to study at Victoria University. He ended up marrying a Kiwi, and starting two start-ups in the capital. He now divides his time between the two cities. Photo / Annaleise Shortland

It was also a vehicle for selling tickets. It was sold the following year. The ticketing element was bought by the Los Angeles-based TixTrack in 2013, while a local company bought the storytelling element and rebranded itself STQRY (it’s still going strong today).

The same year saw Smith move back to Seattle with his wife. There, he was involved in a couple of start-ups before landing roles with Amazon (principal product manager) and Meta (senior engineering manager for internal tools).

Two years ago, Smith returned to the start-up world to found Count, with product development split between Seattle and Wellington, which he still regards as his second home.

He says the US has scale, the capital and a close-knit and collaborative software development community. Developing Count across two territories with different tax systems and financial regulations meant the start-up had to create flexible and adaptable systems from the get-go, Smith said.

‘AI built in, not bolted on’

Count includes a practice management platform for accounting firms and general ledger and bookkeeping tools for its clients, putting it in competition with the likes of Xero and QuickBooks.

Wellington-based David Morrison (ex-Silverstripe and Provoke) is heading Count’s Wellington operation, while Nelson-based Matt Stevens (best known as a co-founder of Parrotdog Brewing) is director of accounting operations on a fractional contract “to make sure Count is genuinely built for New Zealand, from GST to client on-boarding, migration and local reporting requirements” and to help build a network of accounting partners.

David Morrison (left) is heading Count's Wellington operation, while Nelson-based Matt Stevens is the start-up's director of accounting.

“It’s an exciting time to be part of the next wave of modern accounting, with AI built into the core of the platform rather than bolted on.”

Among other features, Smith says Count has a clever way of handling two technologies that artificial intelligence-based software uses to talk to artificial intelligence (AI) agents: API (application programming interface) and MCP tools. The upshot is no usage-based fees, he says, a bugbear in the age of AI and interconnected systems.

US-Kiwi raise

The $11.5m seed round was co-led by New Zealand venture capital firm Global From Day 1 (GD1) and US tax and advisory firm KBF.

KBF is also an early Count customer, using the Kiwi-American firm’s software to help run its 10 offices around the US and thousands of listed, private-equity and venture capital-backed clients.

GD1 general partner Nawaz Ahmed said: “At the early stage, we invest in impressive founders who can execute at pace and attract great talent. Chris fits that mould and is building Count into an accounting challenger to be reckoned with.

“Accounting is a massive market, and slow-moving incumbents have struggled to roll out AI tools people actually want to use.

“Count tackled the hard problem by building their own general ledger from scratch, creating a system that puts accounting on autopilot.”

Will AI take my job?

Does “autopilot” equate to human accountants or practice management staff, losing their jobs?

“I think AI presents a really cool opportunity across all industries for jobs to evolve,” Smith told the Herald.

“[Previously], you would hire someone who’s really good at doing a very specific skill,” he said.

Now, with AI able to execute most technical tasks, employers could focus more on the overall qualities of a candidate.

“We just get to hire awesome people because technology is now enabling everyone to be able to do so much more and take advantage of different talents and skills that they might not have ever developed.”

[TITLE]Goldman Sachs forecasts global stock market returns for 2027:
[TEXT]
Global stock markets are expected to extend their rally in 2027 despite corporate earnings growth slowing sharply across major economies, as moderating inflation, bond yields and oil prices are likely to support equity valuations, according to Goldman Sachs Research.

The firm expects the equity bull market to continue, although investors may face greater volatility and increasingly need to focus on companies capable of sustaining earnings growth as expansion becomes more limited.

Global equities poised for positive returns in 2027

Goldman Sachs forecasts a total return of 16 per cent for the S&P 500 over the next 12 months as of November 12. Japan’s TOPIX is expected to deliver 24 per cent, while the MSCI Emerging Markets index is projected to return 21 per cent. Europe’s STOXX 600 is forecast to generate a 13 per cent return in US dollar terms over the same period.

“We expect equities to generate positive returns, albeit with higher volatility than in recent years,” Peter Oppenheimer, Chief Global Equity Strategist and Head of Macro Research in Europe, said in the report.

“We expect investors to seek companies that can sustain growth as it becomes more scarce,” he added.

Corporate earnings growth expected to slow sharply

However, earnings growth is expected to slow considerably in 2027. US corporate earnings growth is estimated to decline from 36 per cent to 11 per cent, while Europe’s growth rate could fall from around 18 per cent to 7 per cent. Japan’s is projected to slow from 24 per cent to 9 per cent, while Asia’s could decline from nearly 80 per cent to below 30 per cent.

The report attributed the expected slowdown in earnings growth partly to slower capital expenditure growth by large technology companies, which could weigh on businesses supplying AI infrastructure. Moderating energy prices are also expected to put pressure on earnings in the energy sector.

Technology stocks offer potential investment opportunities

However, the moderation in technology valuations has opened up potential investment opportunities. Following recent market declines, global technology stocks are trading near the 20th percentile of their valuation range over the past 20 years.

Goldman Sachs said this could create opportunities in US hyperscalers, power companies, Korean memory chipmakers and businesses involved in optical networking and physical AI.

Defensive sectors may benefit from lower bond yields

Beyond technology, the firm also sees opportunities in defensive sectors such as utilities, telecommunications and healthcare, particularly among companies that could benefit from lower bond yields.

It cautioned that risks to its economic growth outlook were tilted towards the downside, making companies with stable earnings and strong balance sheets important.

Lower energy prices could particularly benefit Europe because of its greater dependence on imported energy. Goldman Sachs expects the US Federal Reserve and European Central Bank to move towards interest rate cuts in late 2027, potentially supporting lower bond yields and equities.
