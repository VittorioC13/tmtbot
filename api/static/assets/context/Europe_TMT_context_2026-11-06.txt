[TITLE]Robinhood CEO Vlad Tenev predicts all code will be formally verified within two years:
[TEXT]
The Robinhood chief, who also chairs AI startup Harmonic, says mathematical proof of software correctness is coming fast, starting with safety-critical systems

Vlad Tenev has a bold forecast for the software industry. The Robinhood co-founder and CEO says all code will be formally verified within two years.

That is a big claim in an industry that still ships patches to fix last week’s patches. Most software today is tested and then trusted. Very little of it is mathematically proven to work.

What Tenev actually said

Tenev made the prediction in a post on X on November 4, 2026. He framed the shift as a phased rollout, with safety-critical components going first.

It was not a one-off thought. In an October 27, 2026 post, Tenev had already argued that all software and hardware will eventually be verified.

The timing of the latest post matters. It landed alongside an announcement from Harmonic, the AI startup where Tenev serves as Executive Chairman.

Harmonic said its Aristotle system can now verify C code and Verilog hardware designs. Tenev referenced that capability in his post.

So when Tenev talks about formal verification, he is not commenting from the sidelines. He chairs a company building the tools to do it.

The prediction also is not tied exclusively to Robinhood’s own codebase. It reads more like a call on where the entire software world is heading.

Formal verification, explained without a math degree

Think about how most code gets checked today. Engineers write tests, run them, and see what breaks.

Formal verification is closer to a structural engineer proving, with equations, that the building cannot collapse under specified loads. It does not sample behavior. It proves properties hold in every case covered by the specification.

Technically, the process checks code against a precise written specification of what it is supposed to do. If the proof goes through, the code matches the spec. If it fails, something is wrong, either in the code or in the spec.

The catch has always been cost. Writing those proofs by hand is slow, specialized work, which is why it has historically been reserved for high-stakes systems.

That is where AI enters the picture. Tools like Aristotle aim to automate the proof-writing step, turning a months-long expert task into something software can generate.

C is one of the most widely used programming languages for low-level systems. Verilog is used to describe hardware designs. Covering both means Harmonic is pitching verification for the code that runs on chips and the chips themselves.

Why the timing makes sense

AI is now producing code at volumes that outpace what humans can realistically review.

Harmonic itself has drawn substantial investor interest, with funding rounds reported to total hundreds of millions.

Background: a CEO wearing two hats

Tenev co-founded Robinhood in 2013 and still runs it. The brokerage made its name by pulling retail investors into markets through a slick, commission-free app.

His Harmonic role adds a second track. As Executive Chairman of an AI startup focused on mathematical reasoning, he has a front-row seat to how quickly automated proof tools are improving.

That dual position explains the confidence. It also explains why some readers will treat the forecast with a measure of caution: the person predicting a verification boom chairs a company selling verification.

What this means for fintech and beyond

For Robinhood, the implications are fairly direct. A trading platform runs on software that handles orders, balances, and money movement. Bugs in that stack are not cosmetic.

Still, there are real limits worth watching. Formal verification proves code matches a specification. If the specification itself is flawed, the proof will happily confirm the flaw.

Then there is the timeline. Two years is short for any industry-wide shift, let alone one that touches every line of code ever written. Tenev’s own phrasing, starting with safety-critical components, hints at a gradual rollout rather than a switch flipped overnight.

The signals to track are practical ones. Watch whether Harmonic’s Aristotle gets adopted beyond demos, whether Robinhood discloses verified components in its own stack, and whether competitors start making similar commitments.

[TITLE]SEC Crypto Guidance Update: What Makes a Network Truly Decentralized?:
[TEXT]
SEC crypto guidance just got sharper, and the latest update tackles one of the biggest questions in digital asset law: when is a network truly free of central control?

On November 2, 2026, the SEC’s Division of Corporation Finance added three new answers to its crypto asset FAQs.

The new questions, numbered 1.4 to 1.6, explain how staff view “central parties,” broad-based governance and the link between functionality and decentralization.

We believe these answers strengthen the case for open, decentralized networks like Bitcoin. Here is why.

ADVERTISEMENT

What Are the SEC Crypto FAQs?

The FAQs were first issued on September 25, 2026 and revised on September 28, before the latest update on November 2.

They explain how staff apply the Interpretive Release the Commission issued on March 17, 2026, which set out how the agency classifies crypto assets.

The SEC stresses that the FAQs represent staff views only. They are “not a rule, regulation or statement” of the Commission and have “no legal force or effect.”

Even so, staff guidance like this gives builders and investors a practical roadmap for how the rules are likely to be applied.

What Is a “Central Party” Under SEC Crypto Guidance?

Under the Interpretive Release, digital commodities are not themselves securities. A functional crypto system linked to a digital commodity does not have a “central party” that oversees participation or distributes rewards.

ADVERTISEMENT

The release defines a central party as a person, entity or group having “operational, economic, or voting control” of a crypto system.

The new Question 1.4 asks whether participants in a broad-based governance system count as such a group.

Staff say that is a facts and circumstances test. But participants generally will not be treated as a group if they are not affiliated and have no agreement to act in concert outside the governance system itself.

The same answer applies even when governance runs through a foundation or association, if participation is based on holding the network’s native asset.

What Does It Mean to “Oversee Participation”?

Question 1.5 gives concrete examples. Staff say a central party oversees participation when it has unilateral authority to:

Grant or limit access to the crypto system

Determine or change voting processes for software upgrades, protocol changes or token distributions

Control who can participate in consensus to validate transactions

Modify the system’s rules-based code, reverse transactions or revoke access

Staff add that no central party generally oversees participation where every material aspect of operation runs automatically through rules-based code without anyone’s discretion.

The same is true where discretion is exercised solely through a broad-based governance system whose participants are not themselves a central party.

How Does the SEC Define Broad-Based Governance?

In a footnote, staff describe a broad-based governance system as transparent and rules-based. It must let a “wide and diverse group” of stakeholders independently contribute and reach consensus.

The stakeholder examples listed include developers, node operators, validators or miners, and holders of the native asset.

“Wide and diverse” means decision-making authority is not concentrated with a single stakeholder or a coordinated group.

We believe that description closely matches how Bitcoin has worked for years. Changes require broad agreement among independent developers, node operators and miners, and no single party can rewrite the rules or reverse transactions.

Why Does Functional vs. Decentralized Matter?

Question 1.6 clarifies that “functional” and “decentralized” are distinct concepts.

A system is functional if its native asset can be used in line with its programmatic utility. It is decentralized if it operates autonomously with no person or group holding operational, economic or voting control.

Staff conclude that a functional crypto system tied to a digital commodity “has characteristics of decentralization,” although the degree can vary.

That nuance matters. It recognises that decentralization exists on a spectrum, rather than being an all-or-nothing test.

What Does This Mean for Bitcoin and the Wider Market?

The FAQs also connect to the Commission’s proposed Regulation Crypto Assets, Release No. 33-11434, dated August 18, 2026.

Citing that proposal, staff note the Commission’s view that once a system is functional, work to secure, maintain or improve it would not involve “essential managerial efforts” under the Howey test.

We read that as an important signal for open-source projects. Ongoing development and maintenance of a functional network should not, by themselves, be treated as the kind of managerial efforts that create an investment contract.

For Bitcoin, we see this update as further confirmation of a regulatory direction that rewards genuine decentralization.

Clear criteria help exchanges, custodians and fund managers judge which assets fit the digital commodity category with more confidence.

The guidance also builds on wider progress. The SEC has proposed changes that could let registered advisers hold Bitcoin directly, and staff recently classified Canton Coin as a digital commodity in a digital commodity no-action letter.

Meanwhile, Congress continues to debate a federal crypto market structure bill that could put similar concepts into law.

We believe each step toward clear, principle-based rules lowers the barriers for traditional capital to move into Bitcoin.

The coming days will reveal how quickly the industry puts this guidance to work, and how much more institutional confidence it could unlock for Bitcoin.

The company also produces silicon carbide chips and custom circuits for power systems.

Its manufacturing footprint spans around 40 chip sites worldwide. Bosch has invested approximately €3bn in its wafer plants in Dresden and Reutlingen, and is spending around $2bn to convert its Roseville, California facility to silicon carbide production.

The company also plans to expand its test centre in Penang, Malaysia, through the mid-2030s. It holds a 10% stake in ESMC, the TSMC-led semiconductor joint venture building a factory in Dresden.

The move comes as Europe’s automotive industry continues to grapple with chip supply risks.

Carmakers spent part of the autumn managing shortages during the Nexperia dispute, which exposed the industry’s dependence on a limited number of suppliers.

By offering manufacturing capacity to outside customers, Bosch could help diversify Europe’s chip supply while seeking new markets for its own technology.

Bosch deputy chairman Markus Heyn said the company’s sensors are “the key” to its products and “the technological basis” for its customers’ innovations.

The surge, boosted by having more working ​days than a year earlier due to the timing difference in the Chuseok holidays, exceeded September's 83.7% gain and was the strongest since January 1974.

Riding the global boom in AI investment, South Korea's economy grew faster than expected in the third quarter on ​strong semiconductor exports and investments, with chipmakers Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix posting surging profits.

"With exports continuing ‌solid growth of more than $110 billion in October, it is becoming certain that ​annual exports of $1 trillion will be reached within November," said Kim Jung-kwan, the minister of trade, industry and resources.

South Korea's exports for the January-October period totalled $930 billion. Annual exports of more than $1 trillion will ‌make South Korea the fourth ​country in the world to reach the ‌milestone, after the US, China and Germany.

Overseas sales of semiconductors jumped 259.4% ‌in October to $56.44 billion, as prices and volume remained high on continued facility investment by global ​big tech firms in the competition for AI industry leadership, the trade ministry said.

Computer sales spiked 496.6% on increasing server demand for ​AI services.

Shipments to China rose 133.6%, while those to the US grew by 185.8%, to Europe by 56.4% and to Southeast Asia by ‌128.0%.

South Korea's imports rose 36.0% to $72.78 billion, after rising 26.0% in September, to post the ‌biggest annual increase since January 2022 on higher crude oil prices, as well as demand for semiconductor manufacturing equipment and automobiles.

The nation posted a monthly trade surplus of $42.50 billion, narrower than a record $49.97 billion surplus in the previous month.

[TITLE]Streaming Coalition Urges Squires to Support Enforcement Against Abusive SEP Licensing Practices:
[TEXT]
“Once the standard is widely adopted, [these SEP holders] renege on that promise and demand excessive royalties on threat of an injunction.” – Coalition letter

A coalition of ten streaming, broadcasting, and audiovisual media organizations, including the Motion Picture Association (MPA) and the Streaming Innovation Alliance, sent a letter today to U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) Director John Squires, urging him to “support strong public and private sector enforcement against abusive SEP licensing practices.” The signatories said that abuse of standard essential patents (SEPs) was undercutting the open process of setting standards for the video streaming technologies their members use.

In December 2025, the USPTO established an SEP Working Group that reports directly to Director Squires. The agency said the group would continue its practice of filing court statements supporting SEP injunctions, such as the statement of interest filed in Radian Memory Systems v. Samsung Electronics in June 2025.

The remaining signatories were the Association of Commercial Television and Video on Demand Services in Europe, the Commercial On-Demand and Broadcasting Association, Strima, the Asociación Española de Video on Demand, the European Video On Demand Coalition, Streaming for Australia, Streamingdiensten Nederland and the Television Association of Programmers Latin America. If left unaddressed, the letter said SEP abuse would harm consumers and competition, significantly affect investment in local creative economies, “misappropriate millions” intended for creative content and new offerings, and lower the quality of the viewing experience.

Standardized codec technologies such as AVC and HEVC allow competing streamers to provide dependable, high-quality video on devices from many manufacturers, the signatories explained. Since inclusion in a standard enhances a patent’s market power by requiring every potential competitor to license the technology, the coalition wrote, SEPs must be licensed on fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory (FRAND) terms.

According to the coalition, a growing list of SEP holders, many of which it said invest primarily in lawyers and litigation, had abandoned that principle for video streaming standards. These SEP holders commit to FRAND licensing to convince standards bodies, such as the International Organization for Standardization and the International Telecommunications Union, to incorporate their patented technology into industry standards, and then “once the standard is widely adopted, they renege on that promise and demand excessive royalties on threat of an injunction,” the letter stated. The coalition said those injunctions result from lawsuits filed in a handful of jurisdictions that routinely grant them without prompt review of the asserted patents’ validity or before a final infringement determination.

The signatories described this conduct as a “bait-and-switch patent ‘holdup’” affecting streamers bound to a standard for years, noting that AVC and HEVC were published in 2003 and 2013, respectively. According to the letter, the practice imposes large costs by destabilizing streaming technology standards, raising consumer prices, potentially diminishing commercial offerings and chilling streaming innovation while the industry remains in lengthy litigation.

The coalition stated that “the threat isn’t theoretical,” pointing to advisory FRAND royalty guidelines recently issued by a European court, identified in the letter’s footnotes as the Munich Regional Court. “Without any supporting evidence,” the letter said, the court proposed an annual codec royalty for two leading subscription video on demand (SVOD) services at 16% of one-third of annual subscription revenues, increasing to 25% for premium services. The signatories cited a Statista figure projecting nearly $100 billion in global SVOD revenues for 2026 and said that applying only the lower rate across the industry would drain well over $5 billion from the entertainment sector.

The 7th Civil Chamber of the Munich Regional Court I published the guidelines on August 13, 2026, building on recent decisions including ASUS I, ASUS II, Renault and ZTE v. Samsung. The guidelines reflect only that chamber’s views, and other courts may reach different conclusions.

All signatories support the protection of intellectual property (IP) rights, the coalition stated, adding that “it is precisely because we take IP rights so seriously” that they object to holdup tactics. The signatories said those tactics far exceed the value of the underlying patents, raise competition concerns, and damage the reputation of IP rights. The letter also referenced an amicus curiae brief the MPA submitted to the U.S. District Court in Delaware, which it said explained the conduct in detail.

That brief was filed in Disney Enterprises, Inc. v. InterDigital, Inc., an antitrust lawsuit Disney filed in August 2025 alleging that InterDigital’s licensing practices for video compression patents violated Sections 1 and 2 of the Sherman Act.

According to the letter, these SEP holders’ conduct is “cultivating a new generation of opponents for the IP community,” including car makers, TV manufacturers, healthcare providers and farmers. The signatories said they need to facilitate a FRAND licensing framework that respects IP rights and allows the streaming ecosystem to benefit creators, innovators, consumers, and IP rights holders. The coalition urged Director Squires to support public and private sector enforcement against abusive SEP licensing practices.

[TITLE]Sequoia Capital raises $10 billion in new funds, streamlines investment teams:
[TEXT]
Global venture capital firm Sequoia Capital has restructured its operations, streamlined its investment teams and raised around USD 10 billion in new funds as part of a broader effort to sharpen its focus on selective investments and strengthen support for startups.

In an announcement on X, marking a year since its leadership transition, the firm said it had simplified its operating structure, discontinued its Arc accelerator and Scouts programmes, and streamlined its investment and operating teams.

It said the changes would allow it to concentrate resources on fewer activities and provide better support to the founders it backs.

“We are best at high-conviction, highly selective venture capital investing. This is our focus,” the firm said.

As part of the restructuring, Sequoia has brought its early-stage and growth investment operations under a single structure, ending the distinction between the two teams. The move is aimed at making the firm’s engagement with founders more seamless as their businesses move through different stages of growth.

London office closure and organisational changes

The firm also announced the closure of its London office, saying it would continue supporting European founders from its base in the San Francisco Bay Area. It clarified that the decision was not related to the performance of its London team but reflected its view that a local presence was no longer necessary.

Sequoia said it had raised around USD 10 billion in new funds and strengthened its team by bringing in new talent, reuniting with former leaders and giving greater responsibilities to its operating team.

It has also made changes to flatten its partnership structure and reduce internal hierarchy, allowing founders to access the wider team’s expertise regardless of their lead investment partner.

The firm said it had moved away from its fixed Monday partner meeting cycle to make its internal processes more responsive to founders’ requirements. “Founders shouldn’t have to adhere to our process - we will do our best to adhere to yours,” it said.

Focus on emerging technologies

Sequoia further said it intends to focus on emerging areas including artificial intelligence, space, biology and reindustrialisation, which it believes could drive significant changes across industries.

The firm said its objective was not to expand its scale but to improve the quality of its investments and deliver better returns to investors while supporting founders in building companies.
