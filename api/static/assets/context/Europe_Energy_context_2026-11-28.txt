[TITLE]COP31 breakdown exposed a deepening Global South-West rift over climate transition:
[TEXT]
For India and other developing economies, COP31 reinforced the argument that shared climate goals cannot come at the expense of energy security and development needs

Negotiators at the UN climate summit in Antalya spent two weeks searching for an agreement that could satisfy nearly 200 countries. References to moving away from fossil fuels disappeared from the proposed agreement, while an earlier $120 billion adaptation-finance target was also dropped. Yet these concessions failed to secure a compromise.

COP31 ended without an overarching political agreement, exposing divisions extending beyond coal, oil, and gas. Countries remained divided over the pace of decarbonization, the financing of alternative energy systems, and the extent to which global commitments should reflect national circumstances.

For India, COP31’s breakdown reinforces a long-standing concern that global climate ambitions must accommodate the energy security and development priorities of emerging economies. The failure to reach an agreement in Antalya shows how difficult it remains to reconcile these competing demands.

Why fossil fuels are there to stay

At COP28 in Dubai, countries agreed to contribute towards transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems in a just, orderly, and equitable manner. Three years later, negotiators were still divided over what this commitment should mean in practice.

In Antalya, the European Union and climate-vulnerable countries pressed for stronger language on fossil fuels. Major petroleum-producing economies resisted a prescriptive phase-out approach, while developing countries questioned whether additional commitments would adequately recognize their financing and development requirements.

These differences reflect competing economic interests. For oil exporters, the transition threatens future hydrocarbon revenues. For import-dependent countries such as India, reducing fossil fuel consumption offers long-term benefits, but replacing existing energy infrastructure carries substantial costs.

The International Energy Agency’s World Energy Outlook 2026, released during the summit, illustrates this tension. Under its Stated Policies Scenario, global oil demand levels off at approximately 103 million barrels per day around 2030 before declining gradually. Natural gas demand continues growing into the mid-2030s.

These projections show how gradually global energy systems are likely to evolve under current and announced policies. But the 2026 disruption of energy flows through the Strait of Hormuz underscored a far more immediate risk. Described by the IEA as the largest energy supply shock in history, the crisis exposed the vulnerability of countries dependent on oil and gas imports transported through strategic maritime chokepoint

The crisis has also reinforced the energy security case for electrification. According to the IEA’s July 2026 assessment, electric car sales in India roughly doubled during March-June compared with the same period in 2025. Separately, ICRA reported that India’s electric two-wheeler sales reached 192,023 units in March, up 47.4% year-on-year. Although these dynamics cannot be attributed solely to the disruption, they show how electric mobility is gaining ground as countries seek to reduce their exposure to volatile oil markets.

The challenge extends beyond energy security. A November 2026 study by the Council on Energy, Environment and Water (CEEW), examining 21 submissions representing 115 countries, found broad recognition of the need to transition away from fossil fuels. Yet disagreements persisted over financing, technology, and implementation.

These differences help explain why agreement on reducing fossil fuel dependence has proved easier than reaching a consensus on how quickly countries should act and what support they need.

India’s case for energy transition

The differences over financing, technology, and implementation were also reflected in India’s intervention in Antalya. In his national statement on November 18, Environment Minister Bhupender Yadav emphasized equity, historical responsibility, and Common but Differentiated Responsibilities and Respective Capabilities (CBDR-RC). He also urged developed countries to fulfil their climate finance obligations.

Following the summit’s breakdown, Yadav reaffirmed India’s commitment to multilateral climate action, while maintaining that CBDR-RC must remain central to international negotiations.

These concerns are closely linked to India’s energy strategy. The country has committed to achieving net-zero emissions by 2070 and has already crossed the 50% threshold for non-fossil installed electricity capacity, ahead of its original 2030 target. Reducing dependence on imported petroleum is another important objective, making electrification relevant to both decarbonization and long-term energy security.

COP31’s proposed global electrification target brought these priorities into sharper focus. Türkiye advocated increasing electricity’s share of global final energy consumption to 35% by 2035, compared with approximately 20% today.

Electrification offers India an opportunity to reduce petroleum consumption, particularly in transport, while expanding domestic clean energy industries. However, the benefits depend on reliable electricity supplies, adequate grid infrastructure, and increasingly cleaner generation.

A CEEW assessment estimated that electricity could account for approximately 27% of India’s final energy consumption by 2035 under current trends. Reaching the proposed global benchmark would therefore require faster deployment of supporting infrastructure and technologies. India did not join the voluntary 35-by-2035 pledge, maintaining that electrification pathways should reflect national circumstances and development priorities.

The distinction is significant. An energy-importing economy can benefit substantially from accelerated electrification, but transition targets must account for infrastructure readiness, financing conditions and industrial requirements. India’s approach consequently highlights a broader consideration for global climate negotiations, as common decarbonization objectives need not require identical national pathways or implementation timelines.

For an energy-importing economy, accelerated electrification can bring significant benefits. But transition targets must also reflect infrastructure readiness, financing conditions, and industrial needs. India’s approach underscores a broader point for global climate negotiations: shared decarbonization goals do not require identical national pathways or timelines.

Climate finance and the implementation gap

The debate over fossil fuels cannot be separated from the unresolved question of finance.

Developing countries need investment beyond renewable generation, particularly in electricity grids, storage and climate adaptation. The IEA estimates that annual global grid investment must rise from $400 billion to approximately $600 billion by 2030.

Meanwhile, UNEP’s 2026 assessment puts developing countries’ adaptation finance needs at $305 billion annually by 2035, against international public flows of just $28.2-31.7 billion in 2024. Financing remains another obstacle, with IEA research finding that capital costs for renewable power and battery projects in emerging economies are at least twice those in advanced economies.

\These figures reflect distinct but overlapping financing needs, from adapting to climate impacts to building cleaner energy systems. At COP31, disagreements over adaptation funding persisted despite efforts to narrow differences. The removal of a quantified financing target from the revised negotiating text underscored the difficulty of securing commitments that match developing countries’ growing climate needs.

Beyond international financing commitments, domestic infrastructure planning presents another challenge. According to an August 2026 Reuters report, Indian renewable-energy developers had incurred approximately 4,500 crore rupees in losses since February 2025 as transmission constraints restricted power evacuation. ICRA estimated that nearly one-third of 54.8 GW of recently commissioned renewable capacity relied on temporary grid access as of May 2026.

These constraints demonstrate that financing clean energy generation is only part of the transition. Coordinated investment in transmission infrastructure is equally important if additional renewable capacity is to deliver its intended benefits.

The failure in Antalya therefore has competing interpretations. Countries demanding stronger fossil fuel language viewed the proposed agreement as inadequate for addressing climate change. Developing economies emphasized the importance of equity, finance, and national flexibility. Both concerns remain relevant to a credible transition framework.

Although COP31 failed to produce an overarching agreement, the negotiations did deliver progress on several specific climate decisions. The breakdown should not, therefore, be mistaken for a complete halt to international climate cooperation.

The Hormuz disruption strengthened the case for reducing dependence on petroleum, while constraints in India’s renewable power transmission network highlighted the infrastructure challenges involved in scaling up cleaner alternatives.

As attention turns towards COP32 in Ethiopia, Antalya leaves unresolved questions that have persisted through successive climate summits. COP28 established a commitment to transition away from fossil fuels, while COP29 adopted a $300 billion annual climate finance goal for developing countries by 2035. Yet COP31 demonstrated how difficult it remains to reconcile these commitments with competing national priorities and the practical requirements of implementation. For India, the test lies in whether expanding clean energy capacity can deliver reliable electricity while reducing dependence on imported petroleum.

Yemen's Houthi forces claimed on Wednesday that their strikes on Saudi Arabia's Ras Tanura oil refinery and export complex caused fires, with satellite imagery from NASA and Planet Labs revealing thick black smoke over the site. The Houthis also claimed multiple strikes on Riyadh's financial district, where Saudi civil defense reported 11 people were injured on Tuesday after a strike hit a tower housing the Public Investment Fund for the second time in under a week. U.S. commercial crude oil inventories rose by 1.1 million barrels to 418.9 million barrels in the week ended November 20, marking a third consecutive weekly build as higher net imports and domestic output outweighed increased refinery runs while crude exports dropped to their lowest level since early August amid higher freight costs and softer Asian demand. Traders will be watching the November 29 OPEC+ ministerial meeting, where members are set to discuss 2027 production policy amid competing views on potential supply surpluses and internal quota disputes.

And now, oil and commodity experts at Standard Chartered have weighed in, saying that oil prices are unlikely to sink below recent lows, with further downside needing clearer evidence of sustained inventory builds or a faster recovery in physical supply, even as the geopolitical uncertainty continues to complicate the outlook. Brent crude spent much of the past week consolidating around the low $90s/bbl, following a sharp correction from the late-October highs, before slipping below $90/bbl on Monday with the $89.37/bbl settlement the lowest in nearly three months.

Related: Goldman Sachs Sees U.S. Natural Gas Demand Surging on Data Center Boom

According to the analysts, the price action reflects the increasingly entrenched bearish fundamental narrative, which was reinforced by the IEA’s substantial upward revision to Q4 supply. The IEA raised its Q4 supply estimate by 3.3mb/d in its November Oil Market Report, reflecting a faster recovery in Gulf production and exports than previously expected. This has reinforced expectations of a return to surplus by the end of the year, coupled with a more substantial build in 2027. However, StanChart says that the recent price correction has already discounted much of the anticipated supply surplus, implying that a sustained move lower may require evidence that the surplus will emerge sooner, be larger, or persist for longer than currently expected, rather than simply confirming existing forecasts.

That said, there’s an ongoing broad decline across refined product prices, refining margins and freight rates, suggesting that some of the exceptional tightness associated with geopolitical disruptions is beginning to unwind. According to StanChart, European jet fuel (NWE Jet CIF cargo balance of month) and Asian fuel oil (Singapore 380 M1) prices declined 24% and 31% MTD, respectively, while the European diesel crack (ICE gasoil/Brent spread) has fallen 21%, indicating that the adjustment extends beyond falling crude prices.

Refining economics have also weakened, with both Asian FCC margins and NWE hydrocracking margins down 25% MTD. Meanwhile, Middle East-to-China (TD3C) clean product freight rates have declined 27%, while dirty VLCC freight rates on the Gulf-to-China route have fallen 55% MTD. These developments provide increasingly broad evidence of easing market stress, although absolute prices and transportation costs remain elevated.

However, the gradual easing of physical market tightness continues against a backdrop of uncertain diplomatic progress between Washington and Tehran, with the latest spate of attacks on regional energy infrastructure and shipping underscoring the vulnerability of the physical supply recovery. The muted price response suggests that the market is increasingly discounting persistent hostilities in the absence of material additional supply losses.

StanChart has predicted that a diplomatic breakthrough could accelerate the resumption of physical flows and reinforce downward price pressure, while renewed disruptions to export infrastructure or maritime routes could quickly challenge expectations of a surplus. Continued attacks on Russian energy infrastructure also constrain the recovery in middle distillate availability, even as broader crude supply conditions improve.

Meanwhile, OPEC+ meets on Sunday, three months after it completed the rollback of 1.65 million bpd of voluntary cuts with a final 188,000 bpd increase in September. The group then agreed to hold September targets through December, according to Rystad Energy. StanChart says members now have to decide whether to add barrels, hold steady or cut again as surplus forecasts grow. They also have to settle their 2027 production baselines without splitting the group. Iraq's weight in the group grew when the UAE left in May, "although not necessarily its formal influence," the bank says. Baghdad has a long record of producing above its quota and wants OPEC+ to recognize its higher production capacity. Recent OPEC+ meetings have barely moved prices. StanChart says Sunday's could, particularly if members agree to act together on supply again.

The analysts note that the UAE’s departure in May has increased the relative importance of Iraq, although not necessarily its formal influence. Iraq has historically been among the group’s least compliant members on quotas and is seeking recognition of its higher production capacity. StanChart says that whereas recent OPEC+ meetings have elicited little market reaction, the Sunday meeting could prove more consequential for near-term crude prices, particularly if it signals a renewed and collective willingness to manage supply in response to an increasingly bearish balance.

[TITLE]Ukraine's Strike in Benghazi Threatens the Deal That Keeps Libya at Peace:
[TEXT]
Ukraine's sinking of a Russian warship in Benghazi on November 22 is the spark that could ignite another civil war in Libya, just as Tripoli is pitching foreign oil companies at an energy summit for the $20 billion it says it needs to lift output to 2 million barrels a day. Two officers of eastern strongman Khalifa Haftar's Libyan National Army (LNA) died of their wounds on November 24, and the LNA has vowed to retaliate "at the appropriate time and place." Haftar flew to Turkey the same day on his first known visit, and on November 25 his parliament published the Turkey-Libya maritime deal in Libya's official gazette, completing a ratification that opens waters off the eastern coast to Turkish exploration. On the day of Haftar's visit, EU Mediterranean Commissioner Dubravka Šuica told the European Parliament that the deal "is null and void."

Everyone is scrambling for leverage, and there's plenty of it flying around, making this difficult to contain.

Ukrainian sea drones hit the Russian corvette Merkury in the civilian part of Benghazi's port, next to a naval base, where it had docked to refuel and resupply under a cooperation agreement between Russia and Haftar's eastern government. Moscow has no port of its own in the southern Mediterranean, and it has funded projects at Haftar-controlled air bases including al-Jufra and al-Khadim. The drones were most likely launched from Misrata, a western city where Abdulhamid Dbeibah's Tripoli government hosts Ukrainian drone teams and Turkish troops. Moscow has a reason to strike western Libya, and any strike launched from Haftar's bases would make his family a party to it.

Related: Tanker Traffic at Hormuz Ticks Up Despite Intensified Attacks

The strike puts at risk a business partnership between the two families' heirs, documented by the UN. Ibrahim Dbeibah, the prime minister's nephew and adviser, and Saddam Haftar, Khalifa Haftar's son and deputy, had built control over the National Oil Corporation (NOC) "at every level of the decision-making process," according to the UN Panel of Experts' final report. Their deal created the Arkenu Oil Company, which diverted more than $3 billion between January 2024 and November 2025 alone through illicit fuel exports from Benghazi, Ras Lanuf and Tobruk, all ports outside the NOC's control.

The UN also documented an arms procurement pact between Dbeibah's government and Haftar's forces under their joint patronage. It covered vehicles shipped to Misrata and Benghazi under an end-user certificate the prime minister signed in his capacity as defense minister. By the UN panel's count, Libya's oil revenue in 2025, excluding royalties and taxes, came to $18.78 billion, against the roughly $27 billion it should have earned. Saddam Haftar's office and a Tripoli government official deny that either has any connection to Arkenu.

The rival legislatures have agreed on a unified budget, and Belqasim Haftar runs the state fund rebuilding Derna. Washington is drafting a merger that would keep Abdulhamid Dbeibah as prime minister and seat Saddam Haftar at the head of the Presidential Council. Armed factions in the west are resisting the plan, and Washington has warned it could take measures against anyone who blocks it, Al-Monitor reported.

Earlier this month, an influential Misrata military body demanded an immediate end to the Arkenu deal, declaring that "Libyan oil is a red line." On November 24, two days after the Ukrainian strike, Dbeibah's government asked a Tripoli court to annul the contract. Jalel Harchaoui of the Royal United Services Institute says the pressure on Dbeibah comes partly from powerful groups in Misrata, where the Haftars are mistrusted and where Ukraine's drone teams are reportedly based.

The Strike on Russia's Libya Supply Line

The Ukrainian strike hit the supply line that ties Moscow to the Haftars. Harchaoui said in December 2024, after Assad fell, that the loss of Russia's Syrian bases was pushing Moscow to ramp up its presence in eastern Libya. The Merkury regularly escorted the Russian convoys that supply Syria and Libya, and Kyiv says those convoys included the cargo ship Angara, which was struck offshore hours after the corvette. It was the Merkury's third call at Benghazi in under two months, according to The New Arab.

Moscow has sent investigators to Benghazi, according to The New Arab, and its ambassador to Libya, Aydar Aganin, says the strike will not go unanswered. Haftar's eastern government has opened a joint investigation with Russia and blamed "treacherous Ukrainian aggression," while Dbeibah's government in Tripoli condemned the attack and distanced itself from Kyiv.

Russia could retaliate with a strike on western Libya, but not without involving Haftar. Russia's Africa Corps reportedly operates from Libyan air bases alongside his forces, and the al-Khadim air base in the east has served as a Russian supply hub. A Russian strike on Misrata launched from those bases would be read in Tripoli, Ankara and Washington as a Haftar strike, the scenario most likely to restart the war.

Moscow has few ways to strike Libya from outside the country. It lost its Syrian ports after Assad fell, Turkey has kept the Turkish Straits closed to warships since February 2022, and its remaining Mediterranean vessels are now targets themselves. A strike on Misrata would also risk killing Turkish soldiers and would put Moscow in direct conflict with the NATO member that controls its only sea route out of the Black Sea. Moscow's other options include retaliation inside Ukraine, covert action against Ukrainian personnel in western Libya, diplomatic pressure on Tripoli and a larger military presence in the east.

Harchaoui told Al Jazeera that the strike humiliated Moscow and the Haftars alike, and that "both the Russian state and the Haftar family will feel compelled to retaliate – somehow." Libyan military analyst Abdulhafid al-Saied told Al-Araby al-Jadeed that Haftar now has to keep the Russians, his most important allies, happy without losing Washington and the Europeans.

The Multi-Player Balancing Act

A return to war would cost the Haftars their share of the schemes the UN documented, along with the Presidential Council seat Washington is offering Saddam. Refusing Moscow would risk the patron that arms them. Their forces control many of the fields, pipelines and terminals that feed the Tripoli-based NOC, and shutdowns of that infrastructure by Haftar-aligned groups have repeatedly cut Libyan output. Russia's presence in Libya runs through their bases, and the offshore acreage Turkey wants to explore is off their coast.

Dbeibah's government and Misrata stand to lose the US-backed premiership and the revenue arrangements with the east. Hosting Ukrainian drone teams has made Misrata the most likely target of any retaliation.

Ankara has little leverage over Haftar. Turkey fought him in 2020, when its drones and troops broke his siege of Tripoli, and only began courting his camp in 2022. Turkish intelligence chief ?brahim Kal?n met the Haftars in Benghazi in August 2025. In July, Turkey reached understandings with Haftar's forces on training and military facilities. In August, Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan met Saddam Haftar in Benghazi. Haftar's parliament ratified the maritime deal in October, and Erdogan received Haftar in Ankara on November 25. The UAE is Haftar's main backer, Egypt is his longtime backer, and Russia supplies his weapons.

Turkish analyst Göktu? Çal??kan warned in August that Ankara's biggest risk in courting Haftar was losing trust in western Libya, where Misrata and Tripoli could read the opening to the east as abandonment, in exchange for only limited room to balance Russia there. The strike leaves Turkey with a partner on each side. Misrata, its oldest ally in Libya and host to its troops, is the likely target of any reprisal. Haftar, its newest partner, whose parliament ratified its offshore deal, controls the bases a reprisal would come from.

The Libya-Turkey maritime deal could also be used for leverage in this game.

Turkey signed it in 2019 with Fayez al-Sarraj, the then-head of Libya's Tripoli government. It draws a boundary between Turkish and Libyan waters across the eastern Mediterranean and gives Turkey a claim to drill off eastern Libya, where its state oil company, TPAO, already holds 40% of an offshore block northwest of Benghazi. Greece, Cyprus, Egypt and the EU say the deal is illegal under the UN Law of the Sea, which Turkey has never signed. Greece argues that Turkey and Libya have no facing coastlines to divide and that the line runs through waters belonging to Crete and other Greek islands. Egypt says it infringes Egypt's own maritime zone.

Haftar's parliament kept the deal off the floor for six years under speaker Aguila Saleh, who is close to Cairo. Saleh was replaced on October 6 by the head of the parliament's Libyan-Turkish friendship committee, and two weeks later the house approved the deal unanimously. Essentially, Saddam Haftar pushed it through to win Turkish backing for his bid to head the Presidential Council under the US merger plan.

A Russian strike on Misrata launched from Haftar's bases would put Turkish soldiers in the line of fire and make Turkey's position in Libya, and its drilling claim, messier and less certain. It would also cost Haftar the Turkish backing he is counting on for Saddam's seat on the Presidential Council. That would likely mean civil war.

[TITLE]COP31 breakdown exposed a deepening Global South-West rift over climate transition:
[TEXT]
For India and other developing economies, COP31 reinforced the argument that shared climate goals cannot come at the expense of energy security and development needs

Negotiators at the UN climate summit in Antalya spent two weeks searching for an agreement that could satisfy nearly 200 countries. References to moving away from fossil fuels disappeared from the proposed agreement, while an earlier $120 billion adaptation-finance target was also dropped. Yet these concessions failed to secure a compromise.

COP31 ended without an overarching political agreement, exposing divisions extending beyond coal, oil, and gas. Countries remained divided over the pace of decarbonization, the financing of alternative energy systems, and the extent to which global commitments should reflect national circumstances.

For India, COP31’s breakdown reinforces a long-standing concern that global climate ambitions must accommodate the energy security and development priorities of emerging economies. The failure to reach an agreement in Antalya shows how difficult it remains to reconcile these competing demands.

Why fossil fuels are there to stay

At COP28 in Dubai, countries agreed to contribute towards transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems in a just, orderly, and equitable manner. Three years later, negotiators were still divided over what this commitment should mean in practice.

In Antalya, the European Union and climate-vulnerable countries pressed for stronger language on fossil fuels. Major petroleum-producing economies resisted a prescriptive phase-out approach, while developing countries questioned whether additional commitments would adequately recognize their financing and development requirements.

These differences reflect competing economic interests. For oil exporters, the transition threatens future hydrocarbon revenues. For import-dependent countries such as India, reducing fossil fuel consumption offers long-term benefits, but replacing existing energy infrastructure carries substantial costs.

The International Energy Agency’s World Energy Outlook 2026, released during the summit, illustrates this tension. Under its Stated Policies Scenario, global oil demand levels off at approximately 103 million barrels per day around 2030 before declining gradually. Natural gas demand continues growing into the mid-2030s.

These projections show how gradually global energy systems are likely to evolve under current and announced policies. But the 2026 disruption of energy flows through the Strait of Hormuz underscored a far more immediate risk. Described by the IEA as the largest energy supply shock in history, the crisis exposed the vulnerability of countries dependent on oil and gas imports transported through strategic maritime chokepoint

The crisis has also reinforced the energy security case for electrification. According to the IEA’s July 2026 assessment, electric car sales in India roughly doubled during March-June compared with the same period in 2025. Separately, ICRA reported that India’s electric two-wheeler sales reached 192,023 units in March, up 47.4% year-on-year. Although these dynamics cannot be attributed solely to the disruption, they show how electric mobility is gaining ground as countries seek to reduce their exposure to volatile oil markets.

The challenge extends beyond energy security. A November 2026 study by the Council on Energy, Environment and Water (CEEW), examining 21 submissions representing 115 countries, found broad recognition of the need to transition away from fossil fuels. Yet disagreements persisted over financing, technology, and implementation.

These differences help explain why agreement on reducing fossil fuel dependence has proved easier than reaching a consensus on how quickly countries should act and what support they need.

India’s case for energy transition

The differences over financing, technology, and implementation were also reflected in India’s intervention in Antalya. In his national statement on November 18, Environment Minister Bhupender Yadav emphasized equity, historical responsibility, and Common but Differentiated Responsibilities and Respective Capabilities (CBDR-RC). He also urged developed countries to fulfil their climate finance obligations.

Following the summit’s breakdown, Yadav reaffirmed India’s commitment to multilateral climate action, while maintaining that CBDR-RC must remain central to international negotiations.

These concerns are closely linked to India’s energy strategy. The country has committed to achieving net-zero emissions by 2070 and has already crossed the 50% threshold for non-fossil installed electricity capacity, ahead of its original 2030 target. Reducing dependence on imported petroleum is another important objective, making electrification relevant to both decarbonization and long-term energy security.

COP31’s proposed global electrification target brought these priorities into sharper focus. Türkiye advocated increasing electricity’s share of global final energy consumption to 35% by 2035, compared with approximately 20% today.

Electrification offers India an opportunity to reduce petroleum consumption, particularly in transport, while expanding domestic clean energy industries. However, the benefits depend on reliable electricity supplies, adequate grid infrastructure, and increasingly cleaner generation.

A CEEW assessment estimated that electricity could account for approximately 27% of India’s final energy consumption by 2035 under current trends. Reaching the proposed global benchmark would therefore require faster deployment of supporting infrastructure and technologies. India did not join the voluntary 35-by-2035 pledge, maintaining that electrification pathways should reflect national circumstances and development priorities.

The distinction is significant. An energy-importing economy can benefit substantially from accelerated electrification, but transition targets must account for infrastructure readiness, financing conditions and industrial requirements. India’s approach consequently highlights a broader consideration for global climate negotiations, as common decarbonization objectives need not require identical national pathways or implementation timelines.

For an energy-importing economy, accelerated electrification can bring significant benefits. But transition targets must also reflect infrastructure readiness, financing conditions, and industrial needs. India’s approach underscores a broader point for global climate negotiations: shared decarbonization goals do not require identical national pathways or timelines.

Climate finance and the implementation gap

The debate over fossil fuels cannot be separated from the unresolved question of finance.

Developing countries need investment beyond renewable generation, particularly in electricity grids, storage and climate adaptation. The IEA estimates that annual global grid investment must rise from $400 billion to approximately $600 billion by 2030.

Meanwhile, UNEP’s 2026 assessment puts developing countries’ adaptation finance needs at $305 billion annually by 2035, against international public flows of just $28.2-31.7 billion in 2024. Financing remains another obstacle, with IEA research finding that capital costs for renewable power and battery projects in emerging economies are at least twice those in advanced economies.

\These figures reflect distinct but overlapping financing needs, from adapting to climate impacts to building cleaner energy systems. At COP31, disagreements over adaptation funding persisted despite efforts to narrow differences. The removal of a quantified financing target from the revised negotiating text underscored the difficulty of securing commitments that match developing countries’ growing climate needs.

Beyond international financing commitments, domestic infrastructure planning presents another challenge. According to an August 2026 Reuters report, Indian renewable-energy developers had incurred approximately 4,500 crore rupees in losses since February 2025 as transmission constraints restricted power evacuation. ICRA estimated that nearly one-third of 54.8 GW of recently commissioned renewable capacity relied on temporary grid access as of May 2026.

These constraints demonstrate that financing clean energy generation is only part of the transition. Coordinated investment in transmission infrastructure is equally important if additional renewable capacity is to deliver its intended benefits.

The failure in Antalya therefore has competing interpretations. Countries demanding stronger fossil fuel language viewed the proposed agreement as inadequate for addressing climate change. Developing economies emphasized the importance of equity, finance, and national flexibility. Both concerns remain relevant to a credible transition framework.

Although COP31 failed to produce an overarching agreement, the negotiations did deliver progress on several specific climate decisions. The breakdown should not, therefore, be mistaken for a complete halt to international climate cooperation.

The Hormuz disruption strengthened the case for reducing dependence on petroleum, while constraints in India’s renewable power transmission network highlighted the infrastructure challenges involved in scaling up cleaner alternatives.

As attention turns towards COP32 in Ethiopia, Antalya leaves unresolved questions that have persisted through successive climate summits. COP28 established a commitment to transition away from fossil fuels, while COP29 adopted a $300 billion annual climate finance goal for developing countries by 2035. Yet COP31 demonstrated how difficult it remains to reconcile these commitments with competing national priorities and the practical requirements of implementation. For India, the test lies in whether expanding clean energy capacity can deliver reliable electricity while reducing dependence on imported petroleum.
