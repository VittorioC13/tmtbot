[TITLE]The Irish Times view on Trump’s tariffs: the US president doubles down:
[TEXT]
Any hope that the midterm election defeat might lead US president Donald Trump to back away from his tariffs policy has been dashed by the latest announcement from the White House. The Trump administration is to impose a round of new tariffs, though in reality much of this is finding a way to deal with the rejection by the US Supreme Court earlier this year of the legal basis for earlier tariffs.

Trump is doubling down on his misguided trade policies, even if the White House is indicating it will respect previous deals. This means that the maximum tariff to be imposed on exports from EU countries such as Ireland will be the 15 per cent agreed at Turnberry in Scotland last year.

This may mean somewhat higher tariffs on some Irish exporters to the US, though the overall impact looks set to be limited. The expectation is that pharmaceuticals, by far the largest Irish export to the US, will continue to be excluded from tariffs. Trump has stuck deals with the major US pharma companies, believed to involve investment commitments by them in the US, in return for tariff-free trade.

However, the official US documents used to justify the tariffs without recourse to Congress – based on alleged unfair trade practices – again focuses on Ireland and its pharma sector. The Trump administration remains unhappy with the large trade surplus Ireland holds with the US, presenting this as a sign of unfair economic practices.

It then goes on to discuss the large production of pharma drugs and medicines in Ireland for the US market and argues this poses a risk for the US market and its consumers. If any disruption were to hit, it says, US supply chains could face shortages or delays.

Much of this is economically incoherent. And it is not clear what it means for Trump’s policy, besides doubling down on efforts to get future pharma investment in the US. But the fixation in the White House with having key products manufactured at home remains very much in place. And this continues to carry risks for Ireland.

[TITLE]The Sec61-EMC complex promotes biosynthesis of the SARS-CoV-2 non-structural proteins:
[TEXT]
The genomic RNA of SARS-CoV-2, the virus responsible for the global COVID-19 pandemic, encodes polyproteins pp1a and pp1ab which contain 16 non-structural proteins (NSPs) that support critical steps in infection. Despite this, how the polyproteins are synthesized and membrane-inserted in the host cell is unclear. Here we demonstrate that SARS-CoV-2 exploits the Sec61-endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane complex (EMC) translation machinery to promote biosynthesis of the virus polyproteins, leading to successful replication and infection. The Sec61-EMC complex engages NSP3—the first transmembrane (TM) protein in pp1a/pp1ab—during active translation. Strikingly, of the four TM segments in NSP3, a marginally hydrophobic domain between TM3 and TM4 is required for EMC-binding and confers EMC-dependent expression. Our results reveal an ER-dependent mechanism for translation of the SARS-CoV-2 NSPs during virus infection, providing insights that should lead to new anti-SARS-CoV-2 therapeutic approaches.

SARS-CoV-2 viral non-structural proteins (NSPs) are necessary for several key steps of viral infection including double membrane vesicle (DMV) formation, genome replication and transcription, and immune evasion. The NSPs are initially synthesized as one large polyprotein containing several multipass transmembrane proteins that require insertion into the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane for productive infection. How the host cell translates and inserts this unique client protein remains unclear. In this study, we demonstrate that the Sec61-ER membrane complex (EMC) is required for efficient NSP biosynthesis. Mechanistically, we further demonstrate that the marginally hydrophobic domain between transmembrane domains 3 and 4 of NSP3 is the molecular determinant for its EMC dependency.

Here, using a combination of chemical and genetic loss-of-function strategies, we found that SARS-CoV-2 co-opts the Sec61-EMC biosynthetic machinery to translate the virus NSPs, leading to genome replication and productive infection. Mechanistically, the Sec61-EMC complex interacts with NSP3—the first TM protein in pp1a/pp1ab—during active translation. We further identify a marginally hydrophobic domain between TM3 and TM4 of NSP3 is required for EMC interaction and renders expression of NSP3 EMC-dependent. Our findings reveal a new mechanism responsible for translation of SARS-CoV-2 NSPs, and these insights should provide rational therapeutic approaches to combat virus infection.

Non-structural viral membrane proteins often harbor marginally hydrophobic TM segments [ 37 – 39 ]. This marginally hydrophobic feature, also present in cellular proteins, destabilizes insertion of the membrane proteins. To rectify this, a Sec61-binding partner called the ER membrane complex (EMC) acts as a membrane chaperone, binding to the marginally hydrophobic TM segments to ensure stable membrane-insertion of these membrane proteins. For example, NS4A and NS4B of DENV and ZIKV possess marginally hydrophobic domains that are dependent on the EMC for expression [ 37 , 40 ]. Intriguingly, structural analysis suggests that NSP3 and NSP6 of SARS-CoV-1 contain marginally hydrophobic domains [ 38 ]. Whether this is conserved across the coronavirus family (including SARS-CoV-2) and whether translation of the virus polyprotein harboring these membrane proteins depends on the EMC, remain unknown.

There is no reported evidence that the pp1a/pp1ab polyproteins of SARS-CoV-2 exploit the ER-dependent Sec61 translocon for biosynthesis and membrane-insertion. Because these polyproteins lack the canonical SS at their N-terminus, the first membrane protein NSP3 may instead be used to target the polyproteins for Sec61-dependent translation. Thus, if translation of SARS-CoV-2 pp1a/pp1ab is Sec61-dependent, NSP3 may be the key determinant that links vRNA translation and NSP membrane insertion. Interestingly, other positive-sense RNA viruses—including flaviviruses such as Zika virus (ZIKV), Dengue virus (DENV), and West Nile virus (WNV)—exploit the Sec61 translocon for infection [ 36 ] and biogenesis of their non-structural proteins [ 37 ].

The Sec61 translocation channel (“translocon”) is the central component of the well-characterized ER-associated translation apparatus responsible for co-translational translocation of soluble and membrane proteins targeted to the ER [ 32 ]. Co-translational translocation is initiated when an N-terminal signal sequence (SS) in a soluble or membrane protein—or the first TM segment in a membrane protein lacking the SS—emerges from the ribosome during translation of the mRNA [ 33 , 34 ]. The cytosolic signal recognition particle (SRP) recognizes and binds to the exposed SS (or the TM segment), temporarily pausing translation and targeting the mRNA-ribosome-nascent chain-SRP complex to the SRP receptor on the ER membrane. The SRP-SRP receptor interaction results in transfer of the mRNA-ribosome-nascent chain complex from the SRP receptor to the Sec61 translocon, enabling translation to resume. Mechanistically, a soluble protein threads through the central pore formed by the Sec61 translocon to reach the ER lumen, while the TM segment of a membrane protein instead moves across the Sec61 “lateral gate” and partitions into the lipid bilayer of the ER membrane to assume its membrane-inserted topology [ 35 ].

A major enigma after virus entry is how the SARS-CoV-2 vRNA (encoding the NSPs) is translated so that the resulting TM NSPs are inserted into the ER membrane which is required for their biological function. Although current models posit that vRNA translation occurs strictly in the cytosol by exploiting the cytosol-localized ribosome translation machinery [ 22 , 24 – 31 ], how the multi-pass NSP3, NSP4, and NSP6 proteins are synthesized in the cytosol and inserted into the ER membrane without utilizing any ER-associated components is difficult to envision. Indeed, the lack of primary data supporting this model raises the distinct possibility that another translation mechanism is responsible for biosynthesis and membrane-insertion of the NSPs.

The NSPs are initially synthesized as a large polyprotein, pp1a or pp1ab, with pp1a comprising NSPs 1–10, and pp1ab comprising NSPs 1–16 which is generated by a ribosomal frameshift event [ 21 , 22 ]. After translation, proteolytic cleavage of the polyproteins, mediated primarily by NSP3 and NSP5, generates the individual NSPs. Of the 16 NSPs, NSP3, NSP4, and NSP6 are multi-pass transmembrane (TM) proteins. These three TM proteins act in concert to remodel the ER membrane, with NSP3 and NSP4 being both necessary and sufficient to drive DMV formation [ 19 , 23 ]. Although insights into the mechanism of vRNA replication in DMVs are slowly emerging, events preceding DMV formation after virus entry remain unclear.

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), an enveloped positive-sense single-stranded RNA virus, is the causative agent of the global COVID-19 pandemic [ 1 – 4 ]. Despite the profound impact of SARS-CoV-2 on human health [ 5 ], the cellular basis of infection is not entirely understood. An important infection step is the replication of the viral RNA (vRNA) genome, which occurs in double-membrane vesicles (DMVs) derived from the host endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [ 6 – 13 ]. Formation of these DMVs requires extensive ER membrane remodeling and, importantly, is orchestrated by non-structural proteins (NSPs) encoded by the vRNA [ 7 , 14 – 20 ].

The Sec61 translocon binds to SARS-CoV-2 vRNA and NSPs during live infection NSP3, NSP4, and NSP6 in polyproteins pp1a/pp1ab promote SARS-CoV-2 genome replication [7,16–19], yet how these TM proteins are synthesized and inserted into the host membrane remain poorly understood. We hypothesize that pp1a/pp1ab exploits Sec61 for co-translational translocation, enabling NSP3, NSP4, and NSP6 to insert into the ER membrane in order to function. To test this, we performed immunoprecipitation-mass spectrometry (IP-MS) analysis using extracts derived from cells infected with SARS-CoV-2 to determine whether the viral NSPs physically interact with Sec61. Human liver Huh7 cells stably expressing the virus entry receptor ACE2 (Huh7-ACE2) are routinely used to study SARS-CoV-2 infection [41]. Accordingly, Huh7-ACE2 cells transiently expressing FLAG-tagged Sec61β (a Sec61 subunit) were infected with SARS-CoV-2 (USA-WA1/2020 variant) for 14 h. The infected cell extracts were immunoprecipitated with anti-FLAG beads and eluted with 3xFLAG peptide to isolate the Sec61 complex and any interacting proteins, and the eluted material subjected to MS analysis. FLAG-GFP was used as a negative control because it is a cytosolic protein with no known association with Sec61. Importantly, MS analysis of the FLAG-Sec61β-bound material identified peptides corresponding to NSP3, NSP6, NSP8, and NSP14 of SARS-CoV-2, while none of these viral peptides were detected in the FLAG-GFP control (Fig 1A, with the peptide coverage scores shown in S1A Fig). Notably, in addition to the multi-pass TM proteins NSP3 and NSP6, peptides corresponding to cytosolic NSP8 and NSP14 of the polyprotein were also found in the FLAG-Sec61β (but not FLAG-GFP) immunoprecipitated sample. To support the IP-MS results, we found that NSP3 co-immunoprecipitated with FLAG-Sec61β-GFP 11 during infection (S1B Fig). These findings suggest that full-length polyproteins pp1a/pp1ab interact with the Sec61 translocon prior to proteolytic cleavage, presumably because this biosynthetic machinery is used to translate pp1a/pp1ab. To assess whether virus entry is affected by CK147 or Apra S4 treatment, we collected vRNA at 2 hpi (prior to active replication of the vRNA) and determined the vRNA level. During Sec61-dependent translation, membrane proteins lacking a SS (such as pp1a/pp1ab) rely on their first TM segment to target to Sec61 [33,34]. We therefore focused on evaluating whether Sec61 is responsible for translation of NSP3 because it is the first membrane protein in pp1a/pp1ab. Because NSP3 is part of the virus pp1a/pp1ab polyprotein, translation of other NSPs in this polyprotein is expected to also rely on Sec61. [TITLE]From Waste to Water: Would You Drink Recycled Sewage?:
[TEXT]
Imagine filling a glass from your kitchen tap. The water is clear, cold and seemingly ordinary. Now imagine being told that, only hours earlier, that same water had been sewage.

Would you drink it?

For most, the instinctive answer is no. Yet as climate change, population growth and pollution increase pressure on freshwater supplies, converting wastewater into drinking water is becoming a policy consideration. The question is no longer whether we can treat wastewater; we largely can. The greater challenge is ensuring the water is safe, sustainable and acceptable to those who will consume it.

That debate has become particularly urgent in the UK. In August, testing connected to Thames Water’s proposed £350 million ($470 million) water-recycling scheme found levels of perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS), a toxic “forever chemical,” as high as 13 times the UK surface-water limit during pilot trials. PFOS is a synthetic chemical used in products such as firefighting foams and stain-resistant coatings. It belongs to the Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances, or PFAS, family, known as “forever chemicals” because they persist in the environment for decades.

The project is designed to address future water shortages by taking water from the River Thames and replacing it with highly treated wastewater during droughts. Thames Water, the UK’s largest private water and wastewater services company, has said the pilot was designed to test different technologies and does not represent the final system, while the UK’s Environment Agency is reviewing the results.

This controversy reveals a broader issue: The future water crisis may involve defining acceptable water quality and determining the level of investment required to achieve it. When recycling trials of the Teddington Direct River Abstraction (TDRA) revealed toxic chemical breakthroughs, engineers had to integrate highly expensive advanced filtration systems. Eventually, these small-scale tests serve as financial gatekeepers, forcing projected project budgets to increase to meet strict quality demands.

From waste to resource

The UN’s 2026 World Water Development Report estimates that 2.1 billion people still lack safely managed drinking water. Climate change and water scarcity are intensifying existing inequalities in access to water and sanitation.

For centuries, wastewater has been treated as waste to be removed and discharged. After treatment, water is typically released into rivers, lakes or oceans. Climate change is now challenging this linear approach. Freshwater is therefore becoming too valuable to use once and discard. Meanwhile, wastewater is constantly being produced. Every shower, washing machine, industrial process and toilet creates a potential source of water. With sufficiently advanced treatment, that water can be used again — for agriculture, industrial processes, landscaping, groundwater recharge and, in some cases, drinking.

[TITLE]Thymol attenuates bleomycin‑induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice by regulating the E2F8/NF‑κB/TGF‑β1/smad signaling pathway:
[TEXT]
This study demonstrates that thymol can suppress fibroblast proliferation and activation by regulating the E2F8/NF‑κB/TGF‑β1/Smad signaling axis, and thymol exerts selective partial transcriptional reprogramming thereby alleviating inflammation and pulmonary fibrosis in mice. These findings provide a foundational basis for further exploration of thymol as a candidate antifibrotic agent in IPF therapy.

Thymol at concentrations up to 160 μM exhibited no significant cytotoxicity toward normal human lung fibroblasts. Thymol below 160 μM significantly inhibited TGF-β1-induced proliferation of fibroblasts (P < 0.05). Compared to the control group, the TGF-β1-induced group exhibited increased mRNA and protein levels of α-SMA, Collagen I, and Fibronectin, as well as enhanced phosphorylation of p65, Smad2, and Smad3, and elevated E2F8 mRNA expression (P < 0.05). These changes were significantly suppressed in both the Thymol (80 μM) + TGF-β1 and Thymol (160 μM) + TGF-β1 groups (P < 0.05), except for Collagen I, which remained unaffected. Transcriptomic data revealed that thymol only partially reversed TGF‑β1‑triggered transcriptional alterations, preferentially suppressing myofibroblast‑contractile gene programs rather than broadly abrogating all extracellular‑matrix‑related transcripts. In vivo, the BLM group showed significant increases in TGF-β1 levels, white blood cell, neutrophil, and lymphocyte counts, as well as in alveolar inflammation and fibrosis scores, compared to the control group. These parameters were significantly reduced in the thymol-treated groups (P < 0.05).

A CCK-8 assay was employed to determine the non-toxic concentration range of thymol. Human lung fibroblasts (HFL1 cells) were divided into four groups: Control, TGF-β1-induced, Thymol (80 μM) + TGF-β1, and Thymol (160 μM) + TGF-β1. The mRNA and protein expression levels of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), Collagen I, and Fibronectin were assessed by RT-qPCR and Western blotting. A bleomycin (BLM)-induced mouse model of pulmonary fibrosis was established and divided into five groups: Control, BLM-induced, Thymol (60 mg/kg), Thymol (120 mg/kg), and Nintedanib (100 mg/kg). Survival rate, body weight, and inflammatory indices were recorded. Lung inflammation and fibrosis were evaluated using hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson's trichrome staining. Transcriptome‑wide RNA‑seq analysis was performed to characterize global transcriptional reprogramming induced by TGF‑β1 and thymol, including principal component analysis, differential‑gene screening, and Gene Ontology enrichment analysis. Differentially expressed genes were screened by RNA-seq, and expression levels of E2F8 and components of the NF-κB/TGF-β1/Smad pathway were validated by RT-qPCR and Western blotting.

Traditional herbal medicine has garnered increasing attention due to its safety profile, accessibility, and cost-effectiveness. Thymol, chemically known as 2-isopropyl-5-methylphenol, is a naturally occurring monoterpenoid phenol compound ( Fig 1A ), and one of the principal dietary constituents derived from Thymus species. It has been utilized for centuries in traditional medicine and exhibits a broad spectrum of pharmacological activities, including antioxidant, free radical scavenging, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antispasmodic, antibacterial, antifungal, antiseptic, and antitumor effects, with minimal toxicity reported in humans [ 9 ]. Studies by Wan [ 10 ] and Yao [ 11 ] have demonstrated the protective effects of thymol against lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced acute lung injury (ALI) in murine models. Furthermore, thymol has been shown to inhibit the activation of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and TGF-β1 in the context of diabetic nephropathy [ 12 ].

The primary therapeutic target in IPF is the aberrant activation of fibroblasts and the subsequent overproduction of collagen [ 6 ]. Multiple signaling pathways and their downstream effectors have been implicated in the persistent activation of fibroblast populations in IPF. Myofibroblasts are primarily derived from resident lung fibroblasts, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), or recruited fibroblast precursors [ 7 ]. Among pro-fibrotic mediators, transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) plays a pivotal role [ 8 ], activating the canonical TGF-β/Smad signaling cascade via TGF-β receptors, and contributing critically to fibroblast activation and ECM deposition. As such, targeting the TGF-β1 signaling axis represents a promising therapeutic strategy for IPF.

Currently, nintedanib and pirfenidone have been approved as antifibrotic agents for the treatment of IPF. Clinical studies suggest these agents can modestly slow the decline of pulmonary function and slightly prolong progression-free survival (PFS); however, their efficacy in significantly improving overall survival (OS) remains limited. Moreover, concerns regarding long-term efficacy and tolerability persist, and patient prognosis remains suboptimal [ 5 ]. Consequently, the development of novel therapeutic strategies for IPF is urgently needed.

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), a member of the interstitial lung disease (ILD) family, is a chronic, progressive fibrotic condition of unknown etiology that predominantly affects the elderly population [ 1 , 2 ]. The hallmark pathological features of IPF include excessive accumulation of myofibroblasts within the lung interstitium and aberrant deposition of extracellular matrix (ECM), ultimately resulting in alveolar architectural destruction, progressive dyspnea, and persistent decline in pulmonary function [ 1 ]. Due to the irreversible nature of pulmonary fibrosis, the median survival following diagnosis is only 3–5 years. With the ongoing global trend of population aging, the individual burden and societal economic costs associated with IPF are expected to escalate [ 3 , 4 ].

To further evaluate the therapeutic effects of thymol, key parameters including survival rate, body weight, and inflammation levels were assessed. Body weight measurements indicated that BLM-treated mice experienced significant weight loss, while no notable differences were observed among the other groups ( Fig 4B ).

These findings indicate that thymol effectively mitigates both inflammatory responses and fibrotic remodeling in BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice. Although the absolute reduction in fibrosis scores is modest, the improvement in alveolar structure and collagen deposition is morphologically evident, and the efficacy is comparable to that of nintedanib.

Treatment with thymol (60 or 120 mg/kg/day) or nintedanib (100 mg/kg) markedly preserved alveolar integrity, reduced inflammatory infiltration within the alveolar spaces, and diminished light blue fibrillar collagen deposition. Quantitatively, the alveolitis scores were 2.63 ± 0.19 in the BLM group, 2.54 ± 0.42 in the thymol 60 mg/kg group, 2.27 ± 0.27 in the thymol 120 mg/kg group, and 2.46 ± 0.42 in the nintedanib group. Correspondingly, the fibrosis scores were 4.31 ± 0.57 in the BLM group, 3.85 ± 0.69 in the thymol 60 mg/kg group, 3.62 ± 0.66 in the thymol 120 mg/kg group, and 3.82 ± 0.83 in the nintedanib group ( Fig 3B – 3D ).

Histological evaluations were conducted to assess the extent of fibrotic lesions. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was used to evaluate inflammatory cell infiltration within the alveolar spaces, whereas Masson’s trichrome staining was employed to assess collagen deposition and fibrosis severity. Compared with the control group, the BLM-treated mice exhibited pronounced destruction of alveolar architecture, extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells, marked interstitial and perivascular blue-stained collagen accumulation, as well as vascular congestion and edema.

At the transcriptional level, TGF-β1 stimulation markedly increased the mRNA expression of α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), collagen I, and fibronectin. Thymol treatment significantly suppressed the transcription of α-SMA and fibronectin, while collagen I expression remained largely unaffected ( Fig 2A – 2C ). Consistently, Western blot analysis further confirmed that thymol reduced TGF-β1-induced protein expression of α-SMA and fibronectin, whereas collagen I protein levels exhibited no significant change ( Fig 2D – 2G ).

To investigate the regulatory effect of thymol on TGF-β1-induced activation of human lung fibroblasts, HFL1 cells were treated with TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL) in combination with various concentrations of thymol (40, 80, or 160 μM). The expression levels of representative fibroblast activation markers were assessed by qRT-PCR and Western blot, with GAPDH serving as the internal control.

To investigate whether thymol affects TGF-β1-induced fibroblast proliferation, HFL1 cells were treated with 10 ng/mL TGF-β1 in the presence of varying concentrations of thymol for 48 hours. Cell proliferation was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Thymol exhibited no significant cytotoxicity on normal lung fibroblasts at concentrations up to 160 μM (87.57 ± 9.00, P > 0.05). However, at 320 μM, thymol significantly inhibited cell viability (67.47 ± 3.90, P < 0.05, Fig 1B ). Consequently, concentrations of 80 and 160 μM were selected for subsequent experiments. Within this range, thymol effectively suppressed TGF-β1-induced fibroblast proliferation ( Fig 1C ).

4. Discussion

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a devastating interstitial lung disease with a poor prognosis. Although antifibrotic agents such as pirfenidone and nintedanib have been approved for clinical use and have demonstrated the ability to delay disease progression to some extent [1], their efficacy in reversing established fibrotic lesions remains limited [14]. For patients in the advanced stages of the disease, lung transplantation continues to be regarded as the only curative option [15], underscoring the urgent need for novel antifibrotic therapeutic strategies.

In recent years, several emerging signaling pathways have been implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of IPF, including TGF-β/Smad, STAT3, and PI3K, which are critically involved in aberrant fibroblast activation and excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition [16,17]. Among these, TGF-β1 is widely recognized as a central mediator of fibrosis. It activates Smad2/3 signaling and promotes their nuclear translocation, thereby regulating the expression of fibrosis-related genes such as those involved in epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and collagen synthesis [18]. Animal studies have also demonstrated that Smad3 deficiency significantly attenuates bleomycin (BLM)-induced pulmonary fibrosis in mice [19,20].

Beyond synthetic small-molecule inhibitors, natural product-derived active monomers have garnered increasing interest in recent years for IPF intervention due to their multi-target regulatory potential and relatively low toxicity profiles [21]. Several studies have reported that natural compounds, such as paeoniflorin from Paeonia suffruticosa, gallic acid (a polyphenol), and flavonoids like baicalein, can effectively mitigate fibrotic pathology by inhibiting activation of the TGF-β1/Smad pathway [22–24]. Similarly, active constituents such as myricetin, rutin, and scutellarein have been shown to suppress excessive ECM deposition and the expression of pro-inflammatory mediators [25,26].

In the present study, thymol treatment significantly suppressed the expression of α-SMA and fibronectin in TGF-β1-induced human lung fibroblasts and attenuated the activation of the NF-κB/TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway. The antifibrotic and anti-inflammatory effects of thymol were further validated in a BLM-induced mouse model. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the regulatory effects of polyphenolic and flavonoid compounds on fibrotic signaling pathways [27]. The expanded transcriptomic analysis provides a broader context for these targeted findings. TGF-β1 altered 2,349 genes under the prespecified differential-expression thresholds, whereas thymol altered 971 genes relative to TGF-β1, and 306 TGF-β1-responsive genes showed significant opposite-direction changes after thymol treatment. Importantly, the reversed genes included several key contractile/myofibroblast markers, such as ACTA2, MYH11, TAGLN, CNN1, and MYLK. However, the transcriptional response was incomplete: COL1A1 and COL3A1 remained relatively elevated, and several matrix-remodeling genes showed only partial or heterogeneous normalization. These transcriptomic findings are consistent with the experimental observation that thymol reduced α-SMA and fibronectin but did not significantly affect Collagen I, and they support a more precise interpretation in which thymol selectively attenuates fibroblast activation rather than uniformly suppressing all components of extracellular matrix production. GO enrichment analysis further showed that TGF-β1-driven genes were associated with muscle contraction, muscle structure development, tissue/organ morphogenesis, and vascular development, whereas genes reduced by thymol were enriched in MAPK regulation, muscle structure development, organ morphogenesis, and cell motility/migration-related processes. This provides transcriptome-level evidence that the effect of thymol extends beyond individual marker genes and involves coordinated remodeling of fibroblast activation programs. At the same time, thymol-upregulated genes were enriched in mitotic and chromosome-segregation processes, indicating that the treatment response is broader and more complex than a simple reversal of the TGF-β1 state. Therefore, the RNA-seq data should be interpreted as evidence of selective transcriptional reprogramming rather than complete normalization of the fibrotic transcriptome.

This observation reveals an association between E2F8 and the antifibrotic effects of thymol, suggesting that E2F8 may serve as a potential upstream regulator in this process, providing a new perspective on its multi‑target mechanisms [28]. Although thymol’s anti‑inflammatory and antifibrotic properties and the TGF‑β/Smad pathway have been reported, this study is the first to reveal the E2F8–NF‑κB–TGF‑β1/Smad axis as an integrated target network. However, the role of the E2F family in pulmonary fibrosis remains insufficiently characterized, and the function of E2F8 was inferred solely from expression data. To firmly establish the causal link between thymol and the E2F8/NF‑κB/TGF‑β1/Smad axis, future studies will perform rescue experiments using TGF‑β1 inhibitors, NF‑κB inhibitors, and E2F8 knockdown/overexpression in vitro.

Previous studies have established that the NF-κB pathway is persistently activated in chronic inflammation and ECM accumulation, and it interacts synergistically with the TGF-β1/Smad axis to amplify fibrotic responses [29]. For example, astragalus polysaccharides have been reported to inhibit pro-inflammatory cytokine release by blocking the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, thereby delaying fibrosis progression [30]. Similarly, vinpocetine alleviates lung tissue injury and fibrosis by downregulating the NLRP3/NF-κB pathway, illustrating the potential of multi-target therapeutic strategies [31]. In this study, the observed reduction in phosphorylated NF-κB levels following thymol treatment aligns with these findings, further supporting the compound’s capacity to modulate multiple signaling pathways. Notably, the reduction in histological fibrosis scores is moderate, which is common in early‑stage intervention studies of BLM‑induced pulmonary fibrosis; combining quantitative collagen detection will further enhance the reliability of the conclusion.

In addition, the current study mainly used semi-quantitative histological scoring to evaluate fibrosis, which is subjective. However, the antifibrotic effect of thymol was consistently supported by multiple lines of evidence in vitro and in vivo, including down-regulated expression of fibrosis-related genes and proteins (α-SMA, fibronectin), reduced TGF-β1 levels in BALF, and suppressed activation of the NF-κB/TGF-β1/Smad signaling pathway. To further strengthen the objectivity of collagen quantification, a quantitative hydroxyproline assay to measure total lung collagen content will be performed in subsequent studies to provide more solid and reliable evidence for thymol’s antifibrotic effect.

It is important to note, however, that although natural monomers are generally considered to have low toxicity and a reduced risk of resistance [32], this study did not include systematic long-term toxicological or pharmacokinetic evaluations. The effective in vitro concentrations of thymol (80 μM and 160 μM) are relatively high, which may limit direct clinical translation; future studies should explore structural modifications or formulation optimization (e.g., liposomes, inhalable preparations) to improve local bioavailability and reduce effective concentrations. The comparability between the in vitro concentrations and the in vivo doses (60/120 mg/kg) also requires further clarification through pharmacokinetic studies. Moreover, the causal relationship between E2F8 and downstream signaling events has not yet been functionally validated, which is a critical area for future investigation.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that thymol attenuates fibroblast activation and pulmonary fibrosis through coordinated modulation of inflammatory, contractile, extracellular-matrix, and TGF-β/NF-κB-associated programs. The expanded transcriptomic analysis indicates that this effect represents a selective and partial transcriptional reprogramming rather than complete reversal of the TGF-β1-induced state. E2F8 remains an interesting candidate for further mechanistic investigation, but the current data do not establish it as a causal upstream mediator. Further studies focusing on direct E2F8 perturbation, quantitative collagen assessment, long-term safety, pharmacokinetics, and dose translation are required to define the therapeutic and mechanistic relevance of thymol in IPF more rigorously.

Proteomic sample preparation

For mass spectrometry (MS) experiments, THP1-UBA1 conditional knockout cells induced with doxycycline were collected after 10 days of induction. In brief, cells were collected by resuspension of settled cells, washed in PBS and centrifuged at 300g for 5 min. The cell pellets were stored at −80 °C or directly used for isolation of protein peptides for MS analysis. Cell pellets were resuspended in ~0.5–1 ml of ice-cold TB buffer (20 mM HEPES (pH 7.3), 110 mM potassium acetate, 2 mM magnesium acetate, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EGTA, 0.1% NP-40 and protease inhibitors (Roche)) and incubated on ice for 30 min. Cells were further lysed by three cycles of high-frequency sonication with 10 s on 50-s off cycles to prevent heating of the sample. Lysates were subsequently cleared by centrifugation at 4 °C at 15,000g for 30 min and filtered through a 0.22-μm filter (Millex-GV) to remove residual lipids. Isolated proteins were precipitated by adding trichloroacetic acid to 20% (v/v) followed by overnight incubation on ice. Precipitated proteins were collected by centrifugation, 10 min at 20,000g. Pelleted proteins were washed three times with ice-cold 90% acetone in 0.01 M HCl and briefly allowed to air-dry. Dried proteins were prepared for mass spectrometry analysis following standard protocol for S-Trap mini columns (Protifi). Tryptic peptides were dried under vacuum and stored at −20 °C until liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) analysis.

DiGly-peptide isolation and enrichment

Diglycine (diGly; K-ε-GG)-peptides were enriched from isolated total proteomic peptides using the PTMScan Ubiquitin Remnant Motif (K-ε-GG) kit (Cell Signaling Technology). In brief, isolated dehydrated tryptic peptides previously purified by reversed-phase, solid-phase extraction (S-Trap), were applied to an immunoaffinity purification column with the PTMScan Motif antibody conjugated to protein A agarose beads. Desalting and isolation of enriched peptides were performed using the provided reversed-phase purification microtips. Subsequent (K-ε-GG)-enriched peptides were concentrated and submitted for MS analysis.

LC–MS/MS analysis

For global proteomics analysis, nano LC–MS/MS analysis of tryptic peptides was carried out with a Thermo Scientific Fusion Lumos tribrid mass spectrometer interfaced to a UltiMate3000 RSLCnano HPLC system. For each analysis, 1 µg of the tryptic digest was loaded and desalted in an Acclaim PepMap 100 trapping column (75 µm × 2 cm) at 4 µl min−1 for 5 min. Peptides were then eluted into an Aurora Ultimate TS 25 cm × 75 μm C18 UHPLC column (Ion Opticks) and chromatographically separated using a binary solvent system consisting of A: 0.1% formic acid and B: 0.1% formic acid and 80% acetonitrile at a flow rate of 300 nl min−1. A gradient was run from 1% B to 32% B over 110 min, followed by a 5-min wash step with 90% B and 14-min equilibration at 1% B before the next sample was injected. Precursor masses were detected in the Orbitrap at R = 120,000 (m/z 200) in profile mode. Fragment masses were detected in the Orbitrap at R = 15,000 (m/z 200) in centroid mode. Data-independent MS/MS was carried out for m/z 400–1,000 with 13-m/z windows. The automatic gain control target was set at 200%, and maximum injection time was 20 ms.

Enriched diGly peptides were analysed with the same instrument and same chromatography method using data-dependent acquisition with ‘top of speed’ settings. Cycle time was 2 s with 60 s dynamic exclusion. Fragment ions were detected in the Orbitrap at R = 30,000 (m/z 200). All samples were analysed in a block randomized order.

Differential enriched gene and pathway analysis

Bulk RNA-seq transcriptomic data were analysed for differential expression with the R package74. Initial steps included filtering haemoglobin-encoding and lowly expressed genes that could skew normalization (minimum of ten counts in at least two samples). DESeq2’s variance stabilization transformation was fit to data and differentially expressed genes were determined on Benjamini–Hochberg-adjusted P value < 0.05 and |log 2 fold change| > 1 thresholds. Genes were preranked by sign(log 2 fold change) × −log 10 (P value) as input for gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)75. Enriched MSigDB gene sets (v.2014.1)76 were determined with fgsea77. edgeR78 functions calcNormFactors and cpm were used to normalize counts by trimmed mean of M-values for intersample comparison. Differentially expressed proteins were determined by an unpaired t-test without additional adjustment, with significance thresholds set as P < 0.05 and |log 2 fold change| > 0.6. Proteins only detected in one condition were omitted from statistical analysis. Pathway enrichment and functional effect analysis were carried out the same as for mRNA.

For scRNA-seq, differential gene expression analysis was performed between cells from patients with VEXAS and from age-matched healthy controls for both CD14 monocytes and HSPCs. For the CD14 monocyte analysis, donors with <500 CD14+ monocytes were excluded. Each donor was then randomly downsampled without replacement to retain 500 CD14+ monocytes per donor (n = 4 healthy control donors, n = 7 VEXAS donors)14,18 (retaining n = 2,000 healthy control cells and n = 3,500 VEXAS cells for analysis). For the HSC analysis, all donors were retained due to lower cell counts (n = 4 healthy control donors, n = 10 VEXAS donors). Donors with >200 HSCs were downsampled to 200 cells for analysis, without replacement, retaining n = 794 healthy control cells and n = 1,733 VEXAS cells for analysis. Mitochondrial and ribosomal genes were removed from the gene count matrix before analysis, and then log normalization was run with default parameters for Seurat’s NormalizeData function (scale.factor 10,000, normalization.method = ‘LogNormalize’, margin = 1). Genes expressed in <20% of cells tested were excluded from analysis. Differential expression analysis was performed using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. GSEA was performed using the GOBP ERAD pathway, which is publicly available in MSigDB and was obtained via the msigdbr package (v.7.5.1). The fgsea package (v.1.24.0) was then used to perform ranked gene set enrichment analysis. Genes were ranked by effect size (avg_log 2 FC).

Gene module score analysis

HSCs and CD14 monocytes from VEXAS and healthy control donors were scored for enrichment of the GOBP ERAD, UBIQUITIN-DEPENDENT ERAD and REGULATION OF ERAD gene modules using the AddModuleScore function from Seurat within scRNA-seq data. First, mitochondrial and ribosomal genes were removed and then, log normalization was run as described above. Then, the function infers the gene set activity by calculating the average expression levels of each gene set on a single-cell level, subtracted by the aggregated expression of control feature sets. All analysed features are binned based on averaged expression and the control features are randomly selected from each bin. Default parameters were used. Distributions of module scores were compared between VEXAS and control cells and a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to determine significance.

Construction of a THP1 VEXAS model cell line

To generate an inducible UBA1−/− cell line, human THP1 cells were first infected with lentivirus packaged with FUCas9Cherry (Addgene, 70182) to constitutively express Cas9 and mCherry. Next, ten UBA1-targeting guide sequences were generated using CHOPCHOP software79. The doxycycline-inducible FgH1tUTG (Addgene, 70183) plasmid was digested with BsmBI-V2 (NEB, R0739S), DNA gel electrophoresis purified, and T4 DNA ligase (NEB, M0202S) ligated with the annealed product of a forward (5′-TCCCTAACCAGGACAACCCCGGTG-3′) and reverse (5′-AAACCACCGGGGTTGTCCTGGTTA-3′) oligonucleotides containing BsmBI overhangs (underlined) and the UBA1-targeting sequence for the guide RNA (gRNA). The noted UBA1 target sequence was the most efficient in inducing KO of UBA1. This new plasmid, FgH1tUTG-UBA1, was then lentivirally packaged and infected into the Cas9-expressing THP1. These cells were then sorted using FACS on a Sony SH800 Cell Sorter with a 130-μm sorting chip, selecting for dual-positive mCherry (FUCas9Cherry) and GFP (FgH1tUTG-UBA1) THP1 populations. Double-positive cells were then induced with 2 μg ml−1 doxycycline with UBA1−/− validated by loss of endogenous UBA1a/b on immunoblot and eventual cell death.

Next, pHAGE_puro (Addgene, 118692) was modified to contain an IRES-regulating puromycin expression and encode either a modified UBA1WT (pHAGE-UBA1_WT-IRES-Puro) or UBA1M41V (pHAGE-UBA1_M41V-IRES-Puro) complementary DNA product with a Flag–HA tag. To avoid Cas9-mediated KO of the UBA1 transgene, the cDNA regions corresponding to the gRNA target sequence were modified to contain synonymous mutations in the codons corresponding to amino acids 213–222 (5′-GTC ACG AAA GAT AAT CCT GGA GTT GTG ACT-3′). These constructs were then packaged into lentivirus, transduced into the dox-inducible UBA1−/− THP1 cell line, and selected for 7 days in 1 μg ml−1 puromycin. After selection, trans-UBA1 expression was validated with immunoblot against an HA tag. In addition to generating UBA1M41V (c.121A>G) we also generated UBA1M41L (c.121A>T) (pHAGE-UBA1_M41L-IRES-Puro) as an additional control for loss of UBA1b and VEXAS-related studies.

Finally, 3 × 106 control UBA1WT or UBA1M41V-expressing inducible UBA1−/− THP1 cells were plated in a 10-cm diameter tissue culture dish with 15 ml of medium containing 2 μg ml−1 doxycycline and grown for 6 days. For monocyte experimentation, on day 6, cells were then split 1:3 into new medium containing 2 μg ml−1 doxycycline. The medium was changed every day by aspirating half the volume and replacing with new medium before being collected on day 9. For macrophage experimentation, induced cells were counted, spun at 300g for 10 min and the supernatant was removed. Cell pellets were then resuspended in new medium containing 2 μg ml−1 doxycycline and 20 nM PMA at a concentration of 1 × 106 cells per ml. These were then plated on tissue culture-treated six-well plates. Adherence and macrophage differentiation were observed by day 8 and cultures were collected on day 9.

Quantitative PCR of cytokines and interferon score calculation

Quantitative PCR was performed using TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, 4304437) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System. Transmission electron microscopy

Cells for TEM were prepared using the following protocol. Segmentation of volume electron microscopy dataset

Subcellular structures in the volume electron microscopy dataset were segmented using a U-Net82 model implemented with the segmentation_models (https://github.com/qubvel/segmentation_models) library based on Keras and TensorFlow. The U-Net architecture used a ResNet34 encoder pretrained on ImageNet to leverage feature representations. Ground truth annotations were manually generated using the Labkit plugin in ImageJ and 20% of the dataset was used for training and validation. The greyscale electron microscopy images were normalized and divided into non-overlapping 256 × 256 pixel patches using the patchify (https://github.com/dovahcrow/patchify.py) library for training. To enhance model generalization, data augmentation (geometric transformation: rotation, zoom, shear and flipping) was applied using Keras ImageDataGenerator. The U-Net model was compiled using the Adam optimizer and binary cross-entropy as loss function and trained for 100 epochs with early stopping and model checkpoint callbacks to prevent overfitting. Model performance was monitored using the Intersection over union score as the primary evaluation metric. Two separate models were trained for the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondria, respectively. During inference, output probability maps were thresholded at 0.5 to generate binary segmentations. Segmentation artifacts were manually inspected and corrected in ImageJ. Final binary masks were imported into Imaris (Oxford Instruments, v.9.9) for three-dimensional volume reconstruction and quantitative analysis.

Immunofluorescence of STING localization

Live, doxycycline-induced UBA1WT or UBA1M41V THP1 model monocytes were differentiated into adherent macrophages on poly-L-lysine coverslips. On day 9, coverslips were gently washed with PBS before fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Following three PBS washes, coverslips were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (MilliporeSigma) in PBS for 10 min at room temperature and washed three times with PBS. To quench the GFP or RFP reporter signal in model macrophages, the slides were incubated with cold 1% w/v sodium borohydride (MilliporeSigma) for 10 min. Coverslips were then visualized for GFP or RFP signal with the quenching step repeated until no fluorescence was appreciable. Coverslips were then incubated in blocking buffer (5% normal goat serum + 1% BSA in PBS) for 60 min at room temperature. Coverslips were then incubated with both mouse anti-GM130 (1:200 dilution; BD Bioscience, 610823) and rabbit anti-STING (1:200 dilution; Cell Signaling Technology, 90947S) overnight at 4 °C. Following three washes with PBS + 0.05% Tween-20, coverslips were then incubated with goat anti-mouse Cy3 (1:500 dilution; Jackson ImmunoResearch, 115-165-164) and goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:500 dilution; Jackson ImmunoResearch, 111-545-144) secondary antibodies or 60 min at room temperature. Coverslips were then washed and briefly stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) before mounting on slides using ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Fisher, P36930).

Slides were imaged using a modified Nikon Eclipse Ti widefield microscope using an Olympus ×60 objective. For each condition, 12 hyperstacks were acquired, at 200 μm × 200 μm × 6.25 μm with 27 z-steps and a step size of 0.25 μm. Z-stacks were centred on the z-position where nuclei appeared uniformly in focus. Three channels were used, with excitation wavelengths at 405 nm (DAPI), 488 nm (STING) and 568 nm (GM130). Laser intensity and exposure time for each channel were standardized across all images. Analysis was performed by creating maximum intensity projections of DAPI, STING and GM130 channels, and segmenting nuclei, the cytoplasm and Golgi, respectively using the Cellpose 4.0 SAM model. Diameters for segmentation were set as 150 px for nuclei, 300 px for cytoplasm and 50 px for Golgi, with all other parameters left as the default setting. Masks were exported as .tif files and imported into a Python script for further analysis. Segmentation accuracy was checked by eye after processing to confirm the parameters used were optimal. Additionally, each hyperstack was split into individual channel stacks and imported into the same Python script. Mask files were used to assemble cell regions together. Only valid cells (cytoplasmic masks fully encompassing exactly one nucleus mask and one Golgi mask) were used to record signal intensity. Signal intensities were calculated by summing all pixels found in regions defined by masks. The STING enrichment metric was calculated by taking the intensity recorded in the Golgi mask and dividing it by the intensity recorded in the cytoplasmic mask and multiplying by 100 to generate a percentage of STING signal found within the Golgi for each valid cell. The resulting tables for each condition were exported as .csv files from the Python script for plotting and statistical analysis.

Samples of each THP1 UBA1WT and UBA1M41V macrophages were resuspended with SDS lysis buffer and incubated at 95 °C for 15 min to yield whole-cell extract. Samples of THP1 UBA1WT and UBA1M41V were gently resuspended in digitonin lysis buffer, lysed for 9 min at 4 °C and centrifuged at 950g for 5 min at 4 °C. The resultant supernatant was used to collect the cytosolic fraction. The remaining pellet was resuspended in 500 μl of NP-40 lysis buffer, incubated on ice for 10 min and then spun at ~21,000g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resultant supernatant was used to collect the mitochondrial fraction. DNA purification from the isolated aqueous whole-cell extract, cytosolic and mitochondrial fractions was conducted using phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol precipitation followed by an ethanol wash and resuspension of the DNA pellet in nuclease-free water. Quantification of mtDNA in the cellular fractions was performed using TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, 4304437) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System per the manufacturer’s directions. A probe targeting KCNJ10 (Applied Biosystems, Hs01922935_s1) was used as a nuclear DNA control in conjunction with two mtDNA probes targeting MTDN1 (Applied Biosystems, Hs02596873_s1) and Mt7s D-Loop (Applied Biosystems, Hs02596861_s1).

Xenotransplantation of NSG-S mice with base-edited HSPCs

For each mouse, 1.0 × 106 base-edited HSPCs were injected retro-orbitally 24 h after editing into sublethally irradiated (a single dose of 250 cGy 3 h before transplant) NSG-S mice. Mice were all male, at 4–8 weeks of age and randomly distributed to each experimental group. Non-injected HSPCs were set aside for ddPCR to calculate the UBA1 and STING variant allele frequency at week 0. Human CD45+ cell engraftment was monitored by serial collection of blood at week 4 and at the end of the experiment 7 weeks after transplantation by flow cytometry using anti-human CD45 (BioLegend, PE/Cy7 conjugated, no. 304016, clone HI30). At the end of the experiment, bone-marrow cells were extracted and purified by AutoMACs using human CD45 microbeads (Miltenyi). These cells were analysed by ddPCR to assess end point variant allele frequencies. Mice were housed in specific-pathogen-free colony rooms under the Division of Comparative Medicine’s health surveillance and were fed with LabDiet (Purina Mills). No animals or data points were excluded from the analyses for any reason.
