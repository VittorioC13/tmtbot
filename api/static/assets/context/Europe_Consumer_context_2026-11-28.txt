[TEXT]
LONDON — Long before wellness became a $6.3 trillion juggernaut, growing at twice the pace of traditional luxury goods, Iris Ventures founder Montse Suarez was already making the connection between consumers’ pursuit of the inside and outside feel good factor.

A eureka moment came when the former Morgan Stanley banker was setting up Walgreens Boots Alliance’s corporate venture fund nearly 15 years ago. It didn’t take her long to realize that women going into Boots were not thinking in terms of product categories.

Instead, “they were looking for benefits, thinking, ‘I want to feel glamorous, I want to feel good, I want to feel empowered or I want to to solve a certain problem,'” Suarez said in a wide-ranging interview, adding that it was clear to her at the time those women were looking to achieve an overall sense of well-being.

Suarez would later cofound Vaultier7, an early backer of the baby food company Little Spoon; honey-based hair care expert Gisou, and 111Skin, the skin care brand born from the London Harley Street clinic.

Olistic is a drinkable supplement designed to support hair health and reduce thinning.

She launched Iris in 2021 around her original idea that people were looking to feel good on the inside and the outside, and that they wanted to live healthier lives for longer. The pandemic, when people were working from home and spending more time cooking, exercising and trying to improve their quality of life, intensified that desire for overall well-being.

Iris invests in what Suarez describes as “culturally relevant” brands that help people “live longer, and feel healthier and happier,” with as little fuss as possible. Investments range from home interiors companies to food and nutrition ones, to those targeting gut and hormonal health, skin and hair care.

It takes stakes at the early growth stage, typically Series A, but can sometimes swoop in at seed, or Series B level. It is on track to close Fund II, a 200 million-euro vehicle, in the first quarter of 2027 and, in doing so, will roughly double its debut fund.

Its checks are now running 10 million euros to 20 million euros per investment, with backers including Puig, and some of Europe’s leading private family offices. Each fund has a 10-year life, with Iris looking to help build a brand’s value over a four- to six-year period.

No Time Like the Present

Suarez believes the global wellness economy is brimming with opportunity due to growing interest in high-end services, specialist skin care and hair care, pharmacy-grade formulations and the pharmacy as a retail channel.

She added that macro factors such as the pandemic, the rise of GLP-1 drugs and the interest in longterm quality of life have only accelerated consumers’ needs for the brands that Iris backs. There are currently 16 businesses in the portfolio, with one more set to be revealed before the end of the year.

Iris has also invested in the plant-based, probiotic drinks brand Biomel; Reformed, which blends coffee or ceremonial-grade matcha with vitamins and minerals, and Lucille, a healthy, high-protein, calorically dense shake aimed at older people, and which anyone can consume as a snack or light meal substitute.

Room for Disruption and Expansion

Iris is always on the hunt for new opportunities.

A year ago, Iris led a $15 million investment into Innerskin, a French company specializing in noninvasive skin care treatments at its clinics. The brand also has a medical-grade product line with signature formulas that are able to mimic hands-on skin care treatments, such as microneedling.

Iris invested for a number of reasons, including Innerskin’s potential to scale further. Innerskin opened its first clinic outside France, in London’s Notting Hill, and the plan is to continue rolling out more sites, potentially in well-heeled neighborhoods such as Chelsea and Marylebone.

[TEXT]
Hours after Bangladesh’s minimum wage board raised the monthly pay threshold for garment workers by 36 percent to 17,000 Bangladeshi taka ($136)—the sector’s smallest percentage hike in four decades—the mood in the world’s second-largest apparel exporter after China would best be described as mixed.

It’s difficult to predict how the workers who have poured into the streets en masse over the past month will react once the news has had a chance to sink in; as of Wednesday, 87 garment factories in Ashulia, Savar and Gazipur remained shuttered as protesters continued their demands for a 30,000-32,000 taka ($239-$255) wage that can keep pace with the increasing cost of rent, food and transportation in the face of stubborn inflation.

The six-member board, which includes a chairman, a neutral member and two representatives each for employers and workers, made its decision in a 4-2 vote less than a month after it formed in response to the brewing unrest. Speaking to reporters after 12 hours of deliberation, Babul Akhter, a labor representative on the board, urged workers to return to their factories, adding, “Although we are not completely satisfied, the outcome is not entirely bad either. Others were less conciliatory. Nazma Akter, founder and president of the Sommilito Garments Sramik Federation, a union with more than 70,000 members, summed up her feelings as dissatisfaction. Workers are becoming “poorer and poorer” under the current pay structure, she said, adding that the continuation of a “not respectful” wage only shows that the broader system under which Bangladesh’s garment industry operates needs to change. The government, she said, needs to step up to help stabilize the prices of goods and services and provide better social protections.

Citing industry calculations that put direct labor in Bangladesh at roughly 42 cents per garment, compared with roughly 31 cents in Vietnam, 36 cents in Pakistan and 38 cents in India, he said that employers will be hard-pressed to shoulder the higher minimum wage unless buyers share the burden by “paying the right prices.”

“There’s got to be a complete shift, otherwise it’ll be very challenging for the industry as a whole to survive out of this,” Samad said. “Maybe a handful of factories will survive who are financially strong, but the small-and-medium-enterprise factories, which are exporting less than $5 million, will be under tremendous pressure to absorb this. Samad’s appeal to brands was echoed by Mohiuddin Rubel, founder and CEO of Bangladesh Apparel Voice and additional managing director at Denim Expert in Chittagong, who said buyers, as “our partners and the biggest stakeholders,” have a responsibility for the higher wage, too.

Anne Bienias, living wage coordinator at the Clean Clothes Campaign, the garment industry’s largest consortium of trade unions and civil society organizations, also criticized brands, but for a slightly different reason: They haven’t done enough to push for a higher wage.

“It’s disappointing to see, time and time again, how brands fail to act during critical moments like minimum wage negotiations,” she said. “Though they can increase their purchasing prices at any given moment to increase wages of garment workers, speaking out in support of workers’ demands during minimum wage negotiations is nevertheless important, because a meaningful minimum wage increase positively affects 4.4 million workers at once.”

“Although 17,000 taka is far below what workers and trade unions are protesting for, it is a big jump from the current 12,500 taka minimum,” Islam said. “The main challenge is implementing it more effectively so workers don’t lose their jobs, as factory owners tend to save costs while brands and retailers continue to squeeze prices. The Bangladesh government needs to enforce this strictly.”

Rubel, meanwhile, had another worry: the industry’s competitiveness. Low cost, he said, was the country’s “main edge”; now it has to find another to “stay in the market,” especially since the reputational damage he thinks the unrest has dealt the industry will be harder to shake off than the financial blow. There’s also a difference, he said, between raising the statutory minimum wage and meaningfully improving workers’ purchasing power and living standards.

“Bangladesh cannot indefinitely rely on low labor costs as a primary source of competitive advantage,” Rana said. “The industry needs to accelerate its transition toward higher-value, higher-efficiency manufacturing through technological investment, workforce development, product diversification and operational excellence.”

If all proceeds as expected, the new minimum wage will take effect on December 1, with workers receiving the revised figure starting in January.

[TITLE]Ordered silver coins, received rakhis: 5 online shopping lessons from Delhi Consumer Commission’s ₹20,000 order:
[TEXT]
Online shopping offers convenience and attractive deals, but receiving the wrong product can quickly turn a routine purchase into a long consumer dispute.

A recent Delhi consumer commission order shows why e-commerce platforms must be transparent and handle delivery complaints properly. It also shows why customers should preserve evidence when an order goes wrong and check what they receive right away.

What are the details of this case? A Delhi consumer commission has directed Amazon Seller Services Pvt Ltd to pay ₹20,000 in compensation to Akshay Mittal, who was delivered two packets of rakhis instead of the four silver coins he had ordered for ₹5,996, according to a Times of India report.

Also Read | Is cash received as loan repayment taxable? ITAT clarifies

According to the report, the aggrieved customer, Mittal, placed the order on November 10, 2023, for four 50-gram silver coins with 999 purity. Later on, he was delivered the wrong product on November 12. Subsequently, he filed a complaint with Amazon, and the platform refunded ₹5,996 on November 26 instead of arranging an appropriate replacement.

After reviewing the facts of the case, the District Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission, South II, Delhi, stated that Mittal had never applied for or requested a refund, and that Amazon should have replaced the original products. Due to this, the commission held the platform guilty of ‘deficiency in services’ and ‘unfair trade practices’, directing it to pay ₹20,000 within 60 days. Furthermore, any delay in payments will attract 9% annual interest.

With this case in mind, let us look at important lessons for online shoppers so they can protect their finances and take appropriate steps when faced with such circumstances.

5 key lessons for online shoppers

1. Check products immediately after delivery If you have ordered an expensive product such as silver coins, high-end mobile sets, televisions, etc., make sure you verify the product, quality, quantity, and other specifications to confirm the accuracy of the product before providing the One-Time Password (OTP) to complete the delivery transaction.

2. Preserve delivery evidence Take photos of the package, speak to the delivery partner if you have any doubts, save the shipping label, and any incorrect product details for future reference. Keep the original bill, invoice and order confirmation details handy as well.

3. Report discrepancies promptly If you find any issues or discrepancies with the supplied product, first raise them with the delivery partner, then, in line with the terms, through the legitimate platforms. For this, you will need original images of the product, the invoice, the order date, and other details.

4. Keep complaint records As soon as you raise the issue, save official emails, communication trails, text messages, WhatsApp messages, transcripts, complaint reference numbers and refund or replacement confirmation details with you. These can support your case if the dispute escalates.

5. Know your consumer rights Every seller on prominent e-commerce platforms such as Amazon has a grievance redressal method. If the seller or the e-commerce platform completely fails to help you resolve your problem in a timely manner, then you can approach the legitimate consumer courts for redressal of your grievances.

What are the key takeaways for this case?

A refund does not mean a grievance has been resolved. Customers should document delivery problems and state clearly what remedy they want, whether a refund or a replacement. E-commerce platforms, for their part, must ensure that their delivery and grievance systems meet consumer protection standards.

[TITLE]More than 4,400 malicious domains are waiting for holiday shoppers:
[TEXT]
Cybercriminals are positioned to exploit the holiday shopping season through fraudulent storefronts, retailer impersonation, payment fraud and account takeover attacks, according to FortiGuard Labs’ Holiday Season Cyber Threats Report 2026.

Researchers identified more than 24,000 newly registered domains containing keywords associated with seasonal shopping over a 90-day period, including more than 4,400 classified as malicious. Underground marketplaces are facilitating the trade in stolen customer information, access to e-commerce infrastructure and services that support financial fraud.

Distribution of holiday-related themes across the newly-registered domain dataset (Source: FortiGuard Labs)

Delivery scams and retailer impersonation

Parcel, delivery and shipping terminology accounted for 34.88% of newly registered holiday-related domains, making it the most prevalent theme in the dataset. Other registrations incorporated references to discounts, rewards, gift cards and retail brands.

Attackers can use imitation delivery websites to request payment information under the pretext of missed deliveries, customs fees or delivery rescheduling. Domains referencing DHL, UPS, FedEx and USPS were identified during the investigation.

Researchers identified a fraudulent website impersonating Walmart and advertising Black Friday deals. The storefront reproduced the retailer’s branding, color scheme and product listings to create the appearance of a legitimate shopping destination.

Consumers directed to such websites through advertisements, search results or promotional emails could be tricked into disclosing login credentials, submitting payment information or purchasing products that never arrive.

Fraudulent storefronts do not always rely on impersonating retailers in domain names. Separate research from Doppel found that Black Friday scams began appearing earlier this year, with most confirmed fake pages using web addresses that do not resemble those of the impersonated brands. Approximately one in four lookalike pages were hosted on legitimate platforms such as Google Sites, Blogger and GitHub Pages.

More than 1.7 million retail-related infostealer logs circulate underground

Between August and October 2026, researchers collected approximately 1.72 million infostealer log entries associated with seven major e-commerce platforms from a Russian marketplace. Amazon accounted for approximately 1.49 million entries, followed by eBay with 82,900 and AliExpress with 66,410.

Infostealer malware extracts browser-stored credentials, session cookies, autofill data and other sensitive information from infected devices. Criminals categorize these records by domain, geography and account type, allowing buyers to locate information associated with specific retailers.

“Stolen session cookies may potentially be reused to hijack authenticated sessions, depending on their validity and the security controls implemented by the affected platforms,” the researchers explained.

Stolen credentials and session data could enable unauthorized account access, fraudulent purchases and misuse of stored payment information.

Cybercrime services facilitate retail fraud

Underground forums contain advertisements for subscription-based phishing services, automated payment card validation tools and phishing kits advertised as capable of bypassing MFA.

Criminals are advertising banking credentials, compromised gift card information and access to merchant systems. Fraudulently obtained gift cards can be resold or converted into cash or cryptocurrency.

Researchers also identified an advertised refund-as-a-service operation targeting retailers, food delivery companies, electronics vendors and travel platforms. The operator claims to secure refunds on purchases in exchange for a commission. Such services could exploit return policies and customer support processes.

Communications infrastructure is another component of the underground economy. Listings advertise caller ID spoofing, SMS delivery and bulk email distribution services that could support impersonation campaigns.

Sellers also advertise ready-made social media accounts, advertising campaign management and artificial engagement services. Purchased followers, comments and views could make fraudulent social media profiles and deceptive product advertisements appear more credible.

E-commerce vulnerabilities put retailers at risk

Cybercrime forums contain advertisements for alleged zero-day vulnerabilities, exploit chains and unauthorized access to e-commerce platforms such as Magento, PrestaShop, WordPress and OpenCart.

Access to compromised retail infrastructure could allow attackers to modify checkout pages, deploy payment-skimming scripts, redirect shoppers to fraudulent payment pages and extract customer databases.

Documented exploitation of vulnerabilities affecting Adobe Commerce (Magento) and other web application environments adds to the risks facing online businesses. Security flaws involving remote code execution, unrestricted file uploads and authorization bypass can leave merchant systems vulnerable to compromise.

AI tools enter the holiday fraud ecosystem

Cybercriminals are exploring the use of AI to automate parts of their operations, develop malicious software and produce social engineering content tailored to specific audiences.

One forum user sought an unrestricted LLM capable of generating Romanian-language communications that reflect local financial practices. Such capabilities could make fraudulent messages more convincing to recipients.

Another user described developing an orchestration framework that reportedly coordinates multiple AI agents. The developer claims the framework can circumvent model safety restrictions. Such systems could reduce the manual effort involved in reconnaissance and attack development.

AI-generated videos present another potential avenue for deception. Commercial media generation platforms could be misused to produce fabricated customer testimonials, misleading product demonstrations and advertisements impersonating established brands.
