[TITLE]Microsoft outage shows that big tech can’t be treated as too big to fail:
[TEXT]
When a massive outage hit Microsoft late last week, much of the digital world ground to a halt.

The outage wiped out key Microsoft services including Outlook, Teams and Copilot, which are used by more than 430 million people around the world. As email and internal communications froze, the operations of governments and businesses went dark alongside them, triggering an economic paralysis that could cost billions of dollars.

While the global economy absorbs the brunt of this paralysis, Microsoft faces both a severe reputational hit and contractual penalties that could reach up to US$800 million per day for services they failed to deliver.

Such a catastrophic outage shows just how precarious our modern digital infrastructure is. It depends on a handful of big tech companies, which have essentially become too big to fail. So how did we end up in this situation? And is there a way out?

Digital dependency

The initial source of Microsoft’s failure was incredibly innocuous. Data centres usually share the load of user demands across systems all over the world. However, last week, a small configuration change caused the company to lose control of some of their computers.

Unable to share the load, these computers dropped user requests onto other systems, overwhelming them and producing more outages. This pattern repeated over and over again until the company lost almost complete control of their network.

Worse, disconnected user devices – phones, email clients and business systems – all automatically tried to reconnect at once, crashing Microsoft’s systems all over again.

Historically, a tech outage might affect a single company. But the reason Microsoft’s outage could impact millions of people comes down to a business revolution that silently took place over 20 years ago.

Software was once something that was sold to companies once for an upfront cost. In this model, purchasers held great power – only buying new versions if the update justified the expense.

Yet, these upfront expenses also scared consumers off. Drawing inspiration from telecommunications, gyms and real estate leasing, the tech industry transitioned to renting access to their products, creating what is now known as “software as a service”.

In theory, everyone profited from this model. Software vendors could bank more reliable cash flow, and customers could transition to regular smaller payments, rather than stumping up for large upfront costs.

Ultimately, this change shifted the balance of power towards tech companies. Instead of purchasing features they needed, consumers handed the keys to their operational kingdoms to the “software as service” providers, and locked themselves into highly expensive long-term contracts in the process.

Too big to fail

The global financial crisis in 2008 introduced the world to the idea of too big to fail. When it hit, the public was left to pick up the pieces of banks that had become too systemically important to be allowed to fail.

Unfortunately, it appears we haven’t really learned those lessons. Instead, we’ve spent the last few years living through a slow-motion version of that with the tech industry. Centralisation, consolidation and unchecked monopolies have left our digital lives, our businesses and our governments in an incredibly precarious place.

On one level or another, almost everything on the internet goes through Google, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon Web Services or CrowdStrike – all of which have had major outages over recent years.

These big tech giants earned our trust by providing unified, seemingly invincible infrastructure. But the convenience that comes with centralisation holds a trap.

Unlike the banking sector in 2008, when these tech titans fail, they don’t need to ask governments to bail them out. Instead, their centrality forces the entire digital economy to bear the costs of their outage.

Developing digital sovereignty

Escaping the reliance on a few tech giants for our national infrastructure, businesses and citizens’ data has spurned the push for so-called digital sovereignty.

Europe is leading the way on this. By actively funding decentralised cloud infrastructure and homegrown software, governments are ensuring that an American tech failure cannot knock out a local hospital.

The world must adopt a similar blueprint. Australia, in particular, is well placed to grasp the opportunities that digital sovereignty brings. We already have the raw engineering talent. Companies such as Atlassian, Apromore, Airwallex and Canva have proven we can build world-class digital tools.

If governments strategically invested in local tech ecosystems, they wouldn’t just be buying digital insurance. They would drive much-needed market competition, dilute the power of big-tech monopolies and spread out systemic risks – ensuring that a single point of failure could never bring the economy to its knees.

This is not to say we should divest ourselves of any connection to big-tech platforms, which do and will continue to provide useful services. But if our entire global economy continues to rely on a handful of too-big-to-fail digital monopolies, we will have no one but ourselves to blame when our screens inevitably go black again.

[TITLE]From gaming chips to AI dominance: How Nvidia became a tech powerhouse:
[TEXT]
Nvidia has emerged as one of the biggest beneficiaries of the artificial intelligence (AI) boom, becoming a key supplier of the chips that power some of the world's most advanced AI systems. Once known mainly for making graphics chips for video games, the US-based company has transformed itself into a major force in data centres, AI computing and advanced technology.

Its rise has been driven by years of investment in chip technology, software development and a shift towards AI computing. However, its growing importance has also brought it into the middle of the technology rivalry between the United States and China, with restrictions on the sale of its advanced chips threatening its access to one of the world's largest markets.

However, the chipmaker is now facing growing scrutiny in the US following controversy surrounding its founder Jensen Huang , its China business and allegations involving a suspected Chinese spy.

Here is a look at how Nvidia started, what it does and why it has become so valuable.

How Nvidia started: From gaming to computing

Nvidia was founded on April 5, 1993, by Jensen Huang, Chris Malachowsky and Curtis Priem. According to the company's corporate timeline, the founders wanted to develop technology that could improve 3D graphics in video games and multimedia applications.

The company initially focused on graphics processing units (GPUs), chips designed to handle the complex calculations needed to produce images and videos on computer screens.

In 1999, Nvidia introduced the GPU, helping transform computer graphics and the gaming industry. Its GeForce range later became widely used in gaming computers.

However, the technology had applications beyond video games. Unlike central processing units (CPUs), which generally handle tasks one after another, GPUs can perform many calculations simultaneously. This makes them useful for processing large volumes of data.

ALSO READ: Meet Colette Kress, Nvidia's interim CEO after Jensen Huang's exit Nvidia began exploring these applications well before the current AI boom. In 2006, it introduced CUDA, a software platform that allowed developers and researchers to use GPUs for scientific research and other computing tasks. This proved important in preparing the company for the growth of AI.

What does Nvidia do?

Nvidia designs GPUs, computer chips and related software and systems used for high-performance computing. It does not manufacture most of its chips itself. Instead, it relies on semiconductor manufacturing partners to produce the designs.

Its products serve several industries, including gaming, automotive, healthcare, robotics and scientific research. However, data centres have become its biggest growth engine.

AI systems require enormous computing power to process information, recognise patterns and generate responses. Nvidia's GPUs are designed to handle these workloads, making them useful for both training AI models and running them after they have been developed.

ALSO READ: Nvidia's next chapter: What its new leaders told staff after Huang's exit The company also provides networking equipment, software and complete computing systems that help businesses build AI infrastructure.

In effect, Nvidia is no longer just a chip supplier. It provides much of the technology needed to build and operate large-scale AI systems.

How the AI boom made Nvidia a giant

The launch of OpenAI's ChatGPT in November 2022 brought generative AI into the mainstream and triggered a sharp rise in demand for computing infrastructure.

Technology companies began investing heavily in data centres and AI systems to develop and operate their own models. This created enormous demand for powerful GPUs, a market in which Nvidia already had a strong position.

According to Bloomberg, Nvidia's share price rose around 14-fold from the launch of ChatGPT to its peak on October 6, 2026.

Major technology companies, including Microsoft, Amazon and Alphabet, have emerged as important customers as they expand their AI infrastructure. The scale of this demand has helped Nvidia grow rapidly and become one of the most valuable companies in the world.

What separates Nvidia from its competitors?

Nvidia's advantage is not limited to the performance of its chips. Its combination of hardware, software and computing infrastructure has made it difficult for competitors to match its position.

One of its biggest advantages is CUDA. Over the years, technology companies have built applications around this platform. This has created an established ecosystem of developers, software and tools that competitors must overcome to attract customers.

Nvidia has also maintained its lead by regularly introducing new chip designs and investing in its computing infrastructure.

Nvidia accounts for more than 80 per cent of the market for high-end chips used in AI and data centres, based on some estimates.

[TITLE]Microsoft outage shows that big tech can’t be treated as too big to fail:
[TEXT]
When a massive outage hit Microsoft late last week, much of the digital world ground to a halt.

The outage wiped out key Microsoft services including Outlook, Teams and Copilot, which are used by more than 430 million people around the world. As email and internal communications froze, the operations of governments and businesses went dark alongside them, triggering an economic paralysis that could cost billions of dollars.

While the global economy absorbs the brunt of this paralysis, Microsoft faces both a severe reputational hit and contractual penalties that could reach up to US$800 million per day for services they failed to deliver.

Such a catastrophic outage shows just how precarious our modern digital infrastructure is. It depends on a handful of big tech companies, which have essentially become too big to fail. So how did we end up in this situation? And is there a way out?

Digital dependency

The initial source of Microsoft’s failure was incredibly innocuous. Data centres usually share the load of user demands across systems all over the world. However, last week, a small configuration change caused the company to lose control of some of their computers.

Unable to share the load, these computers dropped user requests onto other systems, overwhelming them and producing more outages. This pattern repeated over and over again until the company lost almost complete control of their network.

Worse, disconnected user devices – phones, email clients and business systems – all automatically tried to reconnect at once, crashing Microsoft’s systems all over again.

Historically, a tech outage might affect a single company. But the reason Microsoft’s outage could impact millions of people comes down to a business revolution that silently took place over 20 years ago.

Software was once something that was sold to companies once for an upfront cost. In this model, purchasers held great power – only buying new versions if the update justified the expense.

Yet, these upfront expenses also scared consumers off. Drawing inspiration from telecommunications, gyms and real estate leasing, the tech industry transitioned to renting access to their products, creating what is now known as “software as a service”.

In theory, everyone profited from this model. Software vendors could bank more reliable cash flow, and customers could transition to regular smaller payments, rather than stumping up for large upfront costs.

Ultimately, this change shifted the balance of power towards tech companies. Instead of purchasing features they needed, consumers handed the keys to their operational kingdoms to the “software as service” providers, and locked themselves into highly expensive long-term contracts in the process.

Too big to fail

The global financial crisis in 2008 introduced the world to the idea of too big to fail. When it hit, the public was left to pick up the pieces of banks that had become too systemically important to be allowed to fail.

Unfortunately, it appears we haven’t really learned those lessons. Instead, we’ve spent the last few years living through a slow-motion version of that with the tech industry. Centralisation, consolidation and unchecked monopolies have left our digital lives, our businesses and our governments in an incredibly precarious place.

On one level or another, almost everything on the internet goes through Google, Microsoft, Meta, Amazon Web Services or CrowdStrike – all of which have had major outages over recent years.

These big tech giants earned our trust by providing unified, seemingly invincible infrastructure. But the convenience that comes with centralisation holds a trap.

Unlike the banking sector in 2008, when these tech titans fail, they don’t need to ask governments to bail them out. Instead, their centrality forces the entire digital economy to bear the costs of their outage.

Developing digital sovereignty

Escaping the reliance on a few tech giants for our national infrastructure, businesses and citizens’ data has spurned the push for so-called digital sovereignty.

Europe is leading the way on this. By actively funding decentralised cloud infrastructure and homegrown software, governments are ensuring that an American tech failure cannot knock out a local hospital.

The world must adopt a similar blueprint. Australia, in particular, is well placed to grasp the opportunities that digital sovereignty brings. We already have the raw engineering talent. Companies such as Atlassian, Apromore, Airwallex and Canva have proven we can build world-class digital tools.

If governments strategically invested in local tech ecosystems, they wouldn’t just be buying digital insurance. They would drive much-needed market competition, dilute the power of big-tech monopolies and spread out systemic risks – ensuring that a single point of failure could never bring the economy to its knees.

This is not to say we should divest ourselves of any connection to big-tech platforms, which do and will continue to provide useful services. But if our entire global economy continues to rely on a handful of too-big-to-fail digital monopolies, we will have no one but ourselves to blame when our screens inevitably go black again.
