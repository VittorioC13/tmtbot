Amazon said Monday that it will acquire AI startup Decart's technology and recruit its founders and other staff, thus marking the latest Big Tech deal to secure AI talent and technology.

While Amazon has declined to disclose the deal's financial terms or comment on its value, Bloomberg News reported that the deal is worth around $7.5 billion.

This acquisition by Amazon offers a glimpse into the company's larger ambition to reduce its dependence on Nvidia's computing ecosystem and make its own AI chips a more attractive alternative for businesses.

Founded in 2023, Decart has been developing software to improve the efficiency of AI workloads across different hardware platforms, including chips from Nvidia, Amazon and Google. The company has been working with Amazon since 2025 to improve the performance of its Trainium processors.

The acquisition is significant because one of the biggest obstacles to Amazon's ambition of becoming a serious AI-chip supplier has not necessarily been the hardware itself, but the software ecosystem surrounding it.

What is the problem Amazon wishes to solve? The advantage that Nvidia has in artificial intelligence extends well beyond the performance of its graphics processing units (GPUs).

Its CUDA software platform has become deeply embedded in the workflows of AI researchers, developers and enterprises.

With its years of investment in software libraries, development tools, and optimized applications, Nvidia has become the default hardware choice for a large portion of the AI industry.

Even if an alternative processor offers attractive price-performance characteristics, customers will have to consider the engineering effort required to migrate their existing workloads, adapt their software, and optimize their models for a new architecture.

This is where Amazon's Trainium chips come in. They are designed to address this challenge by offering customers an alternative for training and running AI models through AWS.

But Amazon needs to demonstrate that the performance and cost benefits are substantial enough to justify switching, and this is exactly where Decart will be a strategic tool for the company.

Founded in 2023, the startup offers a software engine called Decart Optimization Stack (DOS), which helps companies run AI systems faster and switch more easily between a range of AI chips, including those from Nvidia, Amazon and Google.

For Amazon, this technology could make Trainium more competitive without requiring the company to depend entirely on increasingly expensive investments in additional computing capacity.

Trainium is already attracting large customers Amazon has spent years developing its custom silicon chips through Annapurna Labs, which it had acquired in 2015. Its portfolio includes Graviton processors for general-purpose cloud computing, Trainium for AI training and inference and Inferentia for AI inference workloads.

Trainium has gained particular strategic importance following Amazon's partnership with Anthropic.

Anthropic has been using AWS infrastructure to train and serve its Claude models. According to AWS, nearly one million Trainium2 chips are being used for Anthropic's workloads associated with Project Rainier. Decart itself, Karakuri, and Poolside are some of the other Trainium customers.

Amazon is not replacing Nvidia completely Despite this push towards its own custom chips, Amazon is not replacing Nvidia completely, and only in August this year, AWS and Nvidia announced an expanded partnership that includes plans to deploy two million additional Nvidia GPUs across AWS infrastructure.

Amazon wants to offer customers a broader choice of computing platforms while developing its own chips as a competitive alternative.

This is also where Decart's technology becomes particularly interesting.

Since its optimisation stack was designed to work across different chip architectures, it could help Amazon develop software capabilities that are not limited to a single hardware platform.

In the AI semiconductor industry, competition is no longer limited to companies capable of manufacturing the most powerful processors. It increasingly involves software, developer tools, model compatibility, networking and the ability to extract more computing performance from existing infrastructure.

For Nvidia, this integrated ecosystem has been a formidable competitive advantage.

For Amazon, acquiring Decart offers an opportunity to strengthen one of the areas where competing with Nvidia has historically been difficult.

[TITLE]Europe Tightens Smart Glasses Privacy Rules:
[TEXT]
Regulators in Europe are clamping down on smart glasses in response to growing concerns over covert filming, harassment, and breaches of digital privacy law.

AI glasses made by the likes of Meta and Snap are surging in popularity, but built-in cameras and microphones and potentially soon facial recognition, have drawn criticism from human rights and civil liberties advocates.

Charlie Whelton, policy lead on smart glasses at UK-based human rights organisation Liberty says: “Smart glasses facilitate covert filming of people without their consent. This is often focused on women and girls, or the harassment of vulnerable people for reactions on social media ...the current lack of regulation facilitates abuse.”

On 19 November, the Dutch Data Protection Authority issued an unprecedented warning advising consumers against buying smart glasses after its own study found they endangered the privacy and safety of bystanders and users.

Threading the needle between useful wearables and bystander privacy is important because successful smart glasses could be navigational aids for blind and low-vision wearers, help train robots, or act as real-time interpreters.

Europe converges on smart glasses restrictions

Earlier in November, the UK’s Minister for Safeguarding said the government is considering new restrictions on smart glasses in certain settings and limiting sales to certain users over concerns the technology is being used to surreptitiously film women. Several UK venues, including a major pub chain, have already restricted use of the eyewear.

Italy’s data protection watchdog, Garante, has called for a range of safeguards including changes to devices to prevent the automated publishing of captured footage online.

The toughest stance came from the Norwegian government, announcing plans for a temporary ban on smart glasses and other wearables in public spaces, including parks, beaches, museums, shopping malls, and places frequented by children.

National governments are responding to campaign groups, public discomfort, and emerging EU guidance.

The European Commission’s policy advisor, the European Data Protection Board (EDPB), issued a statement in October outlining its stance on the risks of smart glasses, backed up by findings in a report The connected gaze. It said the devices raise “significant concerns” around the protection of personal data and rights to privacy, security, freedom of expression, and freedom of movement.

Under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), a household exemption allows the use of AI glasses in a purely personal capacity, for example sharing photos with family or friends.

However, public use, including posting the videos on social media or using them for official, professional, political, or other non-personal activities makes the user a “data controller”, said EDPB, giving the wearer legal responsibility for compliance with obligations including transparency toward people whose data they are capturing. In practice that means, people recording in public must alert bystanders that they are recording personal data and for what purposes.

But manufacturers’ GDPR obligations cannot be shifted to users, explains Katie Hewson, partner at legal firm Stephenson Harwood: “A device that can record continuously, process biometric data, and transmit information to third-party servers – all without the knowledge of those being recorded – is in conflict with the regulatory framework, which is why regulators are flagging concerns.”

The data protection board called for a combination of new design features on devices to warn bystanders when they are recording, beyond the standard warning light on frames. Proposals include: brighter flashing lights that stop the recording if covered, audible voice commands for activation, beeps while recording, and a Bluetooth beacon that would notify nearby smartphones when the smart glasses are recording.

Smart glasses makers adapt to tighter rules

Device OEMs say they are on board with modifying their hardware and exploring less invasive designs and form factors.

“Some manufacturers are either changing designs or bringing in new designs, aware of an increasing backlash,” says Benjamin Hatton, connected devices research analyst at FDM CCS Insight. “One manufacturer told me that in order to get a footing in Europe they will probably need to offer a product that alleviates some of the concerns around cameras.”

Apple has reportedly delayed the launch of its first smart glasses to late 2027 to address privacy concerns. Meta, which dominates smart glasses with an 84% share of global shipments, updated its second generation Ray-Ban Meta frames software over the summer to prevent covert recording with a modified built-in LED.

The software detects a blocked, or modified, light and disables the camera. If the light is removed altogether, the camera shuts down permanently. Gentle Monster AI glasses, powered by Google, run a similar system.

Camera-free models are gaining traction. Meta has introduced audio-only frames, and China-based Even Realities specializes in camera-free smart glasses. Its founder Will Wang has argued that integrated cameras should wait until companies can put stronger protections in place.

But omitting cameras “completely changes the value proposition,” adds Hatton, making the eyewear “more akin to a note taker with a personal assistant built in.”

In its statement, the EDPB proposed additional technical measures to give bystanders more meaningful control over their personal data. These include limits on continuous recording and live streaming and blurring bystanders’ faces or avoiding processing their identities. Where possible, it said, processing should happen on the device, instead of on remote cloud servers.

“It’s really important that smart glasses technologies have privacy by design and by default built in from the outset, making it the responsibility of manufacturers and not leaving the burden of lawful use to individual users,” says Akvilė Šikšniūtė, policy officer for AI at The European Consumer Organisation (BEUC), which has formally urged EU authorities to investigate smart glasses. “It could mean minimizing the collection of personal data, prioritizing on device processing, or disabling unnecessary recording and storage functionalities by default to better protect the data of both the wearer and bystanders simultaneously.”

Incorporating such features could mean difficult design trade offs. Larger frames could make them less desirable to consumers, even as they allow additional functions. Next-generation chips powerful enough to handle local processing of data normally pushed to the cloud, could also be uncomfortably hot for wearers.

Existing chips already have sufficient processing power to handle safety features such as obscuring faces, says Hatton “if manufacturers chose to implement them.”

Spy-wear or fair to wear?

But not everyone is convinced that modifications to devices or tougher transparency rules will go far enough to protect consumers.

Berlin-based human rights organization HateAid is calling for a ban on sales of Meta AI glasses in Germany. The NGO filed a criminal complaint against Meta, Ray-Ban, Oakley, and several eyewear retailers in August arguing that they violate the Telecommunications, Digital Services, and Data Protection Act.

“Under this law it is prohibited to sell espionage gadgets that are hidden in everyday objects and intended to film people without their knowledge,” says Josephine Ballon, chief executive of HateAid, “This is exactly what smart glasses are designed for.”

A Meta spokesperson responded to the complaint indirectly in a Politico article, saying the smart glasses were approved by Germany’s Federal Network Agency in 2022 and were built with privacy safeguards that exceed those of a smartphone.

Expect the debate over covert filming, bystander consent, and GDPR compliance to gather pace over the coming months as European regulators continue to review the technology and how to police it. Meantime, engineers will be racing to preserve the beneficial uses of smart glasses while ensuring they don’t harm bystanders.

It comes alongside new figures showing coordinated action between government and mobile networks over the past year has blocked over a billion scam calls and messages. Mobile giants have rolled out artificial intelligence (AI) to detect scam calls, tightened checks on suspicious texts and are improving tracing capabilities to track down scam centres overseas.

Fraudsters are increasingly exploiting people’s trust in familiar mobile providers, using convincing fake offers, discounted handsets and promises of free or upgraded devices to try and steal money or personal information from victims. They use time-limited Black Friday and Christmas deals to create a sense of urgency, pushing victims to snap up offers and share their personal details.

New data published ahead of Black Friday from Cifas shows more than 15,000 cases of mobile provider impersonation fraud so far this year. That’s the equivalent of 58 incidents a day, an 85% increase on 2025 with estimated losses surpassing £32 million.

Minister for Fraud, Lord Hanson, said:

Fraudsters will use any trick they can to cash in on those of us hunting for bargains on Black Friday. Whether it’s a too-good-to-be-true phone deal or a fake upgrade offer, criminals count on people acting quickly and letting their guard down. We’ve teamed up with the UK’s biggest mobile networks to block over a billion scam calls and messages in the past year. This Black Friday, our message is Stop! Think Fraud. Take a moment to check an offer is genuine before handing over your money or personal details.

How to protect yourself from fraud this Black Friday Stop and think If a deal looks too good to be true, it probably is. Don’t be rushed into a quick decision. Don’t rush Never respond to unexpected calls, texts or emails claiming to be from your mobile provider without checking they are genuine. Go direct If in doubt, hang up or break contact immediately and contact your provider using the official website or app, rather than clicking links or calling numbers included in unexpected messages. Keep your details safe Never share your password or one-time passcode over the phone. Remember Your mobile provider will never get impatient or discourage you if you want to hang up and call back on an official number, and they will never ask you to share your mobile screen with them.

The new advice forms part of the government’s wider efforts to tackle fraud at its source and protect the public from becoming victims. It provides the public with practical advice which is incorporated into communications sent by mobile providers to customers, helping more people spot the warning signs and protect themselves. It has been coupled with a law enforcement blitz, with over 700 addresses involved in phone scams being identified across Birmingham, London, Oldham, Preston and Lancashire.

One year on from the signing of the Telecommunications Fraud Sector Charter, the government and mobile giants are continuing to work together to block scam calls and disrupt fraudsters. Signatories included BT EE, Virgin Media O2, VodafoneThree, Tesco Mobile, Talk Talk, Sky and Comms Council UK (CCUK). Phone networks will continue to take action to make it harder than ever for criminals to trick people through scam calls, using cutting edge technology to expose fraudsters and bring them to justice.

Fraud is one of the most common crimes in the country, with devastating consequences for victims, their families and businesses. The Fraud Strategy, published earlier this year, sets out how the government will work with law enforcement and industry to dismantle criminal networks, bring offenders to justice, and protect the public.

Rachel Andrews, Corporate Security and Fraud Director at VodafoneThree said:

One year on from the launch of the Fraud Charter, we’ve made significant strides in strengthening the UK’s collective response to fraud. By bringing together telecoms operators, government, regulators and law enforcement, we’re making it harder for criminals to exploit communications networks and target consumers. At VodafoneThree, that has meant moving beyond simply blocking scams to using intelligence to help disrupt the criminal networks behind them, including SIM farms being used to target people at scale. But we cannot stand still. Criminals are constantly changing their tactics, increasingly using social engineering across digital and social media channels to impersonate trusted organisations and manipulate people into sharing information or making payments. As Black Friday and Christmas approach, we’re urging everyone to stop, think and take a moment before responding to an unexpected message, call or request for personal or financial information.

Murray Mackenzie, Director of Fraud Prevention at Virgin Media O2, said:

Sophisticated fraud gangs will stop at nothing to target victims if they think there’s money to be made. That’s why it’s more important than ever to stop and think before responding to unexpected calls, texts or messages this Black Friday. We’re now using new AI technology to block 3 million scam calls a week on the O2 network and have prevented more than 1.6 billion scam texts from reaching our customers to date. But we know busy shopping periods can make it harder to spot what’s genuine and what’s not. So, we’ve teamed up across the sector to raise awareness and are calling on everyone to report suspicious activity to 7726 to help keep more people safe.

Jonathan Taylor, CEO at Tesco Mobile, said:

At Tesco Mobile, protecting our customers from scams and fraud remains one of our top priorities. Twelve months on from signing the Charter, we’re seeing the real impact of cross-industry collaboration, from identifying and preventing fraudulent activity faster, to raising awareness of the risks and strengthening the support available to people who have fallen victim to scams. Together, we’re helping better protect customers from new and emerging threats.

Andy Mayo, Sky’s Head of Fraud, said:

Fraudsters rely on people trusting familiar brands, which is why we encourage everyone to pause and check before responding to an unexpected offer or request for personal information. Sky is proud to support Stop! Think Fraud and, as we head into the busy Black Friday and Christmas shopping period, continued collaboration between industry, government and law enforcement remains ever-important. By working together to strengthen protections and helping people recognise the warning signs, we can make it increasingly difficult for criminals to reach and deceive consumers.

Alex Towers, Director of Policy and Public Affairs at BT Group, said:

Fraudsters exploit busy shopping periods like Black Friday to make bogus handset deals and upgrade offers seem convincing, often pressuring people to act quickly. Genuine providers will never pressure customers into an immediate decision, ask for a password or one-time passcode, or discourage them from hanging up and checking an offer independently. In the first year of the Charter, BT and EE have continued to invest in cutting-edge technology to identify and block millions of scam calls and messages. We have also strengthened account and SIM security, using AI-powered protection to identify threats across calls, texts, emails and websites, and increasing customer awareness to prevent fraud and theft. But technology alone isn’t enough. Through the Telecommunications Fraud Sector Charter, government and industry are working together to strengthen our defences, share intelligence and help people recognise the warning signs. This Black Friday, stop, think and check: if an offer doesn’t feel right, contact your provider directly.

Emma Jones, Assistant Director of Operations at Catch22, said:

Through the support we deliver in Catch22’s Victims’ Services, we see firsthand the devastating impact fraud can have on people’s wellbeing and sense of security. Our teams provide emotional and practical support, helping victims rebuild their confidence and understand that what has happened isn’t their fault. We also work within communities to raise awareness of common scams and help people recognise the warning signs. As fraud tactics continue to evolve, it’s vital that people have access to the information and support they need to stay safe, particularly during periods like Black Friday when scams are more prevalent.

John Hayward-Cripps, Chief Executive of Neighbourhood Watch, said:

The extent to which criminals are willing and able to exploit new technology is both frightening and frustrating. They clearly use Black Friday as an opportunity to target shoppers, and it’s a growing problem. It is vital that phone companies play their part in protecting the public, the first contact many of our members have with a fraudster is often through a phone call, text, or other type of message. And as scams become increasingly sophisticated, they are harder to spot. Phones are such an essential part of everyday life, particularly for people who may be lonely or isolated. Our members tell us that these crimes are about far more than financial loss. They can take a significant emotional toll, leaving people feeling fearful, less trusting, and less confident in carrying out everyday activities.
