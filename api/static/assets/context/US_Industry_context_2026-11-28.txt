That change merged its two existing New Zealand and Australian drinks businesses, Frucor Suntory and Suntory Global Spirits (formerly Beam Suntory) into one manufacturing, sales and distribution group.

Speaking exclusively to the Herald after moving into its new Auckland CBD office, Suntory Oceania’s New Zealand general manager, Deb Cooper, said the change had made a palpable difference.

“It’s been such a fantastic space to move into, more for the connections that you’re starting to see develop and evolve,” Cooper said.

“We’ve previously operated out of three different offices in Auckland, and to finally bring all of us under one Suntory roof is just awesome.”

Suntory Oceania's New Zealand general manager, Deb Cooper, and licensed sales director Jared Chamberlain.

Cooper has worked with Suntory for 12 years across two stints, from 2009-2014 and across Australia and New Zealand since 2019.

She said the beverage market had continued to grow over the past seven years, but acknowledged consumer needs had evolved.

“They’re not just looking for water or a soft drink; they’re looking for a beverage that’s going to do that little bit extra for them.

“Functionality is something we’re really seeing start to come into the consumer’s needs. At the end of the day, it comes down to the value that they’re looking for when they’re going to spend their money.”

Shifting consumer behaviour and growing sales volumes were key factors behind the company’s decision to invest in its Wiri manufacturing facilities this year.

Cooper confirmed the business had spent $20m on a new canning line which can produce up to 1.4 million cans per day.

Suntory Oceania has also invested $13m in a new raw materials warehouse to satisfy future needs.

“We have been around in New Zealand for some time, and our manufacturing site has been there with us. We really needed to make sure we upgraded our manufacturing capability.

“Beverages do continue to grow, and we need to make sure that we’re setting ourselves up for the future, and New Zealand is a really important part of that.”

While the drinks category more widely is growing, alcohol consumption has continued to trend downwards in New Zealand.

Although consumption declines have largely been recorded for beer and wine compared with spirits, Cooper believed Suntory’s strengths were in premium and ready-to-drink (RTD) options.

Suntory Oceania has a wide portfolio of non-alcoholic and alcoholic brands under its stewardship, including V, Pepsi and Gatorade.

Using that multi-beverage portfolio to partner better with Suntory’s customers is a priority for Cooper, pointing to the deal it signed last year with restaurant chain Lone Star as a prime example.

She said combining Suntory Global Spirits and Suntory Beverage and Food had helped customers, as they now only needed to deal with one supplier.

That simplification also extended to how it engages with customers, including joint business planning.

“Ultimately, we’re thinking about the consumer. Both of us are thinking about the consumer in the category, and we really pride ourselves on our consumer insights and our category insights and really knowing what’s at the heart of the consumer’s needs.

“When we’re both focusing on that end goal, it becomes quite easy to build out a joint strategy to service that consumer.”

Cooper said growing the company’s customer base was a challenge in a saturated market, but believed there was opportunity to drive greater volumes through existing customers.

Suntory has invested $13 million in a new raw materials warehouse at its Auckland manufacturing site.

The company’s last financial result took a surprising downturn, with revenue for the 12 months ended December 31, 2025 falling 20.8% from $655.1m to $518.7m.

Net profit fell 78.6% from $36.9m to $7.8m year-on-year.

Despite the performance, Suntory Beverage & Food New Zealand paid a dividend of $30.3m to its international parent, Suntory Beverage & Food, up from $23m the year before.

The company’s outstanding debts to related parties nearly doubled year-on-year, rising from $62.8m to $122.5m.

Manufacturing costs have risen, with Cooper confirming that packaging, ingredients and fuel had all made an impact.

She said the business had been forced to absorb those costs in the short term, but conceded prices could eventually rise if conditions don’t change. She was doing what she could internally to avoid it.

Looking ahead to the summer season, Cooper said it was a crucial period for the business, contributing about 35% of yearly revenue.

“It is the time of the year when people are the most thirsty, so we spend a lot of time out in trade making sure we’ve got all of our new products out and available.

“We’ve got the best activations out in market during this time, and we are quite optimistic about the summer we have coming up.”

[TITLE]NEXIM seeks more non-interest banks to widen export funding:
[TEXT]
The Nigerian Export-Import Bank has called on the Central Bank of Nigeria to license more non-interest financial institutions, warning that limited access to interest-free financing could restrict the participation of Nigerian businesses in regional and international trade.

The Managing Director and Chief Executive Officer of NEXIM, Abubakar Bello, made the appeal on Thursday at a stakeholder engagement forum in Abuja on leveraging the African Continental Free Trade Area to improve Nigeria’s export competitiveness and diversify the economy.

Bello said many Nigerian entrepreneurs were unwilling to access conventional loans because of their interest-based structure, leaving a significant segment of businesses without suitable financing options.

He urged the CBN to expand the number of non-interest banking licences, arguing that a broader range of financing institutions would help businesses that operate outside the conventional banking model.

“We are all aware that there is a large population of Nigerians that don’t borrow interest-based money,” he said.

He disclosed that NEXIM was working with the Islamic Development Bank to establish a dedicated non-interest banking window to provide financing for businesses within the export value chain.

Bello also called for stronger collaboration among government agencies to mobilise financing for small and medium-sized enterprises seeking to enter the continental market.

He disclosed that NEXIM had partnered with the Small and Medium Enterprises Development Agency of Nigeria to source N42bn for businesses operating within the export value chain.

The bank is also collaborating with the Nigerian Export Promotion Council and the African Export-Import Bank to mobilise additional capital and support exporters.

Bello said NEXIM had disbursed more than N800bn to over 1,300 non-oil exporters since its establishment in 1991, supporting the creation and sustenance of approximately 110,000 industrial jobs.

He argued that improving access to financing was essential to helping more Nigerian businesses take advantage of the African market, which has a population of more than one billion people.

On export diversification, the NEXIM chief welcomed Nigeria’s non-oil exports reaching a record $6.1bn in 2025 and intra-African trade rising by 21 per cent to $9.02bn.

However, he warned that crude oil remained a dominant component of Nigeria’s exports to the continent, while a considerable volume of regional trade continued to take place through informal channels.

He urged businesses to move beyond exporting unprocessed commodities such as cocoa, cotton, hides and solid minerals, calling for greater domestic processing to increase the value of goods sold abroad.

The bank’s strategy, which he described as PAVE, centres on producing goods, adding value locally and exporting finished or processed products.

He identified agro-processing, particularly cashew, sesame, shea, ginger and cassava, alongside manufacturing and services such as financial technology, music and film, as sectors with opportunities for expansion across Africa.

However, Bello cautioned that access to preferential tariffs under the AfCFTA would not automatically make Nigerian products competitive, particularly as the agreement also exposes domestic producers to increased competition from other African economies.

He identified high energy costs, inefficient ports and regulatory bottlenecks as major obstacles that increase production and logistics costs for Nigerian exporters.

To address regional transportation challenges, he highlighted NEXIM’s Sealink project, which seeks to develop inland waterways and coastal shipping networks for bulk cargo movement across West and Central Africa.

Meanwhile, the CBN Deputy Governor for Financial System Stability, Abubakar Yuguda, said Nigeria needed to expand its production base and increase the domestic value added to goods and services to strengthen its position in international trade.

[TITLE]Tokyo core inflation at 16-month high, bolsters case for BOJ hike:
[TEXT]
TOKYO: Annual core inflation in Japan's capital accelerated in November at the fastest pace in 16 months, highlighting mounting price pressure and bolstering the case for an interest rate hike as soon as next month.

The data, widely regarded as a leading indicator of nationwide trends, will be among the factors the Bank of Japan scrutinises in deciding whether to raise interest rates at its next two-day policy meeting ending on Dec 18.

The core consumer price index, which includes prices of fuel but not fresh food, in Tokyo rose 2.9 per cent in November from a year earlier, data showed on Friday, compared with the median market forecast of 3.0 per cent. It followed a 2.6 per cent gain in October and marked the fastest year-on-year rise since July 2025.

Core inflation in Tokyo, which is seen as a leading indicator of nationwide trends, thus stayed above the BOJ's 2 per cent target for three straight months, cementing the case for further hikes.

While the increase was driven partly by an end to government utility subsidies, prices rose for a broad range of goods and services including food, daily necessities and hotel charges.

"Core consumer inflation is expected to accelerate due to rising energy prices from the Middle East conflict and ensuing second-round effects," said Masato Koike, senior economist at Sompo Institute Plus.

"If the current market instability stemming from concern over the AI and chip outlook persists, there's a chance the BOJ may forgo a rate hike in December. But our baseline projection is for the BOJ to proceed with one next month," he said.

An index stripping away the effect of volatile fresh food and fuel prices, which is closely watched by the BOJ as a more accurate gauge of trend inflation, rose 2.5 per cent in November after a 2.6 per cent gain in October, the data showed.

Inflation rose for services including public transportation and medical care, suggesting the shift in Japan's inflation dynamics is no longer driven only by temporary supply shocks, said Ayako Fujita, chief Japan economist at JPMorgan Securities.

"We expect the momentum in price increases to remain broad-based" toward the first quarter of 2027, she said. "While momentum in core goods inflation should gradually ease, we expect inflationary pressures to broaden further across food and services."

Short-term Japanese government bond yields edged higher on Friday as signs of persistent inflation reinforced expectations of a rate hike next month.

Investors are focusing on what hints BOJ Governor Kazuo Ueda could drop on the likelihood of a December rate hike, and the future rate-hike path, in a speech scheduled for Monday.

CLOSE TO TERMINAL?

With stubbornly high energy costs and rising import costs from a weak yen adding to inflationary pressures, the BOJ raised its policy rate to 1.25 per cent in September after delivering a hike in June - a shorter interval than the roughly twice-a-year hikes seen in 2024 and 2025.

The central bank kept rates steady in October, but said underlying inflation has roughly hit its 2 per cent target and signalled its readiness to continue hiking rates to prevent price growth from accelerating too much.

A majority of analysts polled by Reuters expect the BOJ to raise rates in December and follow up with another hike by end-March. Many see the terminal rate at around 1.75 per cent or 2.0 per cent.

Hawkish BOJ board member Naoki Tamura said on Thursday the central bank must respond swiftly to signs of an inflation overshoot, including by accelerating the pace of rate hikes.

While many analysts view two more rate hikes as all but certain, there is less conviction on how far the BOJ can tighten beyond that, with inflationary pressures from higher energy costs and a weak yen expected to ease in the second half of next year.

Shigeto Nagai, head of Japan Economics at Oxford Economics, projects headline inflation to slow to 2 per cent in late 2027.

"We think this will allow the BOJ to complete its multi-year rate normalisation process by mid-2027, hitting a terminal policy rate of 1.75 per cent," he said.

Continued attacks on Russian energy infrastructure also constrain the recovery in middle distillate availability, even as broader crude supply conditions improve.
