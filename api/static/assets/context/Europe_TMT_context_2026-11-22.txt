[TITLE]Introducing Amazon SageMaker HyperPod Global Inference Gateway: One endpoint across Regions and clusters:
[TEXT]
During a traffic peak, a request can sit in a queue on a saturated HyperPod cluster while GPUs on another cluster, sometimes in another AWS Region, sit idle. You have already paid for that capacity, but your requests can’t reach it.

In Introducing Amazon SageMaker HyperPod Inference Gateway, we showed how the Inference Gateway gives each Amazon SageMaker HyperPod cluster a single, GPU-aware endpoint. It reads real-time signals such as key-value (KV) cache utilization and queue depth to place every request on the best-suited pod. That solves routing inside one cluster, but not across clusters.

Today, SageMaker HyperPod introduces the Global Inference Gateway. It is one SageMaker-managed gateway that routes across every cluster you register, in one Region or across Regions. The gateway picks the cluster and carries the request over the AWS backbone, with automatic failover and authentication through AWS Signature Version 4 (SigV4) and AWS Identity and Access Management (IAM). Inside each cluster, the Inference Gateway keeps picking the pod, unchanged. The Global Inference Gateway is the second tier of the architecture we described in part 1.

The challenge: Capacity spread across clusters and Regions

Models are getting larger, and the GPU capacity to serve them is harder to find. To get enough of it, you spread it across more clusters and more Regions.

Consider a team that serves four models across five HyperPod clusters in two Regions. Without the Inference Gateway, clients track 20 endpoints: one per model per cluster. The Inference Gateway cuts that to five, one per cluster. But each client still picks a cluster itself.

That choice has costs:

Uneven load. During peaks, requests queue in one Region while GPUs in the other Region are idle.

During peaks, requests queue in one Region while GPUs in the other Region are idle. Manual failover. When a cluster fails, an engineer changes configuration to move traffic away from it.

When a cluster fails, an engineer changes configuration to move traffic away from it. Custom plumbing. Spreading traffic across Regions means building and maintaining load balancers, DNS, and cross-Region networking yourself.

The team needs one endpoint that sends each request to the best-suited cluster and moves traffic off a failed cluster on its own.

The Global Inference Gateway helps you get more from the GPU capacity you already have, with less to operate.

How it works

Setting up the gateway takes two decisions: which clusters to open up for routing, and which of them to group behind one gateway.

Publish a cluster. You do this once per cluster. You turn on the spec.globalRouting block in the cluster’s existing InferenceGatewayConfig . The HyperPod Inference controller then publishes the cluster privately from your own account. Create the gateway. You do this once per gateway. You call CreateGlobalInferenceGateway with the clusters to route across, and SageMaker returns one endpoint.

A cluster is reachable only after you publish it. When you publish a cluster, the controller creates a Network Load Balancer in front of the cluster’s Inference Gateway, publishes a virtual private cloud (VPC) endpoint service on it, and uploads the listener’s certificate to your TLS bucket. These resources live in your account. If you list a cluster in a gateway before you publish it, the call fails with a message that tells you what to fix.

How a request flows

Figure 1 shows the path of each request: (1) your client signs the request with SigV4 and sends it to the gateway’s regional endpoint. (2) The gateway authenticates the caller and picks the best cluster. (3) AWS PrivateLink carries the request to that cluster, in the same Region or another, and (4) the cluster’s Inference Gateway routes it to a model pod, as it does today.

On each cluster, a service-managed metrics publisher with its own IAM role sends the cluster’s load to the gateway.

How the gateway picks a cluster

For each request, the gateway scores every healthy cluster on four signals and sends the request to the highest-scoring cluster. Each cluster’s metrics publisher supplies the load signals.

You control how much each signal counts with routing weights. Each weight is a value from 0.0 to 1.0, and a weight of 0 turns that signal off. If you set weights, you set all four, and they must add up to 1.0. If you omit them, the defaults in the following table apply.

Weight What it scores Default KVHeadroom Available KV cache and GPU memory on the cluster 0.20 QueueDepth How backed up the cluster is 0.25 RegionProximity Distance between the cluster and the gateway’s Region 0.40 ErrorRate Recent failed requests on the cluster 0.15

The defaults favor clusters close to the gateway’s Region, while still moving traffic toward clusters with available capacity and away from clusters that are failing. If resilience matters more than proximity for your workload, raise ErrorRate and lower RegionProximity . For example, the following routing block weights recent errors at 0.7:

{"Weights": {"KVHeadroom": 0.1, "QueueDepth": 0.1, "RegionProximity": 0.1, "ErrorRate": 0.7}}

You pass the weights in the Routing parameter of CreateGlobalInferenceGateway , and you can change them later with UpdateGlobalInferenceGateway .

Security and access

Every request to the gateway is signed with SigV4 and authorized with IAM. Callers need permission to invoke the gateway, and streaming has its own action:

{ "Version": "2012-10-17", "Statement": [{ "Effect": "Allow", "Action": [ "sagemaker:InvokeGlobalInferenceGateway", "sagemaker:InvokeGlobalInferenceGatewayWithResponseStream" ], "Resource": "arn:aws:sagemaker:<region>:<account-id>:global-inference-gateway/<gateway-id>" }] }

Attach this policy to each IAM identity that sends requests. To allow every gateway in the account, use global-inference-gateway/* as the resource.

The execution role follows least privilege. It can describe only the clusters you list, reach only their Amazon Elastic Kubernetes Service (Amazon EKS) API, and read only the routing certificates under the gig/ prefix of your TLS bucket, tagged as created by HyperPod Inference.

Identity tokens. If your clusters check JSON Web Tokens (JWTs), pass the token with --customer-identity-token (the X-Amzn-SageMaker-Customer-Identity-Token header), with no Bearer prefix. The token travels inside the SigV4 signature, so an unsigned token is rejected with 403 and a repeated token with 400. Turn on JWT checks on every cluster that serves a model, or on none of them, so a request doesn’t succeed on one cluster and fail on another.

Operating the gateway

Here are steps to configure the gateway.

Add a cluster

Prepare the new cluster and create the execution role’s EKS access entry on it, as described in the documentation. Also add the new cluster to the Resource lists of the execution role’s permissions policy. Then call AddInferenceGateway in the gateway’s Region, even if the new cluster is in another Region:

aws sagemaker add-inference-gateway \ --identifier <gateway-name> \ --region <region> \ --cluster-arn arn:aws:sagemaker:<region-3>:<account-id>:cluster/<cluster-id-3> \ --name <gateway-config-name-3> \ --namespace inference-gateway

The gateway validates the cluster and starts routing to it once it is healthy. The endpoint, other clusters, and clients aren’t affected.

Detach a cluster

Call RemoveInferenceGateway in the gateway’s Region:

aws sagemaker remove-inference-gateway \ --identifier <gateway-name> \ --region <region> \ --cluster-arn arn:aws:sagemaker:<region-3>:<account-id>:cluster/<cluster-id-3> \ --name <gateway-config-name-3> \ --namespace inference-gateway

The gateway stops sending new requests to the cluster, lets in-flight requests finish, and removes the private endpoint. A gateway always keeps at least one cluster. To withdraw the cluster fully, set globalRouting.enabled: false in its InferenceGatewayConfig and reapply it.

Delete the gateway

Call DeleteGlobalInferenceGateway :

aws sagemaker delete-global-inference-gateway \ --identifier <gateway-name> --region <region>

The gateway endpoint stops serving, and per-cluster endpoints keep serving. After the gateway is deleted, withdraw each cluster with globalRouting.enabled: false , then delete the execution role and the IAM permissions you added for global routing.

[TITLE]Kalshi Adds 36 More Stock Perpetual Futures, From TSMC and ASML to Goldman Sachs:
[TEXT]
Kalshi has added 36 more single-stock perpetual futures to its U.S. exchange, and this batch reaches deep into the chip supply chain. Taiwan Semiconductor, ASML, SK hynix, Arm, KLA and Lam Research are all on the list.

In a certification letter filed with the Commodity Futures Trading Commission dated Nov. 17, KalshiEX LLC said it intends to start listing the contracts on Nov. 18, on a continuous basis. The products are perpetual security futures, meaning they never expire.

The filing also lays out how fast the product line has grown. With this batch, Kalshi’s single-stock perpetual lineup reaches 148 contracts in just over two weeks.

Three Batches in 16 Days

According to the letter, the CFTC approved Kalshi’s first 58 perpetual security futures products on Nov. 2, after the exchange submitted them for review on Sept. 18. We covered that decision when the CFTC cleared stock perps earlier this month.

ADVERTISEMENT

Kalshi then self-certified 54 more on Nov. 4. The Nov. 17 filing adds 36, using the faster self-certification route under CFTC Regulations 40.2(a) and 41.23(a). Under that route, an exchange certifies that a product complies with the law rather than waiting for agency approval.

The letter says the new contracts have the same terms as the earlier ones in every respect except the underlying stock. Kalshi’s New Product Committee determined that each stock meets the listing standards in Chapter 14 of its rulebook, which has been filed with the Securities and Exchange Commission.

That dual oversight is part of the design. Kalshi is notice-registered with the SEC as a national securities exchange for security futures, so both regulators have a hand in these products.

Which Stocks Made the List

The schedule includes 33 common stocks and three American depositary receipts. The ADRs are Arm Holdings, SK hynix and Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing.

Semiconductors and chip equipment stand out. Besides the three ADRs, the batch covers ASML, Analog Devices, KLA, Lam Research, Amphenol, Arista Networks and Dell.

ADVERTISEMENT

Wall Street names are well represented too. Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley, Citigroup, Wells Fargo, BlackRock, Charles Schwab and American Express are all included.

The rest span health care (Abbott, Danaher, Thermo Fisher, Intuitive Surgical, Gilead), industrials (GE Aerospace, GE Vernova, Deere, RTX, Union Pacific) and other sectors, including Accenture, CrowdStrike, Shopify, Linde, NextEra Energy, Philip Morris, TJX, T-Mobile US and Welltower.

By our count, 24 of the underlying shares have their primary listing on the NYSE and 12 on Nasdaq.

How the Contracts Work

Each contract covers 100 shares of its underlying stock, with a minimum tick of $0.005 and a tick value of $0.50. Traders do not need to buy a full contract. The minimum order is one five-thousandth of a contract, which equals 0.02 of a share.

In practice, the smallest position is worth about 2% of one share’s price. That is the same minimum unit Kalshi used for the 54 contracts it certified on Nov. 4.

Instead of an expiry date, the contracts use a once-daily funding payment to stay close to the stock price. Kalshi measures the gap between the contract and an underlying price index minute by minute during the regular U.S. stock session. If the contract trades at a premium, longs pay shorts. If it trades at a discount, shorts pay longs.

Trading runs from 6 p.m. ET on Sunday to 5 p.m. ET on Friday, with a one-hour daily maintenance break from 5 p.m. to 6 p.m. ET. That means the contracts can trade overnight on weekdays, when the stock market itself is closed. Settlement is in cash, and no shares change hands.

A Built-In Leverage Cap

The most important number in the filing may be 15.50%. That is the minimum margin Kalshi requires on each position, measured against its current market value. The exchange can set it higher.

Federal security futures rules already require at least 15% customer margin. Kalshi’s floor sits slightly above that, which caps leverage at roughly 6.45 times. Leverage is not user-selectable, according to the filing.

Kalshi contrasts this with venues outside U.S. rules. Its filing notes that independent builders on Hyperliquid deploy single-stock perpetuals under the HIP-3 standard, introduced in November 2025, with leverage of up to 20 times.

Citing a comment letter from the Hyperliquid Policy Center, the filing says HIP-3 markets recorded more than $480 billion in notional volume in their first ten months, with about $4 billion in open interest.

Crypto’s Product Goes Mainstream

Perpetual futures were invented in crypto, and for years Bitcoin traders were their main users. Kalshi’s filing even cites academic work on hedging with leverage on Bitcoin perpetuals.

Now the same structure is being applied to chipmakers and Wall Street banks inside a regulated, centrally cleared market. We see that as a strong endorsement of a design that the Bitcoin market tested at scale first.

It also connects back to Bitcoin holders. As we reported, Kalshi Prime now lets institutions margin Kalshi perpetual futures with Bitcoin held at Anchorage. Every new contract on the exchange widens the menu that Bitcoin-backed collateral can support.

The race is not limited to Kalshi. The CFTC recently cleared Backpack’s blockchain exchange for U.S. perpetual futures, and rival Polymarket US has certified new oil and gas contracts.

What to Watch

The filing notes that any stock not on the schedule must be submitted to the CFTC before listing. Kalshi has filed three batches in 16 days, so more additions would not be a surprise.

The bigger test is volume. Offshore crypto-native venues already have a head start in stock perps. Kalshi is betting that tighter margin, central clearing and oversight from both the CFTC and SEC will draw traders who want the nearly round-the-clock perpetual format inside U.S. rules.

If it works, the trading tool that Bitcoin popularized will have become a standard part of the U.S. stock market.

[TITLE]FCC Backs AT&T's Plan to Remove Copper Landlines in California:
[TEXT]
The Federal Communications Commission is backing AT&T’s push to override a California regulator so that the carrier can discontinue copper landline service to consumers.

On Friday, the FCC announced that it was preempting "specific requirements in California law that prevent consumers from transitioning off of unreliable, slow copper lines,” even as some residents fear they’ll be saddled with spotty cell service as an alternative.

AT&T argues that California’s "Carrier of the Last Resort” (COLR) rules prevent it from removing the old-school copper network that currently serves only 3% of California households, a number that continues to shrink. In May, the company urged the commission to declare that federal laws preempted California's regulations, thus clearing the way for AT&T to discontinue the service as it prioritizes high-speed fiber and expanded 5G cellular coverage.

Friday’s FCC order partially grants AT&T’s petition, agreeing that under federal law it has “explicit jurisdiction over the discontinuance of interstate telecommunications services.”

Still, the order refrains from “wholly” preempting California’s Carrier of the Last Resort rules, and seems to instead focus on the AT&T situation. “While we preempt those requirements that necessitate that AT&T obtain a variety of CPUC [California Public Utilities Commission] approvals before it can discontinue POTS [plain old telephone service] in accordance with federal authorization, we do not preempt the COLR and other obligations more generally,” the 74-page document says. (Photo by Kevin Dietsch/Getty Images)

The FCC issued the order, even though many locals opposed AT&T’s proposal to discontinue the landline service. “Please do not allow AT&T to eliminate the last reliable communication tool for rural residents. The VOIP and cellular infrastructure is not robust enough to provide...equal access for their communication needs,” one concerned user wrote in public comments.

The CPUC also told the FCC “nothing in California’s COLR rules prevents AT&T from retiring its copper infrastructure," even though it argued against preemption. CPUC’s main concern has been that the company’s replacement, called AT&T Phone - Advanced, won’t perform as well indoors compared with outdoor cell coverage, and it’ll cost more for consumers.

However, the FCC disagreed and was convinced that AT&T Phone - Advanced would be offered at a lower price, including “at a discounted rate of $25 per month to existing Lifeline customers in California,” while providing sufficient signal strength. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr also said: “Importantly, this decision does not come at the expense of consumer safeguards. The FCC’s rules require providers to notify consumers of proposed service discontinuances and provide an opportunity for public comment. We also preserve important protections for service reliability and public safety, including access to 911.”

Despite the FCC’s order, it’s not entirely clear if AT&T will pursue the copper landline phase-out in California. In May, the carrier sued to overturn the local regulations and block the CPUC from enforcing them against AT&T, which noted it spends $1 billion each year to maintain the copper network. That court case remains ongoing.

For now, AT&T EVP Rhonda Johnson praised Chairman Carr for the decision. “California deserves fast, reliable, future-ready technology – and with the right policies in place, we’re delivering it and transitioning away from copper," she said. "No customer will be left without access to voice or 911 service, and we’re committed to working with our customers every step of the way during this transition.”

However, Democratic FCC Commissioner Anna Gomez opposed the order, saying: “Californians deserve to know that when they raise a concern, someone in their state with the authority to act on it is listening. I cannot support an item that moves that authority further away from them. I respectfully dissent.”

In a statement, CPUC also said the FCC's decision "without adequately considering the concerns of California is deeply concerning, particularly for customers in rural communities, high-fire-threat areas, and communities where reliable communications are essential to public safety." The regulator also hinted it'll explore legal options.

"Customers deserve confidence that their communications service they pay for will work when they need it most, CPUC said. "California will continue to ensure that confidence is borne out in practice, and will continue to defend its responsibility to protect consumers, preserve essential communications services, and promote public safety. We will carefully evaluate the FCC’s order and all available legal and regulatory options."

According to the regulator, AT&T has proposed discontinuing the legacy telephone service in the state on or after June 1, 2027. About 184,000 residential customers and 15,000 business customers will be affected.

Wall Street was plunged into panic by a trading meltdown on Friday as stocks plummeted and tech giants lost billions in one of the worst days of the year.

The sell-off, driven by mounting fears over an artificial intelligence bubble, sent the S&P 500 tumbling 3.7 percent and the tech-heavy Nasdaq plunging 5.3 percent.

The carnage capped the S&P 500's worst week since April 2025 and marked the benchmark index's seventh straight day of losses - its longest losing streak since the onset of the COVID pandemic in 2020.

The panic comes just two days after artificial intelligence giant Anthropic (ANT) completed the largest initial public offering in history, pricing its shares at $83, raising $81 billion and giving the company a market capitalization of roughly $1.3 trillion.

Shares of ANT did well on their first day of trading on Thursday, but today they fell back to the IPO price of $83 around midday – a bad sign for the newly minted public company and AI in general.

Meanwhile, shares of Nvidia lost 13.8 percent today, the chipmaker's worst day since the DeepSeek rout in January 2025 and the company’s seventh straight day of declines.

Since founder Jensen Huang was embroiled in a scandal that led to his resignation last week, shares of NVDA have lost nearly half their value.

Beyond the world of AI, investors are eyeing the possibility of an even larger Federal Reserve interest rate hike in December – a move that would further hamper a tech-driven market that can ill afford more expensive credit.

The S&P 500 closed out the day down 3.7 percent, putting it firmly in correction territory - down a total of 11.8 percent from its most recent high in early October.

Shares of Nvidia lost 13.8 percent today, the chipmaker's worst day since the DeepSeek rout in January 2025 and the company’s seventh straight day of declines.

The tech sell-off began in Japan and South Korea overnight, where the Nikkei and Kospi indices each fell more than five percent, with European markets down sharply as well.

AI stocks have hefty market values and have a much bigger impact on the market´s direction. They were responsible for Wall Street’s big gains this year and are now behind the big slide for the market.

AI stocks have been losing ground partly over worries that their big market values and heavy spending are unjustified.

Fears of a bubble within the sector have also been exacerbated by the results of the recent midterm elections, with candidates advocating for a slowdown in data center construction winning at both the federal and state level.

‘The whole notion that AI can continue to spend unlimited amounts of money and support the stock market, I think the notion of that is ending as we speak, that’s what´s being priced right now,’ said Michael Antonelli, market strategist at Baird.

‘But I don’t think that means that the overall bull market that started in October 2022 is over.’

‘The stock market is going through an AI repricing, reflecting an environment of slowing data center buildouts, reduced funding sources for AI-related projects and a slower trajectory of technology-related earnings growth,’ Richard Saperstein, chief investment officer at Treasury Partners, told CNBC.
