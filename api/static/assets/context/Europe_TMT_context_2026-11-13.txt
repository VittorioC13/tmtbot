Foreign investors have returned to Indian equities in recent days, partly due to a correction in technology stocks in the United States (US) and the AI trade. Do you think this trend will sustain?

When you first saw the rotation out of India, valuations in the country were also high. Capital moved to Taiwan, South Korea and other countries that are part of the AI ecosystem. Markets benefiting from the AI investment cycle attracted money.

The recent return of capital is being described as the anti-AI trade. I do not particularly like that characterisation. While it may benefit India in the short term through foreign portfolio and direct investment, I do not want India to be seen as part of an anti-AI wave. I want India to be part of the AI ecosystem.

Ignoring AI can be at your own peril. It will transform economies, companies, the way we work and the way we consume. The questions increasingly being asked of companies and countries are whether they are AI-native and whether they are positioned to emerge as winners in the AI era. At present, the US and China are ahead of other countries. This is particularly important for India because of its large services sector, which has benefited from the technology and software revolution but could be disrupted by AI.

Foreign direct investment (FDI) and foreign institutional investment (FII) are both important and welcome. India needs to become an AI-native nation and an endgame winner in the AI ecosystem.

Consumer, pharmaceuticals and manufacturing — areas where multinationals have a strong presence in the country.

Given global uncertainties and geopolitical tensions, how is the merger and acquisition (M&A) market holding up?

The M&A market is among the best we have seen in recent years. The pipeline remains equally robust. One reason for this is that equity valuations, despite recent corrections, remain healthy. Management teams and boards are under pressure to demonstrate growth and justify their valuations. If companies fail to deliver earnings momentum, investors can punish their stocks. As a result, boards are increasingly looking at acquisitions when organic opportunities are insufficient. Companies are also able to use their stock as acquisition currency because valuations remain reasonable.

There is considerable liquidity in the global financial system. However, the US is attracting a disproportionate share of that capital, resulting in liquidity being drawn away from other markets. This is something to bear in mind. But the money is available, and deals are financeable.

The other point I would make on M&A is that private equity (PE) firms have around $2.5 trillion waiting to be deployed. Their activity has been relatively muted over the past two years, partly because funds that invested during the 2021 vintage have not been able to exit all their investments at valuations they find attractive. However, PE firms are ultimately required to deploy capital, and they are likely to become more active.

That said, activity may not be as prolific as before because the cost of capital has risen. With borrowing costs at around 5 per cent or higher across major currencies, the best-quality companies will continue to access financing, while marginal businesses may find it more difficult.

[TEXT]
Siemens Energy AG rose as much as 10 per cent after the company said profit margins will swell. Photograph: Frank Rumpenhorst/dpa via AP

European stocks set a fresh record on Wednesday, buoyed by rebounding energy and tech shares.

The performance came following upbeat forecasts from Siemens Energy, while investor confidence in artificial intelligence showed signs of returning after positive results at US-based AI cloud computing firm CoreWeave.

The neocloud company saw its shares rally by more than 25 per cent in early trading, which left it on track for its best day of trading since June.

Dublin

The Iseq All-Share Index rose marginally by 0.6 per cent to close at 15,035.08 on Wednesday.

Kingspan, which forecast a spike in 2026 trading profit earlier this week, saw its share price rise to €99 on Tuesday before it fell back to a low of around €92 per share. On Wednesday, it was one of the biggest gainers of the day, as its stock rose to €98.70, up 1.44 per cent since open.

Greencoat Renewables, which last week reported a €11.9 million profit for the first half of the year, was the biggest riser of the day as it added 2.1 per cent to close at €0.82. Kerry Group also registered a gain of near 2 per cent, as its price rose to €99.25.

Iseq-heavyweight Ryanair traded down nearly 2.5 per cent to €26 per share. Last week, the firm reported higher revenues, but profits were lower during the first half of its financial year to the end of September.

Insurance company FBD Holdings fell 2 per cent, while home builder Cairn Homes’ share price was down 3 per cent since open to €2.75.

London

The London stock market closed higher, with the FTSE 100 Index up almost 0.8 per cent at 10,835. The performance on Wednesday means the index is rising towards its record high.

Computer services company Computacenter, up by close to 3.9 per cent, was one of the top risers of the day, followed by investment group Polar Capital, which gained 4.7 per cent.

Rising oil and metals prices also contributed to the gains of the day in London, with BP’s share price up 2.7 per cent, while Shell rose 1.3 per cent.

Mining firms were also among the strong performers, as Antofagasta and Glencore were each up by more than 2 per cent.

British media company ITV slipped 3.6 per cent after warning that fourth-quarter advertising revenue could decline by up to 10 per cent.

Europe

New York
