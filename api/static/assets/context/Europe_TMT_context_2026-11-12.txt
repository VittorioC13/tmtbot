[TITLE]ComReg’s move to allow private 5G networks will transform Ireland’s economy:
[TEXT]
The Port of Liverpool deployed private 5G to support its logistics, robotics and enhanced connectivity requirements, resulting in a tenfold improvement in network performance. Photograph: Anthony Devlin/Bloomberg

Ireland has established a strong international position in technology despite its small size. With the highest third-level-educated population in the European Union, our nation has a highly skilled workforce. Yet in comparison to our EU counterparts, we are trailing when it comes to the next technological evolution for Irish enterprise.

Recently, ComReg published decisions and draft regulations for a licensing framework that will support the next technological leap for Irish businesses.

The framework will enable organisations beyond traditional telecommunications providers (such as Vodafone, Eir or Three) to access the 5G spectrum for the purpose of building their own private 5G networks.

This development will open up a new enterprise connectivity market in Ireland, representing one of the most significant advances in digital infrastructure in recent years.

While the regulation changes still have to be finalised, the direction of travel is clear: an Irish business will be able to commission a connectivity network purpose-built for its own site and operations, rather than depending entirely on the coverage and priorities of a publicly accessible network.

Public networks are getting stronger, which is welcome, but businesses, especially those with complex operations, need uninterrupted coverage, lower latency and greater control, tailored to their own sites.

Access to private 5G spectrum, delivered by industry, is a transformational development for the Republic’s economy, establishing the State as a competitive player on the global stage.

This technology gives organisations complete control over their wireless infrastructure, delivering secure, low-latency connectivity across large operational sites where conventional wifi or public cellular networks may be unable to meet business-critical requirements.

Imagine a crane lifting a shipping container while its operator controls the machinery remotely. If the connection drops, the operator loses the live video and control feed. That interruption could create a serious safety risk and halt operations completely.

A private 5G network is designed for a particular place, such as a factory, port or university campus, giving the organisation complete control to specify where it needs coverage, which devices can connect and how traffic is managed, all the while keeping critical applications separate from general internet use.

This advanced technology supports a new generation of connected operations, including robotics, industrial AI, autonomous vehicles, Internet of Things (IoT) devices, edge computing and real-time operational analytics across sectors including manufacturing, pharmaceuticals, logistics, ports, utilities and higher education.

For Irish businesses, private 5G spectrum creates new opportunities to accelerate digital transformation, automation, artificial intelligence and industry 4.0 initiatives, while strengthening Ireland’s competitiveness as a destination for global investment. Furthermore, as Irish organisations increasingly invest in AI, automation and digital transformation, private 5G will become a foundational technology that supports the next generation of connected enterprise operations.

To date, Ireland has been behind the curve of its European counterparts. With this new licensing framework taking shape, Ireland has an opportunity to catch up, and we have ample opportunities to learn from our neighbours’ success – and also their failures.

One such example can be seen in the Port of Liverpool, which deployed private 5G across its 40-hectare (100-acre) site to support its logistics, robotics and enhanced connectivity requirements. The deployment created a secure, high-performance wireless infrastructure that delivered a tenfold improvement in network performance while enabling real-time operational data, automation and safer, more efficient port operations.

There is no reason why the Republic’s ports, or enterprise businesses in general, should not be using the same technology, with the UK offering local shared access to spectrum since 2019. Germany has also given industry a route to local networks. Now Ireland has an opportunity to give businesses access to technology that provides robust security, greater connectivity capabilities and full oversight of their own private network, designed to minimise interruptions.

The prospect of businesses here adopting private 5G matters beyond just any one Irish company. On the global stage, Ireland competes for investment in advanced manufacturing, pharmaceuticals and logistics. Potential global investors look at the skills available in a market, alongside a region’s ability to run complex facilities. Connectivity, and reliable connectivity, is an integral part of that picture.

Thanks to ComReg’s landmark regulatory change, Irish companies will have the ability to build the wireless infrastructure their operations require by leveraging private 5G networks. This makes Ireland a more attractive and practical location for FDI, when it comes to the next production line or research facility.

The task now is to turn this opportunity into adoption. ComReg must finalise a workable licensing route, with clear application requirements and timelines. Irish businesses should identify where existing networks constrain production and test private 5G against clear targets. Ireland has a chance to open a door to a different kind of technological infrastructure that can power the Irish business of the future.

ComReg must open that door, and businesses must then be ready to step over the threshold.

[TITLE]The unlicensed highway: 6 GHz power comes to Costa Rica:
[TEXT]
As Latin America (LATAM) embraces the age of AI, Wi-Fi and other wireless solutions that operate on unlicensed spectrum without requiring a government-issued license are critical to building the infrastructure these AI-driven applications demand. Cisco recently joined the Latin America Spectrum Management Conference (Figure 1) in San José, Costa Rica, to showcase how its 6 GHz Wi-Fi technology will improve connectivity in high-density events and outdoor venues. Our demonstration also showed that LATAM is quickly adopting the ecosystem to take advantage of 6 GHz connectivity, with up to 40% of end user devices connecting to the band during the event. They experienced high data transfer rates, low latency, and strong uplink and downlink performance throughout the venue.

Figure 1. The America Spectrum Management Conference in San José, Costa Rica

The foundation of Costa Rica’s future connectivity and the economic impact

The 6 GHz band represents the most significant expansion of wireless capacity for unlicensed spectrum in the last two decades, a vital resource for the current and upcoming generations of Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7. By allocating the full 1200 MHz of spectrum for unlicensed use, Costa Rica has positioned itself alongside global leaders using Wi-Fi 6E and Wi-Fi 7 efficiently. This move is more than a technical update. It’s a foundational policy decision that ensures the country can support the growing density of connected devices in homes, offices, and industrial locations, as well as increasing connectivity needs related to AI and broadband. Recent rulings by Superintendencia de Telecomunicaciones (SUTEL), Costa Rica’s telecommunications regulatory authority, have finalized the framework for standard power (SP) devices and the accreditation of automated frequency coordination (AFC) systems. This has cleared the path for high-performance outdoor network standards that were previously focused only on indoor environments.

The deployment of the entire 6 GHz band is essential to avoid the congestion that occurs when too many devices compete for limited space in legacy frequency bands. By providing a massive, clean range of spectrum for indoor and outdoor unlicensed use, Costa Rica has ensured that its wireless infrastructure will remain resilient and capable of handling future data demands. This approach provides the necessary certainty to support long-term technology investment and signals that the nation is ready to support digital services like advanced automation and remote high-definition collaboration.

Technical excellence: IW9167E point-to-point innovation

The event was hosted by SUTEL with the support of the Inter-American Telecommunication Commission (CITEL) and was held alongside the Comisión Técnica Regional de Telecomunicaciones (COMTELCA) Radiocommunications Committee. More than 15 countries from the LATAM region were represented, including local regulators and ministries as well as industries and other interested parties.

A technical demonstration provided by Cisco involved high-performance standalone SP 6 GHz Cisco Catalyst IW9167E Heavy Duty Access Points, designed specifically for mission-critical environments, complex outdoor settings, and heavy industries like mining, ports, transportation, energy, and manufacturing. The demo focused on a point-to-point use case to demonstrate SP capabilities in the 6 GHz band, showcasing the equipment’s readiness for high-speed outdoor networks.

The hardware setup consisted of two IW9167E units paired with IW-ANT-PNL515-NS antennas, which has proven highly effective for the 6 GHz band (Figure 2). The demo was configured with a massive 320 MHz channel in the 6 GHz band; the units were placed outdoors, 20 meters between each other, with a line of sight and an antenna height of 2.5 meters. Even in a non-ideal environment characterized by nearby walls, metal structures, and close floor levels, the system maintained a consistent TCP upstream and downstream throughput of approximately 2 Gbps. This was achieved using a simple local configuration with a small server on one end and a laptop client on the other, both limited by a 2.5 Gbps port, proving the robustness of Cisco Ultra-Reliable Wireless Backhaul (URWB) technology.

Figure 2. Setup of the point-to-point Cisco Catalyst IW9167E Heavy Duty Access Point with IW-ANT-PNL515-NS antennas

The success of this backhaul demo has sparked significant interest among operators and government agencies in Costa Rica that are eager for high-capacity outdoor wireless solutions.

The Cisco Catalyst IW9167 Series provides reliable wireless connectivity for mission-critical applications in a state-of-the-art platform (Figure 3). It can operate in Wi-Fi 6, Workgroup Bridge (WGB), and URWB mode.

Figure 3. Point-to-point Cisco Catalyst IW9167E Heavy Duty Access Point TCP upstream and downstream performance

Transforming the user experience with Wi-Fi 7

In addition to the specialized point-to-point backhaul demonstration, Cisco provided Wi-Fi 7 service that powered the two-day conference room for over 400 connected devices. With a group of Cisco Wireless 9172I and Cisco Wireless 9176I Access Points we established a robust tri-band wireless low-power indoor Wi-Fi 7 environment.

The Wi-Fi service was configured with two distinct networks: one with the legacy bands 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz and a second dedicated exclusively to the full 6 GHz band. This dual-network approach provided a clear demonstration of the efficiency gains provided by the new 6 GHz spectrum, highlighting the capabilities and characteristics of this new standard. Notably, the high data transfer rates and low-latency connection performance were achieved without the need to utilize Multi-Link Operation (MLO).

The results of this live deployment were highly significant, marking it as one of the first events in LATAM to successfully enable Wi-Fi 7 and achieve a remarkably high adoption of 6 GHz devices (Figure 4). During the event, 38% of connected devices utilized the 6 GHz band, reaching more than 100 simultaneous connections, a notable performance milestone for this region.

Speed tests throughout the conference area showed outstanding results for the 6 GHz band (Figure 5). On 160 MHz channels, throughput reached between 600 and 800 Mbps—even with the venue’s 750 Mbps gateway limit. Notably, these speeds held for both downlink and uplink, a key differentiator for AI-driven technologies.

Figure 5. Speed test samples using the 6 GHz band in two different devices and at different times

The Cisco Wireless 9172I and 9176I used at the event are enterprise-class, indoor Wi-Fi 7 (802.11be) access points engineered to deliver high-density connectivity and massive throughput for next-generation wireless networks. Built on tri-band radio architectures supporting 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz, and 6 GHz spectrum, both models provide advanced Wi-Fi 7 capabilities.

Showcasing Cisco wireless and regional collaboration

The full 6 GHz license-exempt spectrum will soon serve as a vital, expansive highway for connectivity in LATAM, enabling significantly faster and more reliable connections for critical communications. Implementing unlicensed operations across the full 6 GHz band provides a stable and reliable foundation for long-term technical assurance for networking infrastructure. By utilizing standardized high-power operational capabilities, countries in LATAM can deploy robust, far-reaching connectivity solutions that mirror the impressive performance results shown in this point-to-point demo, supporting both essential public services and businesses.

The smooth Wi-Fi 7 service and the impressive person-to-person demo results resonated deeply with the Latin American countries in attendance, including regulators and ministries. [TITLE]Lawmakers call for bipartisan approach to AI, quantum policy in next Congress:
[TEXT]
Rep. Pat Harrigan, R-N.C., and Sen. Ben Ray Luján, D-N.M., called for bipartisan cooperation to advance emerging technologies Tuesday, though they emphasized their differing priorities for maintaining U.S. leadership in artificial intelligence and quantum computing.

Speaking at the Special Competitive Studies Project’s AI+ Quantum Summit in Washington, D.C., the lawmakers both stressed the importance of getting tech policy right in the next Congress.

Harrigan advocated for a hands-off approach to emerging technologies, while acknowledging that political disagreements over the government's role could create significant challenges after elected officials are sworn in on Jan. 3.

“To be totally honest, I expect a lot of headwinds in the next Congress,” Harrigan said, adding that conservatives will likely be “playing defense next year.”

The Republican pointed to the election of moderate Democrats in both chambers as a potential opportunity for bipartisan cooperation, saying he hopes lawmakers can find common ground to keep government “hands off nascent and emerging technologies as much as possible.”

In a separate conversation at the event, Luján stressed that Congress must work together to advance quantum policy and ensure federal policies support scientific progress.

“Compromise is not a bad word,” he said. “We have to work together to find some commonality here as well.”

“Policy can get in the way,” Luján added. “Is policy going to support future scientific advancements and discoveries, or is policy going to get in the way?”

Luján’s remarks came as the Commission on U.S. Quantum Primacy, convened by the Special Competitive Studies Project, released a report Tuesday that identified sustained federal investment in foundational quantum research as a near-term priority. The commission is co-chaired by Luján and Sen. Todd Young, R-Ind.

Luján said upcoming federal funding decisions will help determine the country’s direction in quantum, noting that there will be another continuing resolution vote soon. The current continuing resolution funds the government through Dec. 11.

“Budgets are a reflection of our priorities, and in the United States, are we going to make substantial investments and lift up our national labs, the ecosystem around how we can strengthen and advance quantum in the United States, or not?” Luján said. “I really believe that that is going to determine what we will do here.”

Similarly, Harrigan said Washington needs to support the people and organizations building emerging technologies, including quantum and AI.

“Congress needs to do a better job of making sure our research investments translate into something useful, and that's going to take a commitment that lasts longer than anyone in Congress or this administration,” Harrigan said.

“We’ve got to give our national laboratories, our universities and private industry some consistency while holding ourselves accountable to the results,” he added. “I don’t think that this is something that ought to divide Republicans and Democrats.”
